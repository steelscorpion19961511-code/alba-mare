He left Old Bridge early in the morning, and snow still lay on the road, crunching underfoot and reflecting the light of the dawning day. In his new cloak, with the fur hood drawn down over his eyes, August walked toward the neighboring town where, by Heimrir's words, a great fair was to be held.

He felt different — light despite the warm, sturdy clothing, collected and ready for the long road. Around him the snow gradually gave way to fine icy rain, the wind grew sharper, and the sky lower, as if pressing itself to the earth, promising blizzards soon. But August walked and listened to the snow crunching beneath his feet — this sound had become almost familiar to him, like a rhythm that sets the path. The road was deserted; only now and then did carts overtake him, crawling slowly northward laden with supplies; the drivers nodded to him without stopping, and he answered in kind — in this silent exchange of nods there was something of the fellowship of those used to cold and wind.

Toward evening of the second day he came to a small town where the fair was already in full swing. The square was filled with people, tents, stalls and street musicians. The air was thick with the smell of roasted meat, burnt sugar and wet wool, mingled with the smoke of bonfires and the spicy scent of hot mead. Walking along the rows and studying the goods — coarse woolen blankets, forged buckles, clay cups with uneven patterns — he suddenly heard a familiar melody.

He had almost passed the town gates when a familiar sound reached him — a fiddle, a flute, voices. August stopped, listening. It was the very melody he had heard at the bonfire when he traveled with the troupe — simple, yet so dear that for a moment his heart clenched. He followed the sound, pushing through the crowd, feeling his heart begin to beat faster, as if hurrying him toward something important.

And he saw them: the fiddler Thomas, the flutist Lena, old Uncle — and, of course, Mira. She stood at the edge, smiling at passersby, and there was so much light in that smile that even the dusk around seemed to grow a little brighter.

'August!' she cried when he came closer. Alive!

Mira was the first to see him. She sprang up, stepped toward him and embraced him — tightly, like a sister, as if checking that he was real, that he had not vanished the way things vanish in dreams. Her hands squeezed his shoulders, her head pressed against his chest for a moment, and he felt her warmth penetrate through the cloak, felt the smell of her hair — honey and smoke — fill his breathing, carrying him back to those days when the road was not a trial but an adventure.

She drew back, looked at him with shining eyes — and suddenly leaned in and quickly touched his lips with hers. Briefly, almost in a friendly way, as if it were the most natural gesture in the world. A simple, warm, quick touch that for a moment took August's breath away. He froze, not expecting this, and in that moment time seemed to stop for him. And she smiled and stepped back, as if nothing had happened, as if that was how it should be.

'Alive,' she repeated, and there was so much warmth in her voice that he found no words to answer. He simply stood, feeling her touch upon his lips, and did not know what to say. Words seemed superfluous.

The troupe greeted him as if they had seen him only yesterday. Someone clapped him on the shoulder, someone handed him a mug of hot wine smelling of spices and smoke. Around them people laughed and talked, but August felt above all something warm, almost forgotten, spreading within him — the feeling of belonging to something real, to a circle of people for whom he was not a stranger.

'We are playing this evening,' said Mira. 'There will be a great bonfire, dancing and songs. Will you join us?'

August smiled, and in that smile there was not only consent but relief — as if he had finally returned to a place where he was welcome.

'Yes. I am with you.'

In the evening they stood on the main square, on a small platform, and their music flowed above the heads of the crowd, weaving with the hum of conversation, laughter and the crackle of the bonfire. They played together, easily and freely, as if they had always been a single whole. People danced, laughed; some sang along, some simply stood and listened, watching the fire. August stood on the platform, feeling his voice join the common current, his guitar answer the fiddle and the flute, and understood that music is not what one does alone.

It is what one does together with others, and in that unity something greater than mere sounds is born.

In that moment he understood that the power of music lies in how it joins people together, how it turns a crowd into a community, and loneliness into belonging. And each time he looked at Mira, she smiled at him, and he felt her smile remain in him like a warm glow upon the soul.

At the end, when silence fell and the music died away, leaving behind only an echo in the air and a light humming in the fingers, Uncle came over and embraced him — tightly, like a father, without needless words. Thomas watched him with a slight smile, as at a younger brother; in his gaze there was approval and a quiet joy. Mira simply stood nearby, watching the fire, and her smile was quiet and understanding, as if she knew that this evening would stay with him forever.

'We remember how you lay in fever,' she said. 'We were afraid of losing you. But you pulled through. You are strong, August.'

'Thank you,' he said. 'For not abandoning me.'

'There is no need to thank,' said Uncle, and in his voice was the simplicity that requires no confirmation. 'We are wanderers. We are everything to one another. You are ours. Always remember that.'

August nodded. He felt these words would stay with him, would become a support in those days when the road would seem endless and the cold insurmountable.

He looked at them, at the crowd, at the bonfire dying down — and understood: this moment would remain with him forever, like an anchor that does not let the wind carry you away. Whether he would see them again, he did not know. But he knew they would always be with him — in the sound of a fiddle, in the rhythm of a drum, in the warmth of a stranger's shoulder.

He raised the guitar and played one last, quiet song — for this evening, for these faces, for this fire that had warmed him not only without but within. The song was simple, without intricate chords, but it held everything he wanted to say: gratitude, farewell, and the promise to remember.

The next morning he left the town alone. Snow covered his footprints, as if the town wished to keep secret his coming and his going. When he looked back, the troupe was still on the square — gathering their things, laughing, talking. He raised his hand, not shouting, not drawing attention, simply letting them know he remembered them. Mira saw the gesture, smiled and nodded — quietly, almost imperceptibly. He smiled in return, turned, and walked north.

The world was growing colder, the wind sharper, the road no longer what it had been. But now in his rucksack there lay not only warm things and Elsa's herbs. In it were these moments as well: Mira's embrace, Uncle's quiet voice, the laughter of the troupe, music over the square, and the feeling that he was part of something greater. And that was enough to go on.
