At morning Holmstad woke into a silence made of white. The snow had been falling for hours, and overnight it had buried every track, every road, every landmark — as if the world had been wiped down to a clean page, leaving only this even, endless white cover. The world had become flat, white and soundless, and even the wind, which had howled over the roofs only yesterday, now seemed to be lying in wait, dissolved in that thick whiteness.

August stood at the window and looked out at the street. The snow fell so thickly that the neighbouring houses could not be seen — only a white wall in which the outlines of walls, roofs and fences dissolved. It seemed that if you stepped over the threshold you would simply vanish, merge with this snow, become part of it. He stood and looked, feeling a strange calm spreading inside him — not emptiness, but a quiet in which he could hear his own thoughts.

Ulrik came over to him with a mug of hot tea. Steam rose over the rim, light and half-transparent, and for a moment it seemed to tie this frozen world to something alive.

“You aren't going anywhere today,” Ulrik said evenly, without extra words, the way one speaks of things every northerner already understands. “A blizzard. For a day, for two — no one knows. In conditions like these even northerners don't risk going out. Even those who've known these paths since childhood.”

August took the mug, feeling the warmth pass through his fingers, warming not only the skin but something deeper, as if returning to him the sense of himself. He nodded.

“I am not in a hurry.”

He spent the whole day by the stove. He helped Ulrik sort the hides: shook them out, beat off the packed snow and ice, laid them out by grade — soft ones for linings, coarse ones for cloaks, the densest for mittens. The hides smelled of frost, smoke and forest, and the smell was not foreign to him but familiar, almost native. He listened to the wind beginning to howl again beyond the walls — now softer, now louder, as if trying its voice, checking that everything was still in place. And he thought about how much his life had changed. He did not miss the south the way one misses a lost home, but sometimes he caught himself recalling the smell of the azure sea and the sunlight — not as something longed for, but as memories from another life he would not return to. Here the sun was different — cold, bright, almost cruel; it did not warm,

it blinded, reflecting off the snow so that you had to squint to see the road.

“Do you miss the south?” Ulrik asked, watching the fire. The flames threw ruddy reflections onto his face, and in those shadows he seemed older than he was, as if every winter had added to him not years but experience.

“Sometimes,” August said. “But what I was looking for is no longer there. Only shadows remain of what once seemed important.”

Ulrik nodded, asking no new questions. He understood that some things require no words, that sometimes it is enough simply to sit beside the fire, listening to the log crackle, and to know that no one is hurrying you.

Toward evening, when the wind died down and the house was filled with that special stillness that comes only after a long storm, August took up the guitar and began to play. He played slowly, almost weightlessly, as if afraid to frighten away that fragile quiet. But the music did not destroy it — it wove into it, became part of it. Every note sounded distinct, separate, and together they formed a melody that did not tell a story but conveyed a feeling — cold, expectation, hope.

Ulrik sat in the corner, stretching leather over a hoop — for a drum, perhaps, or for a shield; August did not ask. He listened in silence. He said not a word, did not nod, did not smile — simply went on with his work, and in that calm presence there was more warmth than in any words. His movements were unhurried, confident, as if he knew: there was nowhere to hurry, the winter was long, and everything that had to be done would be done.

“Where did you learn this?” he asked quietly, not looking up from the leather and the bone needle.

“I don't know,” August answered, not raising his eyes from the guitar. “It came on its own. As if it had lived somewhere inside and was waiting for things to grow quiet enough that it could be heard.”

Ulrik gave a short laugh — not mockingly, but as if he had heard something he had always known and long since stopped hearing said aloud. He did not interrupt, did not explain, did not teach. He simply went on pulling the leather, and that quiet, familiar sound — the creak of the needle, the tension of the fibres — became part of the music.

And August kept playing until snow began to fall again beyond the windows — not as thickly as by day but lazily, unhurriedly, as though the blizzard had exhausted itself and was now merely sifting down the last flakes. It seemed to him that he was living inside this moment — in this room, in this cold, in this light that knew no summer but knew how to keep warmth. And he wished this evening could last forever: that there would be no need to go anywhere, to prove anything — only to sit by the fire, to play, and to know there was a person beside him who expected no words from him.

By morning the blizzard had died down, but the road was impassable: the drifts lay in dense, heavy waves, and even the most experienced pathfinders would not have looked for a trail in them. August stayed in Holmstad a few more days, growing used to the rhythm of a winter day: to how early it grew dark and how long the evening stretched, to how the silence stopped pressing and became an ally. He sat by the window, watching the white world beyond the glass, and felt the north cease to be foreign. He did not know whether he would stay here forever, but he knew that this house, this fire and this snow had become part of him. And perhaps that was precisely where the truth he had been seeking lay: not in distant lands and not in great deeds, but in the simple warmth that can be found if you are not afraid to stop.
