August did not remember reaching the shore. At first he swam — or did not swim, but simply kept himself on the water, moving his arms and legs in a rhythm the body had worked out on its own, without consulting the mind. Then he clung to pieces of driftwood — to that small, unreliable raft of two planks that held him at the surface. Then a current took hold: he felt the water change direction, felt it carry him without asking, and he stopped resisting. He had no strength left to resist. He had strength only to hold on.

When he came to, he was lying on sand. The sun burned his skin, and the heat was the first thing he felt: not pain, not weariness, but heat — dense, pressing, as if someone had laid a red-hot stone on his back. His throat was so dry that swallowing hurt. His whole body ached — not in one place but everywhere: muscles, joints, skin scorched by the sun and crusted with salt. His eyes would not open: sand on the lashes, salt on the lids, light striking them like a hammer.

He lay without moving, and listened. The sound of waves — near, even, running up and falling back. The cry of a bird — unfamiliar, harsh, like neither gull nor raven. The rustle of something — perhaps wind, perhaps leaves. A silence — not the North's, muffled and pressing, but another kind: alive, full, warm.

He moved his fingers. His arms — intact. His legs — too. He moved his head — it swam, but tolerably. He raised a hand and touched his belt. The sword — there. Bjorn's knife — also. All that remained of his former life: his father's sword and Bjorn's knife. Two pieces of steel, two pieces of memory. The rest — water, fire, the deep.

He opened his eyes. Light struck them, and he squeezed them shut, then opened them again, slowly, growing used to it. Sky — blue, bright, without a single cloud. Sand — white, fine, hot. And everywhere, to the very horizon — water. Blue-green, living, shimmering in the sun. He lay on a shore, and around him there was no one. Not a soul.

He got to his feet — not at once, but in three stages: first he sat up, then got to his knees, then straightened. His head swam, and he grabbed at emptiness but kept his balance.

His body hummed like a taut string, and every muscle made itself known: the arms that had rowed, the legs that had beaten the water, the back that had burned in the sun. He examined himself: clothes — wet, torn, clinging to his skin. No boots. They had gone to the bottom, perhaps still in the water, perhaps by that ship. Bare feet on hot sand. His clothes were drying on him, and the salt tightened his skin — unpleasant, but bearable. Alive. Whole. That was what mattered.

He looked around. An island.

Small — maybe fifteen hundred paces long and a thousand across, judging by eye. Thick with vegetation: palms, some low trees with broad leaves, bushes, grass growing in clumps from the sand. The shore was rocky on one side, sandy on the other. The rocks were low, of reddish stone eaten by wind and water, with hollows and crevices in them that could serve as shelter. Not a soul. Only sand, palms, rocks, and the endless ocean.

August stood on the shore and looked at the water. The place where the ship had burned was far away — perhaps beyond the horizon, perhaps nearer. He did not know how long he had swum, did not know where the current had carried him, did not know whether there were other islands or a mainland nearby. He knew only one thing: he was here, and he had to survive. In the North they taught this from childhood. Do not give in. Do not pity. Do.

He examined the shore — carefully, a yard at a time. No wreckage. No case. No guitar. No pack. Only sand, shells, seaweed thrown up by the surf, and fragments of coral, white as bone. He walked along the shore in both directions — slowly, peering — but found nothing. The sea had taken everything. The sea and the fire.

He sat on the sand and closed his eyes. Not from weariness — to feel what was within. There was emptiness there — not the kind that frightens, but the kind that frees. Everything he had owned was lost. The guitar he had carried through three winters, through deserts, through battle with hyenas and brigands. The herbs gathered in Holmstad when Ulrik taught him to tell plants apart by smell and shape. The coins, the ring with the red stone. All of it — on the bottom, or in the flames.

But the sword — here. And Bjorn's knife — also, because in battle he never takes it off his belt, and into the water he jumped as he was. Two objects, two men, two worlds.

Father and Bjorn. South and North. What was before the fire, and what was after. Everything else can be restored. These two things — never.

He stood. He walked inland.

Bare feet trod the damp ground — first on sand, then on grass, then on something firmer, like forest litter. His fingers closed on the hilt of the sword — not because he expected an enemy, but because the sword was the only thing linking him to the past, and holding it in his hand was like holding his father's hand. He walked, listening to the silence, to the noise of the wind, to the cries of unfamiliar birds. The birds were small and bright — red, yellow, green — and they cried out as if in wonder: who has come to our island? August watched them, and for the first time in a long while thought: the world is beautiful. Even now. Even here. Even after all that has been.

He looked for water. Without water — the end. In the North water was everywhere: snow, ice, streams, rivers. Here there was only the ocean, and ocean water will kill you faster than thirst. He needed a fresh source. The island was small, but the vegetation was thick, green, alive — which meant water existed. Plants do not grow without water. He had to find the place they drew it from.

In an hour he found a stream. Small — no wider than a palm, no deeper than a finger. Water ran down a rocky ledge overgrown with moss and small ferns and gathered in a little hollow cut into the stone. The hollow was the size of two palms, and the water in it was clean, transparent, with a faint taste of stone and earth. August dropped to his knees, scooped water with his palms and drank — long, greedily, without stopping. The water was warm, not icy as in the North, but living, and with every swallow his body returned: first the throat, then the stomach, then the head, then the arms. He drank until he felt his stomach would take no more; then he stopped and sat, breathing hard.

He washed his face. Washed the salt and sand from his face, his hands, his neck. Water ran down his skin, and with the salt something else went too: tension, pain, the memory of battle. Not all of it — part will remain forever, like a scar. But what can be washed away — he washed away.

He examined the stream. Walked along it — up the slope, toward its source. The source was not far — in a crevice in the rock, where water seeped from the stone, drop by drop. Not a rich source, but a constant one. While there is rock, there will be water. August marked the place. In the North, Bjorn taught: "First water. Then fire. Then shelter. Then food. In order. Do not mix them up." Water — found. Fire — needed. Shelter — needed. Food — needed. Everything in order.

He went on, examining the vegetation. He found several trees bearing fruit like wild figs — small, green, thin-skinned. He picked one and smelled it. The scent was slightly sour but not rotten. He bit — carefully, a small piece. The flesh was soft, with fine seeds, and the taste was sweet-sour, with an astringency that pricked the tongue. Not poison — August knew this: in the North the shaman taught him to tell poisonous plants apart. "If it burns — don't eat. If it's bitter but doesn't burn — you may, but little. If it's sour or sweet — eat." This fruit did not burn. He ate another. Then another. Hunger receded — not entirely, but enough to think.

He memorized what the trees looked like: low, with grey, cracked bark and broad leaves like palms. The fruit grew in clusters, right on the trunk, like figs. He noted the place. Then he found another tree — taller, with yellow fruits, oval, the size of a thumb. He tried one — sweet, almost cloying, with soft, mealy flesh. He ate two. He memorized that too.

Then he found crabs. They lived under the rocks by the shore, in pools left by the ebb tide. Small, their shells the size of a palm, they waved their claws and tried to hide when August overturned the stones. He caught one — it clattered its claws, and the sound was like knuckles rapping on wood, like that crab aboard the ship, the one Dariush had dropped into the barrel. Dariush. August closed his eyes for a moment. Dariush was dead. The captain was dead. The crew was dead. Hasan — perhaps dead. Or perhaps not. August did not know and could not know. He opened his eyes. The crab was clattering in his hand. He tucked it into his belt and walked on.

Without fire the crab could not be cooked. Raw would do — in the North they ate raw fish when there was no time for a fire. But better with fire. Fire is warmth, light, safety, the ability to dry clothes and cook food. He needed fire.

There was no flint. It had remained on the ship, in the pack that burned or sank. But without flint, fire can be made another way. In the North the shaman had shown how: by friction. Dry wood, a dry stick, dry grass. And patience. A great deal of patience.

August spent the rest of the day preparing everything for fire. He found a dry branch — hard, light-coloured, untouched by rot. He found a softer stick — for the bow. He made a bow: bent the branch, strung it with a piece of rope found on the shore — perhaps from that same ship, perhaps from another. The piece was short, but it sufficed. The result was a primitive bow-drill: a stick wound with a cord that, moved back and forth, spun the spindle. In the North they made such things from roots and sinew. Here — from a branch and a piece of rope. Different materials, the same principle.

Dry tinder — fine grass, dry leaves, shredded bark — lay in a ready heap on a flat stone. August knelt, set the spindle in the hollow he had cut with Bjorn's knife into the soft stick, and began. The motion of the bow — forward, back, forward, back. The spindle spun, and fine shavings appeared in the hollow, dark, smelling of smoke. But there was no smoke. August quickened. His arms ached, but he did not stop. In the North the shaman said: "Fire does not like impatience. If you are angry, it will not come." August was not angry. He simply worked.

After half an hour — perhaps more — he saw smoke. A thin, barely visible thread rising from the hollow and dissolving into the air at once. His heart beat faster, but he did not stop: he kept spinning, breathing evenly, keeping the rhythm. The smoke grew thicker. Then — a tiny ember, an orange point, smouldering in the tinder like the last star before dawn. Carefully, holding his breath, he lifted the tinder and brought dry grass to it. The ember swelled, licked the grass, and suddenly — it flared. A small, trembling flame, faint as a newborn. August cupped his hands around it, shielding it from the wind, and blew gently.

The fire came alive, caught, and within a minute dry branches were crackling in the little fire, and the smoke rose in an even, calm thread.

He sat by the fire and looked at it. It was the first thing he had made on this island. The first thing he had made himself, with his own hands, from what he had found. In the North, fire is life. Here — also. August felt the warmth on his face, and in that warmth was something the sun did not have: its own warmth, its own life, its own light. This fire was his. Small, fragile — but his.

He roasted the crab on a flat stone set in the coals. The meat was white, firm, faintly sweet. He ate slowly, unhurried. The taste was simple but good: food earned by your own hands is always better. In the North, Bjorn said: "Hunger is the best cook. And honesty is the best seasoning." August was not hungry — he had fruit — but the crab was honest: caught by himself, cooked by himself.

By sunset he had built a shelter. From branches and palm leaves. Low, cramped, but protecting from sun and wind. He chose a place between two rocks, in a natural hollow that gave added protection. He drove branches into the ground at an angle, laid leaves on top in layers, like shingles — each over the one below, so that water would run down and not inward. In the North they built snow shelters this way: branches for the frame, moss for insulation, snow for the roof. Here, instead of moss — dry grass; instead of snow — leaves. Different materials, the same principle.

The shelter turned out cramped: one could lie in it but not stand. But August did not need to stand. He needed to sleep. And to sleep safely. He laid a layer of dry grass inside — bedding that would hold the warmth of the body. Nearby — the sword, the knife, a small heap of dry grass for the morning fire. The fire — at the entrance, small, economical: dry branches burn long and hot, and the island had plenty of them.

He sat in his shelter, watching the water, the waves, the fire. Rare bird cries reached him, a light wind, the unhurried sound of the waves. The sky darkened, and the stars appeared one after another — the same ones as everywhere, the same ones as in the North, and on the ship, and in the water as he swam. August watched them and thought.

His thoughts were simple. He had lost everything. Flint, kettle — nothing remained. The guitar stayed on the ship, and at the thought something ached inside, like an unhealed wound. The guitar had not been merely an instrument. It had been part of him, like his voice, like his memory, like his hands. On it he had played in Teir's village, and the old man had called him "the one they listen to." On it he had played in Torn's tavern, and people had fallen silent. On it he had played on deck, and the sailors had listened, and Hasan had learned to draw clean notes. The guitar had gone with him through forests, deserts, mountains, the sea. And now — on the bottom, or in the ash.

Even the boots in which he had walked the whole North — gone to the bottom. Bare feet on warm ground — not familiar, not comfortable, but bearable. In the North, without footwear — death. Here — an inconvenience. A different world, different rules. One must learn the new ones.

Alone. On an uninhabited island. Without food — no, he had found food. Without fire — no, he had made fire. Without hope of rescue — perhaps. August did not know whether there was hope. He did not know whether anyone would find him, whether a ship would pass by, whether anyone would see the smoke of his fire. He did not know and could not know. But he knew something else: hope is not needed to survive. Will is needed. In the North he had learned: hope is a luxury you can afford when you have a roof and food. When there is nothing — only will. The will to move, to breathe, to seek, to build, not to give in.

But he had not given in. The North had taught: even in the most hopeless situation there is a way out. Perhaps the way out is not to be saved but to live one more day. Then one more. Then one more. Days add up to weeks, weeks to months, and one day the world changes — because you did not stop.

Now every day began the same. He woke with the dawn — his body woke him by itself, without need, without prodding. He washed at the stream, drank, checked the fire. If the coals still smouldered — he blew on them, added dry grass and twigs. If they had died — he lit the fire anew. Patience. In the North they guarded fire like gold: they slept beside it so it would not go out. Here the coals did not die so fast — the humidity was lower, the wind gentler. But he checked all the same. Habit.

Then he went to the shore. He wandered the sand, looking under stones, searching the pools left by the ebb. He gathered crabs — small, quick ones that hid under rocks and in crevices. Sometimes he found seaweed — dark, slippery, with a sharp smell of the sea. Not all of it was edible: some was bitter, and August did not take it. Other kinds were fine, with a light salty taste, and he ate them raw or dried them on the coals. He memorized which rocks yielded the most crabs, at what hour of the day the tide left the largest pools, where the seaweed lay thickest. The island was small, but it lived its own life, and August was learning to read it as he had read the snow in the North.

There came to be more crabs. He found a spot — by the southern rock, where the tide left a deep pool, and the crabs gathered in it by the dozen. Each day he went there, overturned the stones, grabbed the slower ones. He made a trap: from branches and thin vines found in the thickets. He drove the branches into the sand, leaving a gap, and placed a piece of seaweed inside — bait. The crabs crawled in and could not get out. Simple. Effective. In the North they caught fish this way: woven traps of roots, with worm for bait. Different materials, the same principle.

Once he found a fish. A large, silvery one, thrown up on the shore by a wave. Perhaps it was tired, perhaps sick, perhaps simply unlucky. August picked it up and carried it to the fire. He cleaned it with Bjorn's knife — the only knife he had left, and for that reason every time he took it in his hands he thought of Bjorn. The knife was sharp, short, with a handle of deer antler darkened by time and sweat. Bjorn had given it on the last evening, when the snow fell on their shoulders and words were unnecessary. "Take it," he said. "A knife is not a weapon. A knife is life." August held it in his hand and felt: Bjorn was near. Perhaps far away. Perhaps already gone. But near.

The fish was good. He roasted it on the stone, ate slowly, and the taste was such that for a moment he forgot everything. The meat was soft, rich, with a light sweetish aftertaste that lingered on the tongue even after the last bite. He remembered: fish are sometimes thrown up on the shore. One must check every day.

He walked inland as well. Not every day — once every two or three days, so as not to trample the vegetation or frighten the birds. The birds were not shy — they let him come close, and August thought: perhaps they have never seen a man. The island was small and remote, and people, if any had ever been here, had long since gone. The birds lived their own lives: cried, flew, built nests in the branches, and August tried not to disturb them. In the North they taught: do not touch what you do not need. Every creature has a right to its life.

He found another tree — with large green fruit, hard as stone. He cut one open with his sword — inside was white flesh, dense, smelling of milk. He tasted it — good. Sweetish, nourishing, with a faint nutty flavour. He ate one fruit a day — no more, because he did not know how his body would react to excess. In the North the shaman said: "Eat little of a new food. If the body accepts it — eat more. If it rejects it — do not eat it at all." His body accepted it.

He found edible roots — long, thin, with brown skin. He dug them out with the knife, washed them in the stream, roasted them on the coals. The taste was starchy, bland, but filling — like the potatoes he had once eaten in Alba-Mare. His mother had boiled them with herbs and butter. August ate his roasted, without butter or herbs, and the taste was different — but hunger is not particular.

He learned to tell poisonous fruit by colour, and safe ones by smell and texture. One fruit — bright red, smooth, beautiful — he smelled and set aside: the scent was sharp, acrid, like rotting meat. In the North the shaman said: "Nature warns you. Bright colour — danger. Sharp smell — poison. Bitter taste — do not eat." August did not eat. A day later he saw that the fruit had fallen to the ground, and around it there were neither ants nor flies. Confirmation: poisonous. He remembered.

Another fruit — unremarkable, grey-green, with bumpy skin — he smelled: the scent was neutral, faintly sweet. He bit — sour, but not burning. He ate half, and waited. His body did not reject it. He ate the rest. He remembered: unremarkable — safe, bright — dangerous. As in the North: bright mushrooms are poisonous, dull ones are edible. Different worlds, the same rules.

Every night he lay under the shelter, looking at the stars through the leaves. The fire smouldered at the entrance, and its light came inside, drawing on the walls of the shelter shadows that moved, breathed, lived. August lay and listened: the sound of the waves, the cries of night birds, the crackle of coals, the rustle of leaves. All of it was music — not the kind he played on the guitar, but another: living, not made by human hands, endless. He listened and thought: perhaps no guitar is needed to hear music. Perhaps music is not the strings but what lies between them: silence, air, breath. Perhaps it was always within, and the guitar merely helped to let it out. And now there is no guitar, and the music is here. In the sound of the waves. In the cry of birds. In the crackle of the fire.

He did not complain. He did not lose heart. He simply survived. He did what he must: sought food and water, kept the fire alive, mended the shelter when the wind tore the leaves. His clothes dried, and he took them off and hung them on a branch — let them air. He went about in torn trousers, without a shirt, and his tan grew darker still, and the tattoos on his arms seemed brighter, as if the sun had drawn the colour out of them. Every day he checked the sword and the knife: cleaned them with sand, rubbed them with grass, made sure they did not rust. Two objects — all that remained — and they must remain whole.

One evening, as he sat by the fire watching the sunset — red, enormous, like blood on the water — he thought of his mother. Not the one who bore him, but the one who had found him in the south, sheltered him, nursed him. He had not thought of her in a long time. The thoughts came not as longing but as warmth: she was alive, somewhere beyond the sea, and perhaps thought of him. August could not write to her, could not send word. He could only think — and he thought. Not like a prayer, but like a conversation: silent, long, without words. As with Bjorn, when they sat by the fire and said nothing, and that was enough.

He thought of his father too. Of the sword that lay beside him. His father had defended the gates of Alba-Mare to the last. He had not run. He had not surrendered. He had stood and held, as long as he could. August had not been able to protect the crew. He had been too late. But he could protect himself, his sword, his life. That was less than his father had done. But it was what he could do. And he did it.

The days fell into a rhythm. Morning — water, fire, food. Day — the shore, the traps, gathering fruit. Evening — the fire, supper, the stars. Night — sleep, dark, without dreams. Once every two or three days — a circuit of the island, checking everything, renewing supplies. Once a week — a great fire on the shore, so the smoke would be visible from afar. Perhaps someone would see. Perhaps not. But August did it.

A week passed. Then a second. Then a third. August stopped counting the days: not because he lost count, but because the count had no meaning. What mattered was something else: was he alive, was he well, was there water and food, did the fire still smoulder. Everything else could go to ruin. The world had narrowed to the island, and the island — to the stream, the fire, and the shelter. Simple.

Sometimes, on clear nights, he sat on the shore and played an imaginary guitar. Not a real one — there was no real one. He simply sat, closed his eyes, and ran his fingers through the air, and his fingers remembered: where to press, where to release, where to drag a nail. Melodies were born in his head, and he heard them — not with his ears, but with something deeper, within, where memory lives. He played of the North, of snow, of silence. He played of the sea, of the ship, of people who were no more. He played of Hasan, who laughed and learned to draw notes. He played of Dariush, who touched his amulet and spoke little and heavily. He played of the captain, who slept little and watched the stars.

The music sounded only in his head, but it was real. Real, because it was true. And truth is what remains when everything else disappears.

He did not know how long the exile would last. He did not know whether he would find his way back. He did not know whether there was a "back" — perhaps the world had changed so that there was no road back. But he knew: this was not yet the end. Bjorn said: "The end is when you lie down and do not rise. While you stand — it is the road." August stood. On a small island, in the middle of an endless ocean, alone, without a guitar, without possessions, without people. But he stood.

And that was the main thing.
