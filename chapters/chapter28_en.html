On the eve of his departure, August sat by the stove in Elsa's house. Darkness was already falling outside, and the room was flooded with warm orange light that lay on the walls in uneven patches, as if trying to hold on to the day a little longer. The healer sat across from him, sorting through dried herbs — her hands moved slowly, with the same unhurried assurance with which she had touched his forehead in the first days of his illness, checking the fever. In every movement there was a habit honed by years: her fingers found the needed stems unerringly, set aside what was unnecessary, gathered what was needed.

They were silent, and in this silence there was a stillness that did not need to be broken. It was the kind of silence in which words could only get in the way. Elsa sorted the herbs, setting some aside, putting others into a wooden bowl. There was calm in her movements now — she knew that he had survived, and this knowledge lived in her like a quiet, even flame.

'Tomorrow I leave,' said August, and his own voice sounded in that silence a little louder than he intended.

'I knew,' Elsa answered, without raising her eyes. 'You are one of those who do not stay. I saw it in you from the first day. There was always something of the wind in you — as if neither walls nor care could hold you.'

She paused, then set the herbs down and looked at him. In her gaze there was no reproach, only the calm clarity of a person long accustomed to partings.

'You are going north?'

'Yes,' August nodded. 'To the Land of the Iron Cliffs.'

'It is not merely cold,' she said, as if weighing every word. 'It is a land where even the stones remember the wind. And if you do not know how to listen — it will swallow you. But you do know how to listen. I have heard you play. Your guitar speaks a language not everyone understands. In it there is the road, and weariness, and hope.'

August slightly bowed his head, as if wanting to hold these words, to hide them somewhere inside, so that later, in a blizzard or on an empty shore, he might remember them and warm himself with them.

'You remind me of one person,' Elsa said, turning her gaze to the fire. The flame flickered, as if listening to her words. 'He also went north. Many years ago. He was like you — unable to stay in one place, even when they offered him a home and warmth. He had the hands of a master and the heart of a wanderer.'

'And what became of him?' August asked.

'I don't know,' she answered. 'But I know he was happy when he left. And that is the main thing. Sometimes happiness is not walls and not a roof, but the feeling that you are on your path.'

August found no words. He simply looked at her, feeling a warm gratitude rise in his chest, heavy and light at once.

'Take this,' she said, and held out to him a small pouch tied with a cord. The cloth was coarse but neatly sewn, and tiny embroidered marks darkened on the knot — a charm, perhaps, or simply a craftswoman's habit of leaving a trace in every thing. 'Herbs. Brew them when you feel yourself beginning to freeze. It warms from within. It is not magic, of course, but good help when the cold slips under the skin.'

He took the pouch, and for a moment she covered his fingers with her own — warm, dry, sure. She squeezed them briefly, then let go. Her fingers lingered on his palm, as if she wished to give him something more than herbs: a particle of her calm, the ability to see what matters, the strength not to fear the road. He squeezed them in return — a gratitude that could not be expressed in words, but could be shown with this one gesture.

August hid the pouch in his new rucksack, in the same place where the strings and the notebook of songs lay. Beside them it seemed small, almost unnoticeable, but therein lay its strength: not to cry out about itself, but to be quietly nearby when needed.

'I don't know how to thank you,' he said.

'You already have,' Elsa answered. 'You stayed alive. And you will go — and that will be the best thanks of all. It is enough for me to know that I helped you take one more step.'

She rose, straightened her kerchief, brushed down her sleeves out of habit, and moved toward the door.

'For breakfast I will make you warm soup. You will need strength when you pass the gates. Let it be the last piece of this house you carry with you.'

August was left alone. He sat by the stove, watching the fire, and felt calm spreading through him — not emptiness, but an even, assured stillness. For the first time in a long while he knew where he was going. And he was not afraid. The fear had not vanished entirely, but now it was not a master, only a traveling companion — one he could listen to, but not allow to decide for him.

The next morning he left the house when the sun was just beginning to rise above the hills, coloring the sky in pale pink and golden tones. The air was cold and clean, as if washed by the night wind. Snow lay in a thin layer on the roofs, on the paths, on the old bridge. Everything around was white and still, and the stillness seemed almost sacred — like a pause before a song.

He stood on the porch for a while, looking at his hands. His fingers were warm, motionless — they no longer trembled. He clenched them into a fist and unclenched them, and in this simple movement there was a certainty that had not been there a month before. He was ready.

He stood at the gate, adjusted the new rucksack on his shoulders, and looked back. The healer's house stood quiet and warm, with smoke rising above the chimney. Somewhere beyond its walls remained the days of his illness, the nights by the stove, and her calm voice saying to him: 'You will survive.' Those words lived in him now, had become part of his breathing.

He did not turn around. But he felt her gaze on his back, following him like a last warm ray of light. She stood at the gate until he disappeared around the bend, and only then returned to the house.

He did not know whether he would ever come back here. But he knew this place would remain with him — not as an address on a map, but as a quiet voice whispering: 'You can.'

He turned and stepped onto the road leading north. The snow crunched underfoot, and his breath turned to vapor, hanging for a moment in the frosty air and dissolving without a trace. In his new warm cloak with its fur hood, with the guitar on his back and his father's sword at his belt, he felt ready for what lay ahead. His journey went on, and now it held not only movement, but meaning.
