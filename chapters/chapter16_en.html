They entered the strait on the second day after Windholm.

The captain had warned that this place was dangerous — rocks on both sides, dark water, capricious wind. But it was the shortest way to the eastern shores, and he had decided to risk it. During the day all went smoothly. The ship moved evenly, the wind was fair, and even the sailors, usually grim, seemed relaxed. Someone softly whistled an old sea song, someone mended the rigging, glancing at the rocks as if to make sure they would not stir from their places.

August stood on the forecastle, watching the narrow passage between the cliffs. The water here was strange — dark, almost black, its surface smooth as oil. The feeling of stillness was unsettling, like the lull before a storm. He did not know what exactly had put him on his guard, but his fingers tightened on the guitar case of their own accord. The words of the old man of Windholm suddenly came back to him: the sea does not tolerate haste. And now this place seemed to prove him right: here haste could cost a life.

Toward evening the twilight thickened faster than usual. The sky turned grey, and the shadows of the rocks lay across the water, making the passage still narrower, as if the cliffs were slowly shifting to clamp the ship in a vise. The captain ordered the pace reduced. The sailors lit lamps, but their light seemed weak and helpless in this cramped space — it only picked out separate slabs of rock from the darkness, unable to light the whole strait.

August suddenly noticed one of the shadows detaching itself from the rock. It moved toward the ship — slowly, soundlessly. He froze, peering into the dark, and his heart missed a beat when he understood: it was not a shadow. It was a boat. Another. And another. There were three.

They came alongside almost unnoticed, dissolving into the shadows of the cliffs. Those in them did not shout, did not threaten — they simply climbed over the side in silence, like shadows that knew their trade. There were not many — six men, no more — and their movements had a practiced nimbleness, as if they had done this dozens of times.

The air smelled of iron and sweat — foreign, sharp, like the fear that rises from the chest when you understand there is no way back.

The sailors cried out. One fell, clutching his shoulder. Another grabbed an oar, but the raider was quicker. August gripped the hilt of the sword he had not once drawn on this voyage. His father's blade settled into his palm, and he felt the leather of his glove against the worn leather of the hilt. He saw one of the sailors fall, heard the ring of steel, heard the cries mingling with the splash of water. And he stepped forward, feeling neither fear nor doubt.

He did not think about what he was doing. His body remembered what years of service had taught it. His first stroke was a feint — he beat aside a blade aimed at the sailor standing next to him, and stepped back a pace, forcing his opponent to shift sideways. The raiders' movements were sharp and impulsive, but August knew: when several men attack you, the thing is not to let them surround you. He moved between them, making them lose their balance. Every step was precise and measured, like a note in a melody learned long ago.

One of the raiders — young, with wild eyes — threw himself at him with a cry, raising his knife. August stepped off the line of attack, and the man, off balance, swept past. August did not hesitate. He swung the sword into his opponent's open side — deep, with a crunch. The man cried out, clutched the wound, but August was already stepping forward, finishing with a second stroke — to the throat, short and clean. Blood gushed across the deck, hot and sticky, flooding the boards. The raider gave a rattle, fell to his knees and toppled sideways, clutching his throat, from which a dark stream pulsed. August did not look at him. He had already turned to the next.

The second attacker, seeing his companion fall, hesitated for an instant. That instant was enough. August stepped forward, and his sword entered the raider's throat with the same short, cold stroke. The man did not even have time to cry out — only a rattle, only warm blood spattering August's sleeve, the deck, the face of the sailor standing near. August stepped back, seized the body by the shoulder and shoved it with his foot — it tumbled over the side and dropped into the dark water with a muffled splash.

The two remaining raiders retreated. They understood that aboard this ship there was one who did more than defend — one who killed. One of them leapt back into his boat; the other followed. When the last of the attackers had vanished into the darkness, August stood by the side, breathing heavily, his father's blade in his hand, lowered toward the deck. Blood dripped from the edge — slowly, heavily, falling onto the boards, mingling with the pool already congealing at his feet.

He felt no pain, heard no cries — only the roar of blood in his ears. The weight in his hand was different from the weight of the guitar. It was dense, alive, and he knew this weight would stay with him a long time. On the deck lay a pool — dark, viscous, already beginning to stiffen in the cold air. Somewhere near, a sailor moaned, holding his shoulder; someone was silently cleaning the deck, trying not to look at the stains.

The sailors looked at him with respect. But there was a shadow in that respect too — the understanding that this quiet man with the guitar had just done what they would not have dared to do. The captain came up to him, looked at his face, on which another's blood had not yet dried, and said:

"You've earned your place on this ship."

August answered nothing. He slid the sword into its sheath and went to the forecastle, where his guitar lay. He sat, staring into the darkness, trying to make sense of what had happened. The sensation of the short fight still pulsed in his hands, and in his head rang an alien rhythm of blows and breaths. He reached out to the guitar, touched the strings — but his fingers would not obey. They still remembered the weight of the sword, and when he stroked the strings, the guitar did not sound. He drew his hands away and simply watched the water until what he could not name began to quiet in his chest.

No one came to him. But in the morning, on the forecastle, lay a piece of bread and a mug of water.

And then, when the sun rose higher and lit the deck, it became clear how much this night had cost.

The old sailor — the very one who had once shared his dried cod with August — came up and silently laid beside him a clean rag and a small vial of some dark liquid.

"Wipe the blade," he said quietly. "Steel doesn't like blood. It eats into the metal, as it eats into the soul."

August nodded, took the rag and the vial. It took him a moment to understand that those words were not only about the sword.

While he wiped the blade, the captain came to the side and looked long at the dark water of the strait. Then he turned to the crew and said loudly, for all to hear:

"We will not linger here. As soon as we've seen to the wounded and cleared the deck — we go on at once. This strait does not like to be kept waiting."

The sailors nodded in silence. One of them — a very young fellow who had kept to the side before — came up to August and asked quietly:

"Have you fought before?"

August hesitated a moment, then answered briefly:

"Yes. In the guard."

The fellow nodded, as if that explained everything.

"Thank you," he added, barely audibly. "If it weren't for you..."

August gave no answer. He only inclined his head slightly.

The wounded sailor was carried to the forepeak. His shoulder was gashed, but the bone, mercifully, was untouched. The ship's physician — a silent man with hard hands — washed the wound, bound it, and poured a bitter draught into the man, who drifted asleep at once.

When the ship moved on, the strait seemed to exhale: the wind softened a little, and the rocks seemed to step back, giving passage. August stayed on the forecastle. He took the guitar again, but did not play. Instead he simply held it on his knees, feeling the wood and strings slowly warm with his heat. It seemed to him that the guitar was an anchor, holding him back from becoming altogether what he had been in the fight: a man for whom steel matters more than words.

Toward noon the captain came up to him again. This time he said nothing of the battle. Instead he pointed to the horizon, where a thin band of fog hung over the water.

"Beyond that fog lies open sea," he said. "The wind is different there. There you can breathe easier."

August looked where the captain was pointing, and for the first time that day felt his chest grow a little lighter.

"I'm glad," he answered quietly.

The captain nodded and left him alone with his thoughts and his guitar.

The rest of the day dragged slowly, as if time too wanted to lick its wounds. The sailors worked in silence, without fuss. Now and then someone cast August a brief glance, but no one tried to speak. Everyone understood: he needed time.

In the evening, as the sun went down and stained the water crimson, August did play one short melody after all. It was neither merry nor sad. There were no words in it, no complex transitions. It was a simple, even melody, as if he were trying to win back the rhythm he had known before: the rhythm in which there is room for the sea, and the guitar, and the silence.

And when he finished, no one applauded, no one spoke. But the old sailor, passing by, gave him the faintest nod. And in that nod there was everything: recognition, respect, and a silent promise that August was now, beyond doubt, one of their own.

The ship went on, leaving behind the dark rocks and that night which had changed something in every one of them. Ahead lay the open sea. And, perhaps, ahead waited a new hope.
