Several weeks had passed since August began playing in the streets of Al-Sharan. He did not seek fame, did not strive for money. He simply played — in the hours free from work for Matteo, when his hands were tired from sacks and ropes and his soul called for a voice. He went to the market, to the park, to the square, sat in the shade of the old caravanserai and played. The city grew used to him: people nodded at meetings, sometimes left coins or a treat — a flatbread, a handful of dates, a mug of cold ayran. August did not count the earnings the main thing. The main thing was that he was heard, that in this many-voiced city someone for a moment stopped and listened to him alone.

On one of those days, when the sun was already leaning toward evening and long shadows lay on the pavement, a short man with dark, lively eyes came up to him. He was dressed simply but with dignity, as if he knew the worth of humility — and of himself. His voice was soft, with a slight rasp, like that of a man who had spoken much and listened much.

"You are a bard," he said — not asking, but as if confirming something he had long known. "I heard you in the park. Come this evening to the Nanna. It is not far from the market. In the evenings we have music."

The Nanna. A tavern known by day as a place where one could eat hot soup and drink tea to drive the dust from one's tongue, and by night as a place where music sounded and where silence was as important as sound. August nodded. There was no question in it — only an invitation, and he accepted it.

In the evening he came to a low building with a dark wooden door, darkened by time and the smoke of hearths. Above the entrance hung a copper lantern in which a fire already burned — not bright but warm and dim, as if it did not want to disturb those already inside. August pushed the door and stepped in.

Inside, oil lamps burned, throwing uneven, living shadows on the walls. It was half-dark and cozy, and this half-darkness did not hide people but, on the contrary, drew them closer to one another: voices sounded quieter, and words mattered more. People sat at the tables — speaking in low tones, drinking wine, waiting for music. Some laughed softly, as if afraid to startle the evening. Some looked into the flame of a lamp, and in their eyes small tongues of fire were reflected.

The master — the same man with the lively eyes — stood at the counter, but seeing August, he nodded and pointed to a small stage by the wall. The stage was low, almost unnoticeable, but it was exactly from there that sound could fly across the hall without breaking against beams and corners.

August sat down, laid the guitar across his knees, and began to play. Not loudly. Not to gather a crowd at the door and make passersby peer inside. For those already sitting in the hall, waiting for something that cannot be ordered but can be heard.

First he played melodies he had known long — those learned in another world, in another life, where snow lay on rooftops and the wind sang in the gaps between stones. Then he moved to one of those born here, in Al-Sharan — of the evening wind, of the smell of bread, of how easy it is to breathe when one need not hurry anywhere. In that melody were the noise of the market, and the quiet of the park, and the taste of pilaf, and the coolness of the fountain, and even the creak of a cart's wheels, which suddenly became part of the rhythm.

People listened. Some closed their eyes and seemed to go somewhere within themselves, to find there something forgotten. Some looked into the flame of an oil lamp, and it seemed he saw in it not merely fire but a whole story. The master stood at the counter and listened without taking his eyes away, and in that gaze there was no calculation — only respect for what was being born here and now.

When August finished, the tavern for a moment became so quiet that one could hear the wick crackling in the lamp and a child laughing somewhere beyond the wall. Then someone exhaled softly, someone nodded as if confirming something important, and the hall filled again with the murmur of voices — but now they sounded different, softer, calmer, as if the music had lifted from them part of the day's weight.

The master came up and sat on a low bench beside him, unhurried, in such a way as not to startle the silence that still hung in the air.

"You may come here whenever you wish," he said quietly, looking not at August but somewhere ahead, as if he saw not the walls of the tavern but the road along which they both were walking. "I cannot pay you what you are worth, but I can offer supper and a room for each evening. And if one day you wish to leave — you will leave without offense."

August looked at him. At a man who promised no gold but gave something no less valuable: a place where his music had worth — not in coins but in respect, in silence, in eyes that listened.

He smiled — simply, without superfluous words.

"That is fair," he said.

They said nothing more. Words were not needed. August stayed in the tavern until midnight, playing what was born of itself, what came from the fingers, from memory, from the air of this city. Sometimes he caught the master's gaze — calm, attentive, as if he were listening not only to the music but to what remained between the notes.

It was not earnings. But it was a place where his music was needed. Where it did not merely fill the silence but became part of it. Where every evening he could sit on the stage, lay the guitar across his knees, and know: he would be heard. And that was enough.
