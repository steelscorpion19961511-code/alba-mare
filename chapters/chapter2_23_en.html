He came out of the park feeling a lightness spread through his chest, as if together with the morning air he had breathed in part of that silence. Inside was a quiet joy, unloud but real, as if for the first time in a long while he had allowed himself simply to be. He walked the streets of Al-Sharan, and the city no longer seemed foreign, merely very large — and therefore all the more interesting. Now August noticed little things he had not seen before: how the shadow of a minaret lay on the pavement, how copper bowls gleamed on the counters, how children let water from a clay jug run down the gutter and laughed when it spilled into little streams.

He noticed he was hungry. A morning at the market, then the park — time had slipped by unnoticed, as if someone had hidden the clocks so that he would not hurry.

Soon he came out onto the main square — an enormous one, paved with light stone polished by the steps of thousands of people. At its center stood a fountain: water fell from a height and broke into thousands of droplets, and even in this already gathering day it carried coolness. People bustled around the fountain: traders praised their wares, travelers rested, sitting on the rim of the basin, locals talked, laughed, argued. Somewhere music played — simple, rhythmic, on a few strings and a drum; somewhere children laughed, chasing one another between the legs of adults. The air was saturated with the smells of spices, fried meat, fresh baking, and something elusive besides — the smell of the city itself, gathered out of hundreds of small stories.

August stopped for a moment, feeling his appetite wake with new force, as if the hunger were not only of the body but also a thirst to feel this day whole.

Not far off, at the edge of the square, he noticed a small establishment — not a tavern, not an eating-house, but something in between. An open terrace with wooden tables and benches, over which a cloth awning was stretched, like a ship's sail. It hung over the tables, making shade, and stirred in the wind as if it truly wanted to fly away but held on to its ropes. Under the awning people sat — eating, drinking, talking, sometimes falling silent to listen to music or a trader's cry, then returning to their conversations as to something important.

August came closer and, sitting down at a free table in the shade, noticed the master of the place — a spare, swarthy man with quick, attentive eyes — nod to him in greeting, as if he understood at once: this man was not in a hurry.

A few minutes later a hot flatbread appeared before him — round, porous, with ruddy edges that crackled if you broke them. It was so fresh that it steamed, and the steam carried the smell of warm dough, butter, and something spiced. Beside it they set a small bowl of thick sauce — oily, smelling of greens, with a faint bitterness. In the sauce floated finely chopped pieces of onion, garlic, and tomatoes, and on top was sprinkled a pinch of dried mint and a little hot pepper. Flatbread and sauce — simple things, but they looked as if they had been made for him alone, as if that was the chief secret: not to complicate, but to let each ingredient say its own word.

The master came up and, seeing August look at the unfamiliar food, smiled — and there was so much warmth in that smile that it was itself a kind of treat.

"Tear off a piece of the bread," he said in broken speech, but so that the meaning was clear without words. "Dip it in the sauce. Eat."

August followed the advice. The piece of flatbread, soft and hot, soaked in the oily sauce, and the flavor spread over his tongue — spicy, rich, with a light sharpness and freshness, as if the whole morning market had fitted into it. He took another bite and felt the food fill him with warmth, driving away the slight tiredness that had gathered over the morning.

The master brought another dish. It was pilaf. Golden rice mixed with pieces of carrot, onion, and spices, with large chunks of soft, juicy camel meat that melted in the mouth. August looked closely at the rice — he had never seen such grain before: it was slightly larger than usual, translucent, as though it had drunk in the color of the sun. He took a spoon and tried it. The rice was tender, with a soft texture, steeped in the aromas of spices — saffron, cumin, cardamom. He felt the food fill him with warmth, spread through his body, give back strength. The camel meat was soft, almost crumbling, with a light smoky taste, as if it had been cooked over coals and then long simmered under a lid.

"What is this?" asked August, pointing at the rice.

"Rice," answered the master, and there was pride in his voice, as if he spoke not simply of a product but of something dear. "It is brought from the east, from beyond the mountains. It does not grow here, but it is prized for its taste. We cook it with meat, vegetables, and spices. It is called pilaf."

August nodded and tried more. This time he did not hurry, trying to catch every shade: the sweetness of the carrot, the astringency of the onion, the warmth of the spices, the softness of the meat. He did not hurry, feeling that food was returning not only strength but some forgotten feeling of home — not a place but a state, when everything is in its place.

Nearby stood a mug of tea — dark, spiced, with a taste of clove and cinnamon and a light honey sweetness. August drank it slowly, gazing at the square, at the people, at the water in the fountain, at the clouds drifting above the awning, and at the awning itself, which went on stirring as though it wanted to tell some story. He felt that this day, full of sounds and smells, was becoming part of him, weaving itself into memory like a pattern in cloth.

When he finished, he paid. The master took the coins, nodded, and smiled the smile with which one greets not merely a customer but a person one wants to see again.

August came out from under the awning and was in the square again. People around him clamored, laughed, hurried, haggled, called to one another, and that noise no longer pressed but sounded like music — complex, many-voiced, but comprehensible. He felt calm. He was part of this city, and the city part of him. He knew that ahead lay another day, full of music and silence, meetings and partings, smells and tastes.

He took out the guitar and walked toward the fountain. The sun stood high, the water glittered, and in that light the honey wood of the guitar seemed even warmer. August sat on the rim of the fountain's basin, laid the instrument across his knees, and for a moment closed his eyes, listening to the city breathe. Then he touched the strings — quietly, testing how the sound would carry in that noise. And he understood: here too one could play. Not in order to drown the city, but to become its voice — one of many, but one's own.
