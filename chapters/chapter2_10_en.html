The road took exactly one day. By evening August felt the wind — humid, salty, alive, as if the very air here were different, denser and heavier than in the desert or the mountains. It smelled not of dust and resinous pine, but of salt, fish, tar, and something elusive that could be called the scent of distant lands. Gulls wheeled overhead, crying so loudly it seemed they wished to outshout the wind itself. August quickened his pace, and an hour later the port opened before him.

The city had no name — or he simply did not trouble to learn it. It was not a city in the usual sense, not walls and towers, but precisely a port: a cluster of piers, warehouses, sheds and houses pressed against one another, as if seeking support in that eternal wind. It was large, noisy, colorful and slightly wild, like a living creature that never falls still. It smelled of fish, tar, wet ropes, smoke, spices and salt — scents that wove into one dense, complex aroma that made the head swim and, at the same time, brought calm.

Ships stood along the piers — merchant, fishing, old and new, battered by storms and re-tarred, with faded sails and gleaming copper fittings. Some rocked on the swell as if impatiently shifting their weight from foot to foot; others stood motionless, settled under their cargo. People bustled everywhere: stevedores hauled crates, sailors checked the rigging, traders shouted prices, fishermen mended nets, boys ran between barrels and crates, shouting something and laughing. Everything moved, clamored, breathed, and in that movement there was a logic August did not yet understand but already felt.

He stopped at the edge of a pier and simply watched the water. It was not azure as at Alba-Mare, where the sea seemed a precious stone, nor dark and leaden as in the north, where its weight pressed upon the soul. Here the water was green-blue, alive, breathing: now it darkened, now flashed in the sun, now foamed against the rocks, as if it wished to say something. August watched it, and something warm spread within him — not memory, not longing, but recognition: he had finally returned to where the land ends and infinity begins.

He stood a long time, until the sun began to sink toward the horizon, gilding the water with gold and copper. Then he walked along the piers, studying the ships, listening to the conversations, trying to catch the rhythm of the place. Some were seeking stevedores, some sailors; some shouted the names of ports to which the vessels were bound. August did not hurry. He wanted to feel which ship would call to him, not simply take the first that came.

At some point he noticed a small coffee house right by the pier. It stood a little apart, almost pressed against a warehouse, and seemed an islet of quiet amid the port noise. Above the entrance hung a faded wooden board on which a name had once been carved but had since worn away. An old, stooped man in a grease-stained apron sat by the entrance on a low stool, smoking a long pipe. The smoke rose in a thin stream and was at once carried off by the wind. When August came closer, the old man raised his eyes, looked at him calmly, without curiosity and without hostility, and gestured for him to enter.

Inside it smelled of roasted beans, cinnamon, honey and something spiced that August could not name. It smelled of home, though this place was clearly home to no one. Along the wall stood wooden shelves with clay jars bearing no labels, but by their shape and color August guessed they held spices. In a corner a hearth crackled softly, and above it hung a copper coffeepot.

August sat down at a wooden table covered with scratches and the marks of cups, as if each had left its trace here. A minute later a small cup of dark, almost black drink was set before him. Beside it lay a dish with little sweets — honeyed, nutty, dusted with spices.

He raised the cup to his lips and drank. The drink was bitterish, bracing, with a light sourness that seemed to clear not only the palate but the thoughts as well. This taste was unfamiliar: he had never tasted anything like it. And in that newness there was something right: he was not trying to recall the past, but opening the present.

He bit into one of the sweets. The rich nutty taste mingled with honey and spices, and for a moment August found himself again in that day he remembered so often: the last quiet day before the fire, when his mother had baked a pie with figs and almonds. The same warm, dense taste, the same sweetness that was not cloying but seemed to gather within itself all the goodness of the house. And now that taste, that aroma mingled with the bitterness of the dark drink, and the weariness that had accumulated over weeks melted away, giving way to a light, almost forgotten joy.

He sat and drank in small sips, watching the sea, the rocking ships, the gulls wheeling and crying. He did not know how long he sat there. Time flowed differently here: it neither hurried nor dragged — it simply was. When he came out, the sun was already leaning toward sunset, tinting the sky in pink and violet tones and the water in gold. The shadows grew longer, the noise of the port subsided a little, as if the city were drawing a deep breath before night.

August again walked along the piers, studying the ships. Now he was not merely looking — he was searching. He wanted a vessel that looked neither too wealthy nor too desperate, but simply dependable. A ship whose people knew their trade and asked no needless questions.

At last he stopped by one of the vessels — old but sturdy, with high sides and worn sails that nevertheless had been neatly patched and tarred. On deck several sailors were checking the ropes; their movements were calm, confident, without fuss. The captain — a short man with a grey beard and attentive eyes — stood at the rail, saying something quietly to the boatswain.

August came closer and waited until the captain noticed him. The latter raised his gaze, looked attentively, as if reading not only the face but the story standing behind it.

“What do you want?” asked the captain without rudeness, but without warmth either.

“I'm looking for a place,” said August. “I can work. I can stand watch. I can haul rope.”

The captain narrowed his eyes slightly, as if appraising not the words but what stood behind them.

“Where are you from?”

“From the North,” August answered. “Then I was in the desert. Then in Shaan-Tar.”

The captain grunted, as if he had heard something familiar.

“We sail tomorrow morning,” he said. “You work — you get a place and food. If you fail — you go ashore. Agreed?”

August nodded.

“Agreed.”

The captain looked at him once more, as if to make certain he had not erred, then waved a hand to show the conversation was over and turned again to his affairs.

August stepped away, feeling anticipation awaken within him — not joyful, not anxious, but calm, as before a long march, when you know there is a road ahead and you are ready to walk it.

He found lodging by the pier — a small room above a shop, smelling of dried fish and wood, but clean and quiet. The owner, a silent man with rough hands, took payment and showed him a bed covered with a woolen blanket. August ate a simple supper — fish with bread and a mug of water — and lay down.

From outside came the sound of the surf, the cries of gulls, the distant voices of sailors, someone's songs to a guitar, laughter. The sounds wove into one long, living melody of the port, and August listened to it without trying to make out the words. He lay and felt the sea breathing nearby, calling to him, promising a road, wind and salt on the lips. Within him anticipation awoke, quiet and certain: tomorrow he would go out to sea, and a new chapter of his journey would begin.
