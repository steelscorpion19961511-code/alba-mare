He went into the mountains on the tenth day after the feast. He was called along by an old hunter named Bjorn — a broad-shouldered, silent man with a face scored by winds, as if every storm that had ever raged over these lands had left its mark there. He did not ask whether August could walk on snow, did not warn him of dangers. He simply said:

“We set out tomorrow. If you want to see this land — come with us.”

August did not hesitate. He packed his rucksack, checked his flint, took a supply of dried meat and hardtack. He left the guitar with Signe — in the mountains it would have been needless weight, and its sound would have been out of place here: the mountains demanded silence. He knew it would be hard, but he had not imagined how hard.

The journey began in darkness. Four men and August walked along a narrow path that wound between the rocks like a snake grown used to these stones. The snow was deep, and August felt his legs sinking almost to the knee. He tried to step in the tracks of those ahead, but kept going astray — his boots sank again and again into the loose mass, making him founder, lose his balance and claw his way back to the surface. He felt clumsy, almost ridiculous, but tried not to show it.

The bright sun, reflected from the white snow, struck his eyes with merciless light. August squinted, but still saw only blinding whiteness from which his eyes began to ache and water. He did not know that they needed covering until Bjorn silently held out to him a narrow strip of dark leather, showing him as they walked how to tie it around his head. August felt awkward: his breathing faltered, every step demanded effort, and now this bandage too made him look like someone foreign even to himself. But he tied it on, grateful for this simple, life-saving thing.

Bjorn walked ahead, occasionally stopping to point out tracks, a change in the wind, barely visible cracks in the ice. He spoke little, but every word was exact, like the blow of an axe on a log. August listened, memorized, tried not to fall behind. But after an hour his feet were already worn raw, and every step sent pain through his soles as if someone were driving small nails into them. He did not complain. He simply walked, teeth clenched, feeling the sweat freeze on his temples and the cold worm its way under his clothing, testing him for strength.

Toward evening they made camp in a small gorge sheltered from the wind, as if the earth itself had built walls here to shelter travellers. Bjorn lit a fire, and the men sat around it in silence, as if this circle of fire were the only thing holding them all together. August took off his boots and saw with surprise the blisters on his heels — they had already burst, leaving dark stains on the cloth. He bandaged them with a strip of cloth one of the hunters gave him, trying to do it with the same calm with which the others treated their small wounds and bruises. It hurt to look at the fire — even behind the bandage his eyes still watered from the daylight — but he sat, feeling the cold gradually release its hold and give way to an exhausted warmth.

That night he understood what it means to be part of this land. It did not give warmth freely, did not promise comfort and did not soften its blows. But it gave strength — the kind that is born not of joy but of persistence, when you stop waiting for it to change and accept it as it is.

For the next three days they walked along a ridge where snow gave way to ice and the path narrowed until one had to press against the rock, hugging the stone to keep from tumbling down. It grew ever harder to walk, and every new turn brought August new pain and new effort. He no longer stumbled so often; his body was beginning to grow used to this rhythm, to this endless motion forward. But his hands were still unsteady as he clambered over the rocks, and he felt the salt of dried sweat sting the skin of his face, mingling with the cold and the wind.

On the fourth day Bjorn stopped at the edge of a ledge and pointed to the valley below:

“The elder lives here. The one who knows more than we do.”

August looked down, where among the rocks several buildings could be seen, drifted over with snow. The place seemed almost impregnable — as if it had grown out of the very rock, become its continuation rather than an intrusion of man. The roofs were covered with a layer of snow that merged with the stones, making the houses nearly invisible. He understood that his path had brought him where he was meant to arrive — not by chance, but by that quiet logic he had begun to catch in these lands.

He did not know what awaited him down there. But his feet were rubbed raw, his eyes tired of the blinding light, and his body no longer asked for rest — it simply walked, because stopping was not allowed. He felt that his journey continued, and that he was ready to go on, even if ahead there was only cold, stone and silence.

As they began to descend, Bjorn for the first time in the whole journey looked at August directly, unhurried, as if he wanted to make out something in his face.

“The path here is treacherous,” he said quietly, so that only August could hear. “Hold on to the stone, not to the air. And don't look down until you must.”

August nodded, and in that nod there was not merely consent but the recognition that he was finally beginning to understand the language of these mountains — a language in which words were rare and actions spoke louder than any cry.
