He woke before dawn.

The sun had not yet risen above the rooftops, but the city was already breathing — deeply, noisily, impatiently. Somewhere roosters crowed, as if arguing with the first rays; chains clinked, holding cargo fast to carts; wheels creaked, heavy and stubborn as old sailors. He lay on his bed, staring at the ceiling, and listened to Al-Sharan waking. It was not a quiet, careful sound but a thick, many-layered hum — as though the city were pulling the day on like a garment, trying it, grumbling, and putting it on all the same.

Smells were already slipping through the gaps in the shutters — sharp, insistent, alive. It smelled of fried bread, of spices, of smoke from the night braziers that had soaked up the scent of cumin and dried pepper. And it smelled of the sea — salty, cool, as though the sea had come right to the threshold and left a drop of its moisture here. August sat up and stretched, feeling his body fill with something he had not felt for a long time — anticipation, a light tremor like the one that comes before the first chord.

He dressed, buttoned a light shirt, threw on his belt. He took the guitar — the new one, honey-colored, with the carved wave and wind on the soundboard — and left the room. Outside it was still cool, but the sun was already touching the tops of the minarets, drawing golden sparks out of the stone. The air was clear, as if washed by the night dew, and it still held a freshness that the heat of the day would soon chase away.

He walked through narrow lanes, feeling the city close in around him from all sides. Traders were setting out their goods: one laid out bundles of dried lemons, another hung up fabrics that rustled like the wings of birds. Porters carried sacks, heavy and dusty, and their steps rang hollowly off the walls. Children ran between the legs of passersby, laughed, called out something bright and forgot it at once, moving on to a new game. August watched them and sought neither a glance nor a place. He simply walked, letting the city touch him — with noise, with smell, with movement.

When he came out onto a small square by an old fountain, he stopped. The fountain was low, built of darkened stone covered with a thin patina of time. The water flowed down into the basin, filling it with a quiet, even murmur — not loud, not intrusive, but such that it was the heart of this place. Around it were a few stalls, still closed, and a couple of benches on which no one had yet sat.

The place was quiet but not empty: the silence here was alive, filled with expectation. The market clamored around the corner, and that noise was music one wanted to join.

August sat on the rim of the fountain and laid the guitar across his knees. He pulled a light neckerchief from his belt — the one he carried to shield his face from dust and sun — and spread it on the stone slabs in front of him. Yellow, with a faded pattern, as though it had drunk in the sun of hundreds of days. It was not a case. It was just cloth. But that was enough.

He closed his eyes for a moment. He drew a breath — and in that breath fit the whole city: salt, spices, dust, the breathing of people, the rustle of cloth, the far cry of a gull. And he touched the strings.

The sound was pure and deep — the same he had heard in the workshop when he first took the guitar into his hands. But now it sounded different: not in silence, but in dialogue with the city. He did not begin a song. He simply let his fingers go their own way, feeling how the wood answered each touch. The melody was born out of the morning's silence, out of the murmur of the fountain, out of the way the city woke, out of the cry of a trader calling to customers, out of the knock of a wooden spoon on a copper basin, out of a child's laughter, out of the splash of water, out of the far ringing of ship's rigging.

He did not notice when people began to stop. First one — a woman with a basket who slowed her step, listening, as if trying to catch the sound the way a hand catches a falling petal. Then a man with a donkey, who held the animal still so as not to make noise, and stood there, reins slack, while the donkey snorted softly, not understanding why everything had suddenly grown quieter. Then two children who came closer and sat down on the stone rim of the fountain, hugging their knees, and watched August's fingers as if trying to memorize every movement.

He played of the sea. Of the city that had burned. Of how hard it is to walk when you do not know where you will stop. Of how to find yourself when you lose everything. He did not play for coins. He played in order to remember. So that the sound would be a bridge between the man he had been and the man he was becoming.

The first coin fell on the cloth with a quiet chime — as if someone had carefully set a drop of gratitude on the kerchief. August did not look. He simply went on playing. Then a second, a third. Someone tossed a copper coin, heavy and warm; someone a silver one that lay on the cloth like a little moonlight.

A boy of about ten came up, laid down the smallest coin he could find in his pocket, and stepped back to listen, as if afraid that a step closer would break that fragile silence. August noticed, smiled with the corners of his mouth, and played a little louder, as if in thanks.

Slowly a small crowd gathered. There were traders who had left their stalls for a moment, porters, a woman selling roasted nuts, an old man on a low bench who nodded in time with the melody as if recognizing it from a distant past. No one shouted. No one clapped. But everyone listened, and in that attention there was something more than curiosity — it was recognition, quiet and deep, like the shadow of a minaret at noon.

When he finished, he raised his head. Several people stood before him. No one applauded. But no one left. They simply looked — and in their faces there was not curiosity but something else: recognition, as if in this melody they had heard something of their own, long forgotten.

He gathered the coins from the kerchief, tied the little knot, and tucked it into his belt. He rose, took the guitar, and looked at the sky. The sun had climbed high, and the day was beginning — bright, noisy, full of tasks and voices.

He smiled — and walked on. Toward the new music that awaited. Toward the city that went on breathing, crying, laughing, trading, living — and now, in that hum, his own note sounded too, quiet but true.
