He left the village early in the morning, before the sun had risen above the hills. The grass was wet with dew, and the air smelled of freshness, of the sea, and of morning quiet. His mother stood at the gate, but he did not turn around, though he felt her gaze on his back, following him like the last warm ray of light.

He walked until the village disappeared behind the hills. But at the crest he stopped and looked back. The houses were small, like toys, and the smoke above their roofs was dissolving into the morning sky. Somewhere there his mother remained. He stood a moment, feeling the wind touch his face, then turned and went on.

Beyond the hills a road began, leading south — to where, people said, there was a great city. August walked it, feeling his feet tread lightly on solid ground. With each day he felt lighter, calmer, freer. The road here was wide and well-traveled — traders, pilgrims, and wanderers went by it. Wheel ruts alternated with horseshoe prints, and August walked them as though reading lines of someone else's book, not yet read.

He thought about his mother's words. "Your life is not in this village." She had said it without bitterness, without reproach, and that made it sound not like permission to leave but like a blessing. She knew. She had always known he would not stay — and she had not tried to hold him. That was her strength: she let go of those she loved and made no drama of it.

Toward midday he heard wheels creaking behind him. He turned and saw a cart drawn by an old horse. On the driver's bench sat a stocky man with a red beard, dressed in a dusty jacket.

"Hey, traveler!" he shouted, reining in the horse. "Going far?"

August stopped.

"To the big city."

The man grinned, showing a gap-toothed smile:

"Well, I'm headed that way myself. I can give you a lift to the crossroads."

August nodded, slung the guitar over his shoulder, and climbed onto the cart. The horse moved forward again, and the wheels creaked along the road. Sacks of flour lay in the cart, tied with twine, and a couple of wooden barrels that smelled of apples.

"Who are you?" the driver asked without turning around.

"A bard."

"Ah," he drawled. "I see. I wanted to be a bard myself once. Even bought a lute, but my hands weren't made for it. The song doesn't come out of me. And besides — I had a family; I had to feed my children. And songs don't feed anyone when there's no one to sing them. So I became a carter. Not a bad life either — the road, the wind, sometimes you meet someone like you."

August looked at the road stretching ahead.

"It isn't always about the hands."

"Well," the carter chuckled. "I figured that out myself. Figured it out quickly."

He fell silent, and they rode a while without a word, hearing only the creak of wheels and the horse's breathing. A forest appeared in the distance, and the road ducked into the shade of the trees, where the air turned cooler. Leaves rustled overhead, and in that sound there was something soothing, as though the trees were carrying on an unhurried conversation of their own.

The carter spoke again:

"You're from the southern coast? You have a different way of speaking."

"Yes," August said briefly.

"They say a city burned down there."

"I'm from the eastern part of the southern kingdom myself," the man went on. "But trading took me through those parts. I was in Alba-Mare. It was a fine city. White, by the sea."

August was silent a long time. Then he said:

"I'm from there."

The carter turned his head but said nothing. He only looked at him more carefully and nodded — without questions, without pity. There was something human in that look: an understanding that some things do not require words.

"Well, hold on," he said. "We'll be in the city by tomorrow evening."

They rode until dusk. The forest ended, and the road came out into open country again — fields of low grass waving in the wind, and scattered trees standing apart, like sentries. As the sun began to sink, the carter turned off the road to a small stream running between stones. The horse stopped at the water by itself, lowered its head, and began to drink.

"We'll camp here," the carter said, climbing down from the cart. "It's quiet, there's water. And trees all around — the wind won't reach us."

They built a fire. The carter took a pot from the cart, filled it from the stream, and set it to cook a stew — of flour, dried meat, and a handful of herbs he evidently kept under his seat out of habit. August broke off some bread and handed it to his companion. The man took it, nodded, and, looking at the fire, said:

"Do you play, bard?"

August nodded.

"Then play," the carter said. "It's dark. And quiet. Sometimes it's good to listen when there's no need to talk."

August took out the guitar. He was thinking which song to play, but his fingers found the right strings without deliberation. The melody was quiet and sorrowful, like wind touching water. He did not think of the past, did not try to express anything — he simply let the notes sound.

The stew bubbled on the fire, the horse stood nearby grazing, and in that simple, peaceful sound there was more rest than in all the taverns and ports he had seen. The carter sat motionless, watching the fire, and listened. When the melody faded, he closed his eyes for a moment, then raised them and said:

"You do it right, bard. Good music doesn't demand an answer. It speaks for itself."

August put the guitar away. They ate the stew — hot, thick, smelling of herbs and smoke. They ate in silence, and that silence was not heavy but companionable, as if they had known each other for years and had no need of extra words. After supper the carter packed his pipe, lit it, and sent the smoke up into the dark sky, where stars had begun to kindle one after another.

"You know," he said quietly, not looking at August, "sometimes I think it wasn't for nothing that I bought that lute after all. I never learned to play, true. But I learned to listen. And it turns out that's harder than playing."

August looked at him. The carter drew on the pipe, let out the smoke, and added:

"I have a son. He's twelve. He looks at me when I come home and waits for me to tell him about the road. About the cities, about the people, about what I've seen. And I tell him. Then he lies down and falls asleep, and I sit beside him and think: maybe someday he'll leave too. And that's good. It means the road hasn't ended."

August said nothing. The carter's words settled on his heart quietly, without weight. He thought that this man, who hauled flour and apples, who never learned to play the lute but had learned to listen — that he too was walking his own path. And his path was neither worse nor better, only different.

They fell silent, and the night wrapped them in its warm velvet cover.

The next morning the carter woke him early. The fire had long gone out, leaving only a grey handful of ash. The horse was already harnessed, and the carter sat on the bench waiting.

"Let's go," he said shortly. "The road doesn't wait."

They rode in silence. The morning was cool, and fog lay across the fields like water that could not make up its mind to rise. Little by little the fog dispersed, and ahead, beyond a field, walls appeared. Tall, stone, with towers at the corners. The city.

By the time the sun stood high, they reached the crossroads. The carter stopped the horse and nodded toward the road that went on past the hills:

"Straight on from here. In a couple of hours you'll be in the city. There's a big square there where musicians gather. Look for it — maybe you'll find your place."

August jumped down from the cart, settled the guitar on his shoulder, and said:

"Thank you."

"For what?" the carter asked, surprised.

"For the silence, where I could simply be."

The carter smiled, slapped the horse's flank, and set off:

"Then hold on to your silence, bard. It won't let you down."

August watched the cart slowly dwindle down the road to the west. Then he turned and walked toward the city that could already be seen on the horizon. Its walls gleamed white in the morning light, and in that there was something that made him stop for a moment. A white city. Not Alba-Mare, but white all the same. He stood a while, looking at those walls, then drew a deep breath and went on.
