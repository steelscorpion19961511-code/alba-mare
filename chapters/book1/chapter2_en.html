Thirteen years is the age when the world begins to open anew. That is when August first heard the sound that forever changed his life.

It happened in the square, on the day when a foreign merchant ship entered the port. Its ivory sails and a hull decorated with unprecedented carvings attracted the attention of the whole city.

Such ships came rarely — mostly in the season when fruits ripened in Alba-Mare. Here they grew pomegranates, dates, lemons, oranges and a special sort of sweet grapes. Merchants especially valued dried figs, sweet candied fruits and fruit wines.

But their appearance was not only a trading event. People gathered at the pier — not for goods, but for a miracle. For the smells of distant lands, for names that no one had heard, for the faces of those who spoke an unfamiliar language. There was something festive about these ships — they brought not only goods, but also stories that were then retold in taverns until the next season.

August ran to the square when the crowd had already dispersed. He hoped to see foreigners, but the sight that opened before him surpassed all expectations.

On the stone elevation where merchants usually performed, sat a young man of about twenty-five. In his hands he held an instrument that August had never seen: a curved body, six strings, a long neck over which his fingers moved easily and confidently.

The man did not sing. He simply played. And the sounds he produced were like the voice of the city, but now this voice had words that the heart could understand. The music poured over the square, broke against the walls of houses and returned, like a tide. August did not know what melody it was, but it spoke of what he could never put into words: of distant shores, of silence on the open sea, of what remains when you are left alone.

Standing motionless, he forgot that there were people around, forgot that he needed to breathe. His gaze was riveted to the foreigner's fingers, to the way they touched the strings, and something was rising inside him that he could not name. Desire? Envy? Or simply the realization that he, too, wanted to speak like that.

The music ended as suddenly as it had begun. The foreigner removed his hands from the strings, and the square, which had just held its breath, came to life again — voices rang out, carts creaked, seagulls cried.

August wanted to approach him, ask his name, tell him about this instrument and the music, but a voice stopped him:

— He plays beautifully, doesn't he?

Turning around, he saw a merchant — the very one from the trading ship who sold strange things. He was smiling and looking at the young man with mild curiosity. His face was weathered, and his hands were large and rough, but in his eyes burned a quiet, attentive fire.

— There are no such instruments in these parts anymore, — said the merchant, noticing August's gaze. — Want to take a closer look?

August nodded, unable to say a word.

The merchant pointed to the instrument that the foreigner had placed on the ground near the goods chest.

— This is a guitar, — he said. — Its homeland is beyond the sea. Where it was made, it is called "the voice of the wind".

August's gaze slid over the instrument. Its wood was warm, honey-colored, almost golden. On the neck were spots — traces of fingers left by the one who had played it before. The guitar was not new, but it was alive.

— How much does it cost? — he asked.

The merchant grinned:

— A lot. Very much. A thousand copper coins. Your father doesn't have that kind of money.

August's fists clenched. A thousand copper coins. He knew how much his father earned a month — two hundred coins, three hundred in a good month. And he knew his father wouldn't give him a single one if he found out why they were needed.

— But, — said the merchant, noticing his face, — I can make a deal.

— How? — asked August.

— You look like a person who knows how to work. Fishing, helping at the pier, something else?

August was silent.

— So, — continued the merchant, — I came for fruits and wines, for which your city is famous. I myself trade in spices, oils, fabrics and strange things that I bring from the eastern lands. But my ship needs repairs: the seams need to be filled with tar, the sails patched, and loading and unloading will take more than one day. Two days of work, and I can count it towards the payment. And if you ask your father to help, maybe I'll give you this guitar for half the price.

His heart beat faster. This was a chance. A small one, but real.

— I agree, — said August, almost not hearing his own voice.

The merchant looked at him attentively, as if seeing him for the first time. Something flashed in his eyes — respect, or perhaps a memory of something long past.

— My name is Matteo, — he said. — What should I call you?

— August, — he answered.

— Well, August, come tomorrow at dawn. I'll show you what to do.

The day flew by in an instant — he hardly remembered what he did, who he saw, what he talked about. Everything merged into one expectation.

At night sleep did not come. Tossing and turning and looking at the ceiling, he worried that he would oversleep or that his father would not let him go to the pier. In the sleepless silence he listened to the sea breathing outside the window. Every splash, every breath of water — and in this rhythm he fancied the voice of that very guitar that he had not yet held in his hands. He saw it before him: warm wood, darkened neck, strings that awaited his fingers. He did not know how to play it. But he already knew it was his.

He never managed to fall asleep.

When it began to dawn outside the window, there was that special silence in the house that happens only before dawn, when even the sea seems asleep. Dressing hastily, trying not to make noise, he went out into the yard. The air was damp and cool, it smelled of wet grass and salt. Pausing for a moment, he glanced at the stars that had not yet gone out, and felt a strange excitement rising inside him — a mixture of fear and hope.

Voices could already be heard at the pier. The ship stood at the dock — old, but sturdy, with faded sails. Work was in full swing on the deck: sailors dragged bales, hammers knocked, someone swore in an unfamiliar language. Approaching the gangway, August stopped, not knowing where to go.

Matteo appeared from the hold — without a jacket, in just a shirt, with rolled-up sleeves.

— You're early, — he said. — Start by taking these sacks to the warehouse, — he pointed at them with his hand. — Then I'll tell you what's next.

— Okay, — August answered and got to work.

Two days merged into one long, drawn-out moment. He carried sacks of spices, rolled logs for repairing the hull, dragged barrels of oil. He helped pour tar into the seams between the boards, stretched sails together with the sailors, replaced weakened ropes. And in the evening, when the others dispersed, he scrubbed the deck — the boards were warm from the sun, and the water he poured from the bucket dried quickly in the wind. His hands were covered with abrasions, his back ached, his eyes stung from sweat. But he did not complain — he simply did what he was told and waited for evening to come.

August never asked his father for help. He didn't even tell him about the deal. He knew his father wouldn't understand, and might even forbid it. So he went alone. And he worked as if everything depended on it. By the end of the second day he could barely feel his hands. Sitting on his haunches by the side and holding a heavy piece of rope, he was trying to untangle it when footsteps sounded behind him.

— You didn't stop even once, — said Matteo.

August raised his head. Dirty, sweaty, with shadows under his eyes.

— I want to earn this guitar, — he answered quietly.

Matteo looked at him for a long moment. He grinned — not mockingly, but rather with respect.

— Then take it.

The guitar lay in his hands — warm, as if alive. In the rays of the setting sun, golden reflections played on the soundboard. Taking it, August felt the ground go out from under his feet. He could not find words.

— But I... haven't finished... — he mumbled, not raising his eyes.

— You have finished, — said Matteo. — I saw. You did more than I asked. You didn't ask for water, didn't shirk, didn't complain. I'm giving it to you not for the work. I'm giving it to you for not giving up.

Standing on the pier and pressing the guitar to himself, August felt something warm, almost unfamiliar, rising inside him. On his tired hands, on his worn palms lay his first real instrument — and it was worth every hour of work, every drop of sweat, every abrasion and that sleepless night when he was afraid everything would collapse.

Matteo grinned, looking at him.

— I'm not giving it to you, — he said. — I'm leaving it with you for a time.

August felt the ground go out from under his feet.

— But you said...

— I said it's yours, — Matteo interrupted. — And it will be yours. In a year I will return to Alba-Mare. If in that time you learn to play so that at least one person in this city cries or smiles listening to you — it's yours. For free. And if not — I'll take it and sell it in another city.

August was silent, not believing his ears.

— Why? — he asked quietly.

Matteo was silent. He looked at the sea, at the horizon where his ship was almost disappearing. He did not tell August that he would not return. That this year was not a deadline, but an excuse. That he did not need the guitar, and did not need money. He needed this boy to believe in himself.

Instead he said:

— Because I had a son. He also dreamed of playing. He was also a boy when I first saw a guitar in his hands. I thought it was empty. I thought he needed money, land, a trade. And I told him: first earn, then play. And he went to earn. And he never returned.

August looked at him, afraid to move.

— Now I don't need money, — said Matteo quietly. — I need someone to prove that I was wrong. Learn to play, boy. Not for me, for yourself.

Turning, he walked towards the ship. He took a few steps, then stopped. He did not turn around, but froze, looking at the water, as if remembering something.

— And remember, — he said, not raising his voice, but August heard him clearly, as if they were standing next to each other. — An instrument is just wood and strings. The main thing is inside. Don't hide it anywhere. Never.

August wanted to ask what he meant. He wanted to shout after him, to make him explain. But the words would not come. Standing with the guitar in his hands, he looked at the merchant's broad back, which was slowly rising up the gangway, and felt something viscous, warm, almost painful boiling inside him.

He did not know that it was called gratitude. He simply knew: he would remember this man forever.

Matteo had already disappeared on the deck. And a moment later, when August had already decided that he had left, a voice came from behind the side:

— In a year I will return. You will know how to play, won't you?

August opened his mouth, but no sound came out. Then he simply shouted in response — hoarsely, almost a breath:

— I will.

Silence hung over the water. And in this silence he heard a boat creak somewhere in the port, a seagull cry over the pier, the wind touching the strings that he did not yet know how to tune. And it seemed to him that the guitar in his hands grew a little warmer — as if it, too, was listening.

Standing in the empty square, clutching the warm wood in his hands, he watched as the ship slowly departed. White ivory sails filled with wind, the hull rocked on the waves, and his gaze followed it until it turned into a small dot on the horizon. The sea was calm, and light ripples on its surface spread in circles, as if the water, too, was learning to respond to touch.

August gripped the guitar tighter. For the first time in his life he knew what he would do tomorrow. And the day after. And every day until he learned to play so that he would be heard. He did not know then that Matteo would not return. That this year was not a deadline, but a gift. That he would never see this man again, but would carry his voice within him — in every note, in every chord, in every song he would one day compose.

But he knew something else. He knew he would play. He knew he must. And for the first time in thirteen years he felt not like a boy who listens, but like a man who can answer.
