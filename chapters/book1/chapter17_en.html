He had spent almost a month aboard the ship. Three and a half weeks, if counted exactly. Enough to grow used to the roll, the salty wind, the creak of the boards, and to faces that had become almost kin. He knew which of the sailors woke first, who could not fall asleep longest, who sang softly at night when he thought no one could hear. In that time he had become not merely a passenger — he had become part of this crew.

He worked alongside the others, sometimes marveling at how quickly hard labor and monotonous routine could knit together utterly different people. They already knew his habits, knew that he did not drink with them but sometimes took up the guitar and played in the twilight. August did not try to grow close, but between him and these people a silent understanding had arisen. They had almost grown used to his quiet presence, to his manner of keeping slightly apart — and he had not noticed when he had ceased to be a stranger to them.

On the eastern horizon a dark band appeared. At first it was barely visible — a thin line between water and sky. But with every hour it grew sharper, closer. The captain stood at the helm, gazing ahead without looking away. August came up to him and asked, without breaking the silence:

"Is that land?"

The captain nodded.

"In a few hours we'll be there. Behind that shore begins the south. What you were seeking."

August watched the approaching line of land. It was dark, almost black in the morning light. He gripped the rail, feeling something rise in him that he could not name. It was not simply relief that the voyage was over, nor only the anticipation of a new road. Anxiety and a strange hope mingled in that feeling — as if the land itself were calling him, yet promising nothing good.

The ship made for the shore.

When the vessel drew up to a long wooden pier, August stood at the side, watching the land that approached with every second. It was not what he had imagined. Green hills, white houses on the slopes, narrow lanes climbing up from the sea. The water here was lighter than in the north, and even the air seemed different — warmer, denser, as if steeped in the spiced scents of southern herbs.

He stepped ashore, feeling the boards of the pier give a slight spring under his feet. Solid ground, wood, the smell of grass and flowers — all of it crashed over him, reminding him of a world he had nearly forgotten. He had last stood on solid earth a few weeks ago, in Windholm. Now it felt as if he were setting foot on a new continent.

He stood a moment, letting his legs accustom themselves to stillness, and walked slowly up the street. The town was not large, but it felt alive: doors were opening, voices carried, someone carried baskets of fruit, and a boy of about ten, catching sight of August, froze for a second studying him, then tore off down the street, loudly calling to a friend. August smiled at that moment — there was something real in it, untouched by either storms or swords.

The town went about its usual life, and in that ordinariness there was a kind of solace. He walked without knowing where he was going, yet feeling a strange certainty inside: he was on the right road.

The captain stayed aboard, preparing to sail again, but before August left, he came up to him, clapped him on the shoulder and said:

"Well done, lad. The road ahead is yours now."

August thanked him and bade the sailors farewell. When his feet touched the land, he understood that everything before — the sea, the storms, the raiders — no longer mattered. Something new was beginning.

He wandered the town until he came upon an old fisherman's tavern. Inside it smelled of smoke, of the sea, of fried fish. He sat in a corner, asked for a mug of warm wine, and only then allowed himself to breathe.

An old man with a grey beard, dressed in a faded fisherman's jacket, came up to him. He looked at August, then at the guitar, then at him again. There was neither curiosity nor importunity in his gaze — only the calm attentiveness of a man used to noticing details.

"I hear you're looking for refugees from the north," he said, without preamble.

August raised his head and met his gaze. His heart clenched as if in a sudden gust of wind.

"Do you know of them?"

"I do," the old man answered. "They were here. Not all of them stayed, but some settled in a village to the east. They speak of a woman from the north who was looking for her son."

August froze. He gripped the mug so hard his knuckles went white.

"What was her name?"

The old man shrugged:

"I don't know the name. I heard her story from a fisherman. She came on a ship with other refugees, asking about a man with a guitar. I remembered it because she was like you — just as weary, but still walking."

August was silent for a long time. He looked into the mug, feeling the warm wine send warmth into his chest, but that warmth could not melt the icy weight that had suddenly settled on his heart. He thought of his mother — of how she might have walked these same streets, asked the same people, looked at the same hills. And the thought made it at once easier and more painful: he was walking in her footsteps.

Then he stood and asked the way to that village.

The old man explained the road — in simple, clear words, without needless detail. He did not ask why August was going there, and for that August was grateful to him.

"Thank you," August said quietly.

He left several coins on the table and walked out of the tavern, looking east, toward where the new road began. The sun already stood high, and the shadows of the houses lay across the roadway as if pointing the way. August breathed in the southern air, tried to hold on to the strange feeling that had come over him at the ship's rail — and walked on.
