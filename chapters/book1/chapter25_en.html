He woke on the fifth day with the feeling that his body had finally stopped being foreign to him. Weakness still lingered, but it was no longer that viscous, helpless heaviness that had pinned him to the bed. He managed to sit up unaided, to reach the mug of water standing on a chair beside the bed. He drank slowly, feeling the cool water spread through him, drop by drop returning the sensation of being alive. Each swallow seemed a small miracle — as if he were learning all over again how to be alive.

Outside it was gray. The village lived its quiet winter life: voices were rare, sometimes came the knock of an axe, sometimes the creak of a cart over frozen ground. August sat on the bed and listened to this world, so unlike the one where he had grown up. There, in Alba-Mare, the air always smelled of salt and wind; here other smells reigned: cold smoke from the stoves, damp wool, wet wood, and the barely perceptible breath of earth hidden beneath a crust of ice. He breathed them in and tried to grow used to this new quiet — not empty, but filled with the restrained sounds of someone else's daily life.

Elsa came in several times a day. She brought him hot brew, fresh bread, a piece of cheese. She said little, but her presence soothed. Her hands were warm and sure as she straightened the blanket or laid her palm against his forehead, checking whether the fever had returned. In her movements there was no fuss — only the measuredness of a person who knows the worth of every gesture.

'You are mending,' she said one morning. 'A few more days, and you will be able to go out.'

He nodded, feeling impatience rise within him. He wanted to move, wanted to take the guitar in his hands again, wanted to see the road. But he knew that haste now was a sure path back to bed. So he endured, forcing himself to stay where he was, though every fiber of his body strained forward.

Toward the end of the first week he stepped out onto the porch for the first time. Pushing open the door, he walked outside. The air struck his face — cold, clean, smelling of smoke and frozen earth. Drawing a full breath of it, he felt his lungs expand, his blood move faster, life return to his body, waking every muscle.

The village of Old Bridge lay spread before him — small, almost toylike, with low houses roofed in shingles and narrow streets covered in frozen mud. It stood in a hollow, ringed by low hills, bare and dark, as if sketched in charcoal against the gray sky. Smoke curled above the roofs, rising lazily and dissolving into the overcast air. Somewhere below a river ran — he could hear it, a steady, lulling sound like a quiet, endless whisper.

He stood on the porch, feeling the cold air touch his face, and for the first time in a long while it seemed to him that he was in no hurry at all. Time here had slowed, as if, obeying the rhythm of the wind and the knock of drops falling from the roofs.

On the eighth day he felt strong enough to walk through the village. Pulling on the new cloak left on the chair, he stepped carefully past the gate. The streets were deserted; only now and then did he meet local folk — they nodded to him but did not speak. Everything here was slower, simpler, as though time flowed differently, keeping to the breath of the wind rather than the hurry of people.

By the well children were playing — three or four of them, in coarse woolen jackets, cheeks flushed with cold. They stopped when he passed and watched him with the curiosity with which a small village watches a stranger. One of them, a boy with tousled fair hair, stepped forward and asked:

'Are you the one lying at Elsa's?'

'Yes,' August answered.

'Are you getting better?'

'Almost,' he said.

The boy smiled, showing a gap-toothed grin, and waved a hand, inviting him to come over. August walked to the well, looked into its dark depths, and they stood side by side, watching the light break on the surface, scattering into thousands of trembling shards. In this simple moment there was something wonderfully right — as if he had finally found a place where one could simply stand and look at the water.

He walked past a small pond where ducks, sheltered in the reeds, waited for the ice to melt, and turned toward the center of the village. Everything here smelled of smoke, wet earth, and something else, elusive, that was part of the place. He was already beginning to grow used to it — to this smell, to this quiet — and it no longer seemed foreign to him.

In the center stood a small square with a well and a wooden bench. Beside it stood a tavern, a low building with a soot-blackened chimney and a sign reading 'By the Old Bridge.' The letters had darkened with time, the paint peeling in places, but the writing was still legible. August's gaze lingered on it, but he did not go in. He decided to wait until he was fully strong again — not only in body but in spirit — so that he might enter not as a sick man carried in on a cart, but as a person ready to become part of someone else's conversation once more.

He passed stalls where coarse woolen cloth lay, smoked fish, clay pots and bundles of dried herbs. On one of them he saw the same herbs that hung in Elsa's house — he stopped for a moment, breathed in their bitter, familiar scent, but did not buy. He simply took the smell with him, as part of this place, as another line in a song he could not yet play but already heard with his inner ear.

When he returned to the house, Elsa was waiting for him. She set before him a mug of hot milk and a piece of bread, fresh, with a crisp crust. She did not ask where he had been, said not a word about his walking too long. In her silence there was no indifference — only respect for his right to make his own steps in his own time. She simply left her care on the table — warm and wordless — and went out into the yard.

August sat by the stove, watching the fire, feeling the warmth from the mug spread through his fingers, warming not only skin but something within. Little by little the village was becoming real to him, warm and alive — not merely a place where he was being healed, but a place that was beginning to know him, if only by inches, day by day.
