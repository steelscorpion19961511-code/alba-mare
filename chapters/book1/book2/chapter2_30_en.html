A month later they put out of the port.

The morning was cold and windy, and the sea looked grey and restless — as though the sky itself frowned, seeing the travelers off into the unknown. Waves struck the hull, scattering into spray that the wind carried away at once. August stood at the side and watched as Al-Sharan slowly vanished over the horizon. The houses grew smaller and smaller, the towers seemed to draw in their heads, hiding from the gathering storm, and the golden domes dimmed, losing their shine in the grey light of the early day.

The city that had given him a new guitar, new clothes, a new life. The city where he had learned to be himself again, where out of a shadow barely distinguishable from the night dark he had become a man with a name, a trade, and a voice. Now that name sounded not only inside his own head — he was known at the Nanna Tavern, at the quay, in the shops and at the markets. And that was worth more than any fame.

He thought of how much had changed since the day he first set foot on this shore. Then he had been an exhausted, lost man, without gear and without hope. He could barely stand when Matteo had led him into his house. His clothes had turned to rags, his body was worn out, and in his eyes there was an emptiness that even the years of survival could not fill. He remembered sitting in the hammam, feeling the water wash away layers of dirt, sweat and sea salt, as though it tried to wash away the weight he carried inside as well. He remembered looking into the mirror and not recognizing himself: before him stood a stranger, a stranger even to himself.

Now he had a purpose. He had a guitar, his father's sword, new boots, a scabbard, a case, a traveling sack full of provisions. And he had a friend. A true friend who never asked how much he owed, never reminded him of the debt, but was simply there — in silence, in work, in talk, in stillness. That had been enough to learn to breathe again.

Matteo came up, stood beside him, and also looked at the shore. They were silent, feeling the city fall behind, feeling the distance between themselves and that place become not merely a number in sea miles but something greater — a boundary that cannot be crossed twice. Behind them the crew readied the ship: sailors checked the rigging, hauled the ropes taut, shouted short commands, and the wind was already filling the sails, making the vessel slowly pull away from the pier. Somewhere on shore gulls cried, and their voices mingled with the voices of those seeing them off, with the noise of the waves and the creak of the wood. August searched for no one in the crowd. He had already said farewell to everyone dear to him: to the keeper of the Nanna, to the fishermen, to the boys who ran along the pier, to the women selling spices. He had said farewell not in words — in glances, in nods, in quiet "until we meet again" that might never come true.

August turned to Matteo:

“Thank you,” he said, and his voice came out slightly hoarse, as though the words were catching on something inside and would not leave. “For everything. For the guitar. For saving me on the island. For believing in me then, in Alba-Mare, and for believing now.”

Matteo smiled, shaking his head. The wind played with his grey beard, twisting separate strands as though trying to weave them into a story. He narrowed his eyes, gazing at the horizon, where the sky merged with the sea, and August thought that in all these years he had rarely seen Matteo so calm — as though he had finally done what he had to do and could now simply stand and watch the ship depart. In his eyes the light of the morning sun was reflected, golden and pure, like a promise.

“I did not believe in you then,” Matteo said calmly, without pathos, simply as a fact that had long ceased to wound. “I merely gave you a chance. And you did everything else yourself. You worked, you learned, you did not give up when it would have been easier to give up. I simply stood nearby and watched.”

He laid a hand on August's shoulder and squeezed it. His palm was warm and dry, and in the gesture there was more than in any words: the weight of the years lived, the gentleness of understanding, and the firmness of support.

“Do you know what I have understood over these years?” Matteo went on, without taking his hand away. “Fate is not what happens to you. It is what you choose. You chose to go. You chose not to give up. You chose to become what you have become. I simply stood nearby. And I am proud that I stood nearby.”

August smiled in return. He felt Al-Sharan falling behind, and inside him spreading a quiet, assured calm — not serenity, but readiness. He did not know whether he would return here. But he knew the place would remain with him forever: in the sound of the strings, in the smell of spices, in the noise of the city that would now sound in his memory whenever around him there was only wind and sea.

The ship turned west. The wind was fair, filling the sails with force, and Al-Sharan gradually became a thin golden line on the horizon, then a barely visible shadow, and then dissolved altogether in the shimmering haze. August stood at the stern, holding his new guitar in its new case, and felt a new stage of the road begin. The wood under his fingers was warm, familiar, as though the guitar had already become part of himself.

He thought of how once he had fled Alba-Mare with one guitar and his father's sword. Then he had not known where he was going. He had simply run from the fire, from the screams, from what could not be borne. Now he knew where he was going. And that knowledge was both light and heavy at once: it gave direction but took away the right to uncertainty. He did not know what awaited him in the western North. Did not know whether he would find answers there, or only new questions. Did not know whether the one who had united the tribes would hear him, or whether his words would drown in the cold wind. But he knew he was ready. Ready for the cold, for the silence, for the fact that they would not want to listen. Ready even for the fact that he might have to speak not with words but with deeds.

And he knew one thing more: he was not going for vengeance. Vengeance would have been easier. Vengeance would not have demanded so much patience, so much understanding, so many words. He was going for an answer. For the thing that would help him fit the shards of his past into something whole. Or at least understand why those shards had scattered at all.

The wind ruffled his hair, salt spray settled on his skin, and the ship carried him forward — to where the ridge pushed into the sky, where the sea grew wild and the land grew stern. Ahead was the North. Ahead was the road that had waited for him seventeen years. And now he walked it not as a fugitive, but as a man who had finally found where he needed to arrive.
