He left in silence. The snow creaked underfoot, and that sound was the only thing breaking the morning stillness. Behind him remained the village where he had lived three winters. He did not look back — he had already said farewell. Truly, not in words, but in the way he had made his decision.

Before the road he had stopped to see Ulrik in the Trading Settlement. Ulrik kept a shop right by the highway — a small one, but dependable, like the man himself. Everyone knew him: he did not cheat, he did not hurry, and he knew how to keep not only goods but other people's secrets.

August slipped the pack off his shoulder, took out the elder's manuscript and laid it on the table. Thick pages, a dark leather cover, a silver clasp already slightly dulled by time. The book seemed heavier than it actually was.

“There is one thing I would ask of you,” said August. “Keep it for me.”

Ulrik raised his eyes from his accounts. He did not reach for the book at once — he looked at August, as if trying to understand what lay behind the request.

“It is not merely a book,” August added. “It is a chronicle. Tales, customs, omens. The memory of how people live in the North.”

“Are you afraid it will be stolen?” asked Ulrik, tilting his head slightly.

“I am afraid that I will stop hearing it myself,” August answered quietly. “If it stays with me, I will begin seeking the answer to every step in its pages. And I need to seek them on my own. Where I am going, northern tales will not help. Only my hands and what I have already learned.”

Ulrik took the manuscript carefully and ran a finger over the embossing on the cover, as if checking whether it was real.

“It will be here,” he said calmly. “In my cabinet, under lock. No one will touch it.”

“I promise to come back for it,” August nodded.

“Don't stay gone too long,” Ulrik added, returning to his accounts, but there was something warm, almost fatherly, in his voice.

Now August traveled light: with him were only his experience, the guitar, his father's sword, and a memory that already lived within him rather than on pages. The northern tales remained in safe hands, waiting for his return.

The road led southeast, toward lands where the ground changed by degrees and the air grew milder. His pace was unhurried but steady — the kind that holds true for a day and for a week alike. His body had grown used to the rhythm: morning, march, a short rest, another march, the evening fire. It knew on its own when to stop, when to quicken, when simply to stand and listen to the wind.

The first day was easy. The second — the same. The land was changing: the snow grew thinner, the stones sharper, the air drier. Passes, frozen streams, ridges where the wind knocked you off your feet. But he could walk against the wind — he had learned that in the North. Bjorn had once shown him: do not lean forward, clutching at the air; stand upright and let the wind flow around you like water. It worked.

On the third day he came upon an old stone pillar. On one side — snow, rocks, cold. On the other, the tundra began. Bare, dark, damp. The smell of earth, not ice.

He stopped at the pillar and ran his fingers over its surface. The stone was rough, cold, but not icy. The runes were the same as in the North. August did not know what they meant. He felt it: this was a border. Not between lands — between stages.

He stepped over it.

The ground beneath his feet grew softer, more yielding. The snow vanished, giving way to moss, stones and sparse, low grass. The wind became different — damp, warm. It smelled of sea and earth mingled together. He stood still for a moment, breathing it in. It was not southern and not northern. It was something new.

He found a flat stone by a small stream, carefully set down the leather case with the guitar and sat beside it. He removed the upper fur cloak — heavy, smelling of smoke and snow — rolled it up neatly and strapped it to his pack. He took a drink from the waterskin and kneaded his stiff shoulders. His body was fine — it had grown used to the long road. He sat and looked around. Everything was different. Not merely other — different in its very essence.

Here there were no crags hanging over the path. No eternal snow. Instead — boundless, rolling land covered with moss and low growth, with sparse stones jutting from it like the teeth of an old beast. The sky was not grey but deep blue, with clouds that did not crawl but sailed.

Here there was no road — not in the sense he was used to: rutted, trodden down by carts. Only paths — made by beasts, old, barely visible. He followed them because he knew: they led to water. And water led to people. But he had seen no people yet. Only smoke in the distance. Only tracks. Only a silence that did not press — it simply was. Like a space that demands no explanations.

He sat a while longer, listening to the stream flow. Then he rose, tightened the straps, adjusted the guitar and walked on.

Seven days after crossing the border, he saw the forest for the first time. A true forest — tall trees, deep shade, the smell of damp earth and rotting leaves. In the North the trees were low, twisted by the winds, like old men bent by so many blows they could no longer straighten. Here they stood upright, calm, as if frost had never threatened them. Once inside, the trees closed overhead, and the light grew softer, greener. The grass — tall, wet — brushed against his boots, leaving traces on the leather. He walked along a barely visible path, listening to the forest. He was not seeking danger or shelter — he simply listened, as the North had taught him: not with his ears, but with his whole body.

By day's end he found a spot by a forest stream — small, but swift, with clean cold water. He dropped his gear and sat on the bank. His provisions had run out two days earlier — a few hardtack biscuits he had been rationing, and a piece of dried meat he had carried with care. There was water, but his strength was fading. He knew: he had to stop.

He stripped off his clothes, left them on a stone, and stepped into the stream. The water burned with cold, but he did not flinch — the North had taught him not to fear the cold. He washed, rinsing the road dust from his face, feeling the coolness sink beneath the skin, bracing him, bringing back clarity. Coming out onto the bank, he sat on a sun-warmed stone for a long time, watching the water run between his fingers. When his skin had dried in the soft rays, August dressed, gathered firewood — which lay scattered almost everywhere here — and built a fire.

He sat long by the fire, watching the tongues of flame. Listening to the forest. All around was quiet — only the stream and a light breeze in the crowns.

A sudden rustle of leaves nearby put him on alert. Half-rising, he was already ready to draw the blade — his hand was on the hilt before his eyes found the source of the sound. That is how the North had taught him: first the body, then the thought.

A deer. On the far bank — a big one, with branching antlers, its coat glinting copper in the sunset light. It froze upon noticing the man. It did not move; it watched him. August felt calm rise within him — not from pity, but from understanding. He could have killed it easily. He could have. But the deer was too large for one man. Even dried, the surplus would be too heavy a load, one that would slow the journey. The North had taught him to take no excess, to take only what he could carry. Not to kill what would not become food. Bjorn used to say: “Every beast you kill and do not eat is a debt you will pay in winter, when food is scarce.”

The deer vanished into the thicket as suddenly as it had appeared. He did not regret it. He only smiled — not widely, with the corners of his lips — and returned to the fire.

An hour later he spotted hares. Several — by an old stump, not far from the stream. They were small and quick, but he knew how to approach them quietly. He drew the knife Bjorn had given him back in the North — a good, sturdy one, with a handle of elk bone and a blade of dark metal. He did not often take it out, but he remembered how it had first settled into his hand. Bjorn had said then: “This is not for battle. This is for living.” He held it and felt the familiar weight — the weight of a thing that had become an extension of his palm.

He killed two hares — quickly, without superfluous movement. Without suffering. The North had taught him this too: to kill cleanly, respecting the quarry, not prolonging another creature's pain. Now there was meat.

He returned to the fire, dressed one, skewered it on a spit made from a fresh branch and began roasting. The second he carefully cut into thin strips to dry. While the meat cooked, he turned to the pelts. He removed them carefully, cleaned away the remains of flesh, rinsed them in the stream and bound them together with a piece of linen cord he carried in his pack.

In the morning, when he broke camp, he would hang them from his belt — they would come in handy. For clothing, for warmth, for something else.

He dined by the fire, watching the flames, feeling his strength return. The meat was hot and rich, smelling of smoke and herbs. He ate slowly, savoring. Not because he was hungry — but because he knew how to value it. The North had taught him this too: every morsel of food is a victory over emptiness, and it must be treated with respect.

At night he slept by the fire, wrapped in his cloak. The forest was not alien. It breathed evenly, like the breathing of an old friend. Not one you have known for years, but one you are only beginning to know — and already you feel it will do you no harm.

In the morning he continued on. He took no excess. He took only what he could carry. That is how the North had taught him, where every extra gram is a step that might be his last.
