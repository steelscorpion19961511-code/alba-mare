Time on the island moved differently. Days folded into weeks, weeks into months. He stopped counting them. There was no point. He simply lived — rose at dawn, went to the stream, sought food, mended the house, watched the sea.

At first he still tried to keep count. Notches on a stone, knots on a rope. But after several months he gave it up. Not because he had surrendered. He simply understood: on the island it does not matter what day it is. Only one thing matters — whether you live to see the evening.

Little by little he learned to read this world. He understood when the rainy season came and the stream grew full. When the fish came closer to the shore and could be speared in the shallows. When the wild fruit ripened, and when it turned poisonous. Time could be guessed from the smell of the wind, from the movement of clouds, from the cries of birds on the rocks.

The horizon no longer held him. In the first weeks he climbed the hill every day. Looked at the sea, waited. Then — every other day. Then — only when he happened to be near. Then he stopped. Not because he had lost hope. He simply understood: if it is fated, the ship will come. And if not — there is no point spending strength on it.

All that remained was to accept what is.

The days grew alike. Rising at dawn, the stream, washing, a swallow of water. Then the round of traps set for crabs and small animals. Fishing with a spear — a long stick with a sharpened end, cut with the knife that had miraculously survived the shipwreck. Fire was made by friction — hard, but familiar. Then flint turned up, and it grew easier: sparks were struck before the first wisp of smoke.

The house demanded care almost every day. Walls were reinforced, the roof relaid. Life on the island demanded not strength but constancy. He grew thinner in body but stronger in spirit. He stopped fearing the silence. He stopped flinching at the cries of birds. He stopped speaking aloud — words became unnecessary.

But in the evenings he spoke with the guitar.

It remained the only thread binding him to the past. Old, darkened by damp, with cracks in the body and gut strings he guarded as best he could. He played softly, fearing the wind would carry the sound away. But he played. Every evening. Even when his fingers bled, even when the sound grew muffled and discordant.

It was his voice.

He talked to it as to a living thing. Not with words — with sounds. He remembered how it had sounded in Alba-Mare when he was a boy. How it had sung in the square in Vistaria, how it had trembled in his hands in the cold northern wind. How it had been silent when he lay in fever with the healer. How it came alive again the moment one touched the strings.

While the guitar was near, solitude was not felt.

But time went on, and the guitar was dying.

First one string snapped. Then a second. Attempts to replace them with the dried sinews of small animals came to nothing — they would not hold tension. The wood warped from the damp, the neck bent, the frets began to fall out. Once he had to remove all the strings, hoping that after drying they would come back to life. They snapped one after another — right in his hands.

The guitar fell silent.

There were no tears. Only emptiness. That evening the fire never was lit. He sat in the dark, pressing the instrument to his chest, listening to the ocean breathe.

The next day he rose all the same. Went to the stream. Washed. Found food. Mended the house. Life went on, even when the music died.

From soft wood he carved a small figure — neither man nor beast nor spirit. He set it by the hearth. Sometimes he looked at it and said nothing. There was more in that silence than in words.

And then he began to sing. Without the guitar. At first quietly, almost in a whisper. Then louder. For no one. For himself. His voice, hoarse from long silence, gradually warmed. Old songs came back to him. The words returned. Sometimes new ones were born — of the sea, of the sky, of how a man can be alone and not go mad.

These songs were written down nowhere. They were simply kept within, like embers beneath the ash.

When the rainy season came, he built a new awning. When the sun came out again, he smiled for the first time in a long while. Not because anything had changed. He simply understood: he was alive. And that was enough.

One morning, coming out of the house, he saw a point on the horizon. Small, barely visible. He did not stand up, did not run. He simply watched it slowly grow.

The point did not disappear.

Then he rose, took his sword, checked that the knife was in place, and went to the shore. His heart beat evenly, without its former trembling. He no longer waited for rescue. He simply knew: if this was a ship — he would meet it standing.
