Three weeks had passed since they left the port where August killed the hyenas. The ship was still at sea. The wind was favourable, soft but stubborn, and they held their assigned course, keeping along the coast. The shore could barely be made out in the distance: a dark strip that appeared and vanished in the haze, as though the land could not decide whether to show itself. Sometimes, when they came closer, the outlines of hills could be discerned, the occasional flat-crowned tree, and once — the smoke of a distant fire, rising in a thin thread and dissolving into the sky at once. But that was all. No people, no boats, no piers. The shore was wild, silent, and seemed endless, as if someone had drawn a line along the edge of the earth and forgotten to finish it.

August had grown used to the sea. Not to the southern sea, not to the northern one — to this one: warm, salty, with a swell that rocked the ship evenly, slowly, the way a mother rocks a child. He had grown used to the smell of tar and wet wood, to the creak of the masts, to the way the sails flapped when the wind changed. He had grown used to the rhythm of life aboard: watch, work, sleep, watch again. His body knew by itself when to rise, when to lie down, when to come up on deck, when to go down into the hold.

He stood his watch, scanning the horizon, and thought how strangely life was made: three years ago he had stood on a northern shore, seeing the winter off, and now here he was, on a southern ship, meeting a sea not his own. Everything changed: the sky, the water, the wind, the language, the food. Only one thing did not change — that he was still walking. Bjorn had once said: "You are not a wanderer. You are a road. A wanderer stops when he finds his place. A road stops only when it ends." August had not understood those words then. Now, standing on deck and looking at the endless water, he felt he was beginning to.

The crew was small: twelve men, not counting the captain and the mate. The sailors were all different — some silent, some talkative, some young, some old — but united by one thing: they all knew the sea. They did not fear it, but they respected it. August watched how they worked the ropes, how they checked the knots, how a single glance at the water told them what was changing. They did not explain things to him; they simply showed, and that was enough: in the North, too, you were taught not with words but with deeds.

Among them was one who stood out. Hasan. A young sailor, maybe a little over twenty, with quick hands and lively eyes. He worked faster than anyone, but not out of zeal — out of love for the craft: every knot, every rope, every sail was for him not work but a kind of game in which he always knew the next move. He was the first to start talking to August — not out of curiosity, but because he wanted to know who this man was, with tattoos on his arms and a sword at his belt, who could both play the guitar and kill hyenas.

"Where are you from?" he asked once, as they mended a piece of rigging sitting on deck.

"From the north," said August.

"Far away," said Hasan, and there was no question in his voice, only a statement, as if he had known all along. "They say up there people are big and cruel. You're not cruel."

"I'm different," said August.

Hasan grunted and nodded, as if that were the best answer he had ever heard. After that they sometimes talked: briefly, without long stories, exchanging words the way people exchange tools when working at the same task. Hasan spoke of the ports he knew, of the winds that change with the seasons, of how to read clouds. August spoke of the North, of the snow, of a silence so deep you could hear your own heart. Hasan listened open-mouthed: he had never seen snow and could not imagine a world that was white and mute.

"And what do you do when it's cold?" he asked once, and there was no puzzlement in his voice, only genuine curiosity.

"We keep warm," said August. "We light fires, we eat hot food, we hold each other. The cold isn't an enemy. Just a neighbour you have to get used to."

Hasan shook his head, as if he could not believe anyone could speak of the cold that way. Then he asked:

"And if the cold is such that a fire doesn't help?"

August was silent for a moment. He remembered the night in the mountains when the wind knocked men off their feet and the cold fell so low that exhaled breath turned to icy dust. Bjorn had sat down beside him then, pressed his shoulder against his, and said: "Don't sleep. If you fall asleep, you won't wake. Talk to me. About anything."

"Then you warm yourselves against each other," said August. "Human warmth is the last thing left when everything else fails."

Hasan thought about that. Then he nodded, and in his glance flashed something August had not expected: not sympathy, but recognition, as if he too knew what it meant to stay alive thanks to someone else's warmth.

There was another sailor worth mentioning. Dariush. Old, silent, with a face tanned by salt and wind to the colour of old leather. He had been on the ship longer than anyone — longer even than the captain. He spoke little, but when he spoke, his words had the weight of stone. August noticed that even the captain listened to him, though he never showed it openly.

Dariush handled the sails as though they were living things: touched them carefully, smoothed the folds, checked every seam. Once August saw him standing at the rail, looking at the water, his lips moving, though no words could be heard. Perhaps he was praying. Perhaps talking to the sea. In the North they did that too: the shaman would go out to the rocks and speak with the wind. No one thought it strange. It simply was.

One day Dariush came up to August while he was mending a rope on deck. He stood in silence for a while, then said:

"You work well with your hands."

"The North taught me," August answered.

"In the North they teach you to work. At sea they teach you to survive," said Dariush. "Those are different things."

August looked at him. Dariush did not explain what he meant. He simply nodded — with that short, heavy nod that aboard a ship stood in for a whole speech — and went back to his sails. August thought about those words all day. He did not understand them at once, only toward evening, when a wave rocked the ship and everyone on deck grabbed for the nearest handhold. To work means to do your own task well. To survive means to know what to do when you are doing someone else's. Aboard, everyone did everything: a sailor might take the helm, the cook haul on a rope, and a passenger with a sword gut fish. Roles here were not rigid. They flowed like water, and he who could not flow with them became a burden.

The days fell into a rhythm August recognized and accepted. The morning began at dawn: the first watch roused the relief, and the deck filled with quiet, businesslike bustle. They checked the rigging, examined the sails, measured the water in the bilge. Breakfast was simple: flatbread, water, sometimes salted fish. They ate in silence or with a few short phrases, no more. Talk over food was not prized here: food was fuel, not pleasure. That was familiar to August — in the North they ate quickly and without extra words too.

By day the sun beat down until the deck burned, and men walked barefoot or in thick-soled shoes. There was almost no shade: the masts threw narrow bands of shadow that moved with the sun, and the off-duty sailors drifted after them like lizards. August did not hide from the sun. In the North he had learned not to fear the cold; here he was learning not to fear the heat. His body adapted: his skin darkened further, sweat troubled him less, his head grew clearer despite the swelter.

Sometimes, when the wind died and the ship nearly stopped, the sea around grew so calm it seemed frozen. The water turned to glass in which the sky was reflected so precisely that it was impossible to tell which way was up. They stood on deck, looked down, and saw clouds drifting beneath the keel. It was strange and beautiful, and in such moments August felt the world fall silent, as if it too wanted to rest.

In those becalmed hours August would take out his guitar. Not for the sailors — for himself. He would sit at the stern, where the shadow of the deckhouse gave coolness, and play softly, almost inaudibly, for himself and for the sea alone. His fingers found melodies that were born of themselves, without words, without a name, without a form: simply a sound that rose from within and went out into the water. Sometimes it seemed the sea was listening: the waves grew smoother, the wind hushed, and even the gulls that usually cried above the deck flew off somewhere, leaving him alone with the silence and the strings.

Once, in such a calm, he noticed a shadow in the water. Large, dark, it moved beside the ship, slowly, smoothly, as if escorting them. Dariush, standing at the rail, nodded:

"A whale. Big one. Won't touch you."

The shadow dove and vanished, but a minute later it appeared again, on the other side. Then another, and another. Three or four shadows gliding beneath the surface, calm and powerful. August watched them and felt what he had once felt watching the northern lights: awe before something greater that had no interest in men and their concerns. The wrinkled whale snorted, threw up a fountain of misty spray, and went down into the deep, and the rest followed. The sea became empty and quiet again, as if nothing had happened.

Hasan came over, watched them go, and said:

"Good omen. Whales mean luck."

"They say that in the North too," August answered. "About other beasts."

"About which?"

"About wolves. If a wolf crosses your road and does not stop, it means a long journey."

Hasan smirked.

"We say: if a wolf crosses your road — go home."

"Maybe we're both right," said August.

Hasan laughed. His laugh was short, sharp, like a bark, but warm. August did not laugh, but his lips stirred, and Hasan noticed.

In the fourth week the captain announced that they could go no farther: rocks, reefs, shoals stretching for many miles along the coast. They would have to go out into the open sea to round the dangerous stretch.

August heard the sailors talking among themselves. They were not afraid, but they spoke cautiously, as though preparing for something that could not be avoided. The open sea is always a risk.

No shore, no landmarks, no rescue if anything goes wrong. One of the old sailors, a silent man with a face cured by salt and wind, said quietly:

"Last time I sailed the open sea was three years ago. We lost two men then. Storm."

Another nodded but added nothing. Fear was not discussed here; a man simply carried it inside, the way he carried his scars: not hiding them, but not flaunting them either.

Hasan was the only one who spoke of it openly. Not out of boasting — out of honesty.

"I've never sailed the open sea," he told August one evening as they sat on deck. "Only along the coast. My father said: don't trust the open sea. It can be calm in the morning and kill you by evening."

"In the North they say the same about the mountains," said August. "A mountain can stand for a hundred years and then wake up and bury a village. You don't control it. You simply prepare, and you go."

Hasan looked at him.

"Aren't you afraid?"

"I am," said August. "But I don't stop."

Hasan nodded. This time without a smile. But with that quiet understanding that comes between people who have told each other the truth and accepted it.

August was not afraid. In the North he had learned: to fear what is inevitable is a waste of strength. You prepare, and you go. He checked his gear: sword in its sheath, Bjorn's knife, the pouch of herbs, water, provisions. Everything was in place. His body was ready. His mind was calm. He checked twice — a habit he could not shake: Bjorn taught that you check twice, even when you are sure everything is there. "The first time you check with your hands. The second time — with your head."

Before going into the open sea, the captain gathered the crew on deck. He spoke briefly, without extra words, as he did everything.

"We sail at dawn. Course — southeast. We round the reefs and return to the coast. In the open sea — doubled watches, everyone armed, everything lashed down. If there's a storm — no panic. Do as I say. Questions?"

Silence. No one asked anything. Everyone knew there was no need for questions. There was need for doing.

The captain nodded and went to the helm.

The ship turned southeast to round the reefs. Soon the shore disappeared entirely. Not gradually, not hazily, but completely: suddenly, within a single mile, the land was gone, and there was only water — endless, blue, alive. It was everywhere: ahead, behind, right, left, below. Even the sky seemed its continuation, as if sea and sky were one whole, divided only by the thin line of the horizon.

August stood at the rail and looked at that infinity. The wind tugged at his hair, salty spray settled on his face, and he felt something wake within him that he had not felt in a long time: a calm born of accepting the unknown and simply walking into it. You do not try to command the wind, you do not try to predict the wave, you do not try to peer beyond the horizon. You simply trust.

On the first day of the open sea, Hasan stood at the rail in silence. August came up and stood beside him. He said nothing. He simply stood. After a few minutes Hasan said quietly:

"It's big."

"Yes."

"And empty."

"Not empty," said August. "Just quiet."

Hasan looked at him, and there was something in his eyes August had not seen before: not fear, but realization. The realization that the world was larger than he had thought, and that he in it was a small dot which a wave could erase.

"That's good," said August. "A man who knows he is small does no stupid things."

Hasan nodded and moved away from the rail. But something in his movements had changed: he became more careful, more precise, as if the sea were teaching him what the North had taught August.

They held southeast for several days, then turned south, then west and northwest, skirting the dangerous stretch. The sea was calm, the sky clear, but the open sea gave no peace. It was different: not like the coastal water where waves run up the sand and recede, but bottomless, boundless, without a foothold. Here there was no sound of surf, no cries of gulls, no smell of land. Only wind, water, and a silence that was not empty but full: within it you could hear the breathing of the sea itself, deep and slow, like the heartbeat of some enormous beast.

Everything became stricter in the open sea. The watches lengthened: six hours instead of four, because there were few men and the sea forgives no inattention. Rest grew shorter, food sparser: the captain cut the portions so they would last the whole passage. Water was rationed: they drank in small sips, wetted their lips but did not quench their thirst fully. The body grew used to the lack the way it had grown used to the cold in the North: not resisting, but reordering itself.

The captain hardly slept, going over his charts and studying the stars, as though they spoke to him in a language no one else understood. August saw him standing at the helm at night, head thrown back, lips moving as he counted something. The stars here were different: not like those in the North, where the sky seemed low and close as a ceiling, but burning high and bright, as if they were not sparks of fire but eyes looking down with an impartial, eternal attention. August remembered how the shaman in the North had taught him to read the stars: not their names, not their constellations, but their direction. "A star doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. Where to go — you decide yourself."

August saw the mate, a stern man with a weathered face, go up to the captain once and put a hand on his shoulder.

"Go sleep," he said. "I'll stand."

The captain looked at him, nodded, and went below. Two hours later he returned, fresh, as if he had not slept at all. It was always so: he slept little, but it was enough. August had seen this before — Bjorn slept the same way: little, deep, without dreams.

Sometimes August helped with the fishing. When the ship slowed and the water grew calmer, the sailors cast nets and sometimes hauled up a silvery, thrashing mass that glittered in the sun like a scatter of coins. They gutted the fish, salted it, dried it on deck, and the smell of the fresh catch mingled with the smell of tar and salt, creating that special, living scent found only aboard ships. August worked with the others: cleaned fish, hauled nets, helped mend sails. He neither stood out nor fell behind. He simply did his work.

Once they hauled up a net that held, besides fish, a large crab — its shell two hands' width across, its claws able to snap a finger. Hasan, who was pulling in the net, cried out and jerked his hand back. The crab clattered its claws, and the sound was like knuckles rapping on wood. Dariush came over, silently took the crab by the shell, lifted it and dropped it into a barrel of water. Then he looked at Hasan and said:

"You give the sea your hand slowly. Don't jerk it."

Hasan rubbed his fingers and nodded. August smiled with the corners of his mouth. In the North, Bjorn had said something similar: "Give a beast your hand openly. If you tremble, it will bite. If you are sure, it will sniff and go away." Different worlds, the same wisdom.

Sometimes he took out the guitar and played in the evenings, sitting at the stern, when the sun had already gone below the horizon and the sky was darkening, filling with stars. The deck was transformed at such times: the planks cooled, the shadows thickened, and the only light came from oil lamps swaying in the wind, casting trembling, living shadows on the masts and rigging.

The sailors listened in silence. Some sat on the ropes, some lay on deck looking at the sky, some simply stood at the rail looking at the water. Some nodded to the rhythm, some closed their eyes. They did not understand the words, because August sang in the northern tongue, but music needs no translation: it speaks for itself. He played of the northern winds that hum in the rocks, of deserts where sand becomes air, of how water can be both friend and enemy.

The songs were different: some quiet and slow, like lullabies; others sharp and fast, like a wind that falls upon you suddenly and departs, leaving silence behind.

One evening he played a song he had never played before: it was born right there, on deck, from the rhythm of the waves and the creak of wood. At first the melody was even, like the breathing of a sleeper; then it began to rise, gathering strength like a wave that grows without breaking, until at last it poured out in a pure, high note that hung over the water like a gull and slowly came down. When the last note faded, the silence lasted so long that August thought: perhaps they have left. But he turned and saw that everyone was still there. They were simply listening to the silence left behind by the music, and it held more than the music itself.

Dariush came up after that song. He stood a while, then said:

"You have the sea in your voice. Not everyone sings like that."

August looked at him. Dariush did not explain what "the sea in your voice" meant. He simply went away. But August understood: it was not about technique, not about hearing, not about strings. It was about music becoming real when it holds something greater than yourself. In the North he had played of snow, and the northerners understood. Here he played of the sea — and the sailors understood. Music is not translated. It is passed on.

Hasan sat closest of all, listening open-mouthed, stars reflected in his eyes. Once he asked:

"Teach me."

August looked at him.

"To do what?"

"To play."

August was silent a moment. Then he held out the guitar.

"Try. Like this. Fingers — here and here. Don't press, touch. The string must sound, not scream."

Hasan took the guitar awkwardly, as if holding someone else's child. His fingers obeyed poorly; the strings buzzed.

But he did not give up: he tried again and again, grimaced, smiled, tried again. When at last he drew a clean note, his face lit up as if he had caught a star.

August smiled. Not broadly — with the corners of his mouth, the way he had smiled in the North. But Hasan noticed and nodded, as though accepting that smile as praise.

After that Hasan asked for lessons every evening. His fingers were strong from the ropes but unaccustomed to strings: they pressed too hard, and the sound came out harsh, crude. August kept repeating: "Lighter. Don't strike, touch. Imagine the string is skin. You don't press on it — you stroke it." Hasan would grimace, try, and press too hard again. But slowly, day by day, it began to work: the notes grew cleaner, softer, and his fingers found the right place.

One day Hasan played a simple melody — three notes August had shown him: low, middle, high. It sounded clumsy, with pauses and false notes, but it was music. Hasan looked at August, and there were tears in his eyes that he was not ashamed of.

"I've never played," he said. "I never thought I could."

"In the North they say: everyone can make a sound. Not everyone hears themselves," said August.

Hasan nodded and took up the strings again. He played badly, slowly, but with such earnestness that August felt something he had not felt in a long time: a warm, quiet pleasure in having passed something on to another person. Not taught — passed on. The way Bjorn had passed him the knife. The way the shaman had passed him the tattoo. The way Arno had once passed him a wisdom that August did not understand at once but carried within him, the way one carries embers beneath the ash.

When they had almost rounded the reefs and turned northeast to return to the mainland coast, August noticed that the captain began looking at the horizon more often. Not in the direction they were going, but in another — to the south, where sky and sea merged into one line. The sailors spoke more quietly. Hasan, usually talkative, fell silent and became more careful in his movements, as if he sensed something he could not explain.

There hung in the air that special stillness which comes before the inevitable, when the world holds its breath for a moment to draw in air.

August felt it too. Not with his mind — with his body: it grew more tense, his movements more precise, his reactions quicker. It had been like that in the North before a blizzard: first the silence, then the wind, then the snow you do not see but only feel striking your face. Here it was different, yet the same: something was coming, and nature already knew it, even if men did not yet.

Dariush changed too. He began standing longer at the rail, looking at the water more often, and once August saw him touch the amulet at his neck — a small stone one on a leather cord. August had not noticed it before. Perhaps because Dariush had not shown it. Now he touched it, and there was something in that touch August did not like. Not fear — rather, recognition. As if Dariush knew what was approaching and was preparing for it the way only those who have seen it before know how.

One evening, when the sun had already set and the deck lay in semi-darkness, Dariush came up to August. He stood beside him in silence, then said:

"You hold your sword like a man who is used to it."

"I am used to it," said August.

"It may be needed here."

August looked at him. Dariush did not look away. In his eyes was something August had not seen before: not alarm, but the cold, sober readiness of a man who knew that trouble was coming and did not intend to sit waiting for it.

"What did you see?" asked August.

"Sails," said Dariush. "Not ours. Not merchant ones. Small, fast. I haven't seen them in a long time. But I remember."

"Pirates?"

Dariush shrugged. A gesture that here meant: "Maybe. Maybe not. But it's worth preparing."

"When?"

"I don't know. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe in a week. Maybe we'll slip past. But if they come — we have few men and few weapons."

August nodded. Dariush went away. August stood at the rail and looked south, to where sky and sea merged into one black line. Nothing was visible. But now he knew that Dariush was right: one must be ready.

That night he did not sleep. He lay in his hammock and thought. Not of battle — of that he did not think, because it had not yet begun. He thought of the people on the ship. Of Hasan, who had never held a sword and who laughed as if the world could do him no harm. Of Dariush, who touched his amulet and kept silent. Of the captain, who slept little and watched the stars. Of the mate, who had put a hand on the captain's shoulder and said: "Go sleep." All of them were alive, real, and each in his own way was preparing for what might come from the south.

One night, while August stood his watch, he thought he saw fire on the horizon. Not lightning, not a star, but fire itself: warm, flickering, alive. He narrowed his eyes and peered, but there was nothing there. Only stars, only sea, only silence, and the black water reflecting the sky so evenly that the ship seemed to be sailing between two heavens. Deciding it was merely a play of light, he brushed it off. But something inside trembled — whether a premonition, or simply the fatigue that accumulates after weeks at sea and begins to speak in voices that do not exist.

He stood a while longer, listening to himself and to the sea. The wind had not changed. The waves were even. The sails stood as they stood. Nothing foretold anything. But you cannot deceive the body: it knows before the mind does. Bjorn used to say: "If you feel that something is wrong, don't look for an explanation. Simply be ready." August checked his sword, his knife, his pouch of herbs. Everything was in place. He stood a while longer, then went down to the hold and lay down to sleep.

Sleep would not come. He lay in his hammock, listening to the creak of the wood, to the sound of the water beyond the hull, to someone snoring softly in the next hammock. His body was relaxed, but his mind was awake: again and again it returned to that fire that was not there. Or was it? August did not know. In the North he had learned to trust premonitions: the shaman said the body sees what the eyes do not notice, and that this is not magic but simply attention that keeps working even while you sleep. Perhaps this was it — attention that had caught something in the air, in the water, in the wind, and sent a signal the mind had not yet deciphered.

Then he thought of his mother. Not the mother he had found and left in the south, but the one who bore him and raised him in Alba-Mare. Her hands, the smell of bread, the voice that sang him to sleep. He had not thought of her in a long time. In the North there had been no time — there one had to survive, and memories of the past were a burden, like extra weight in the pack. But now, in the darkness of the hold, on a strange ship, between two heavens, she had come of her own accord. Not as longing, but as a presence, as if she were near and silently watching him. August closed his eyes and felt a quiet come over him. Not emptiness — quiet. As if someone had laid a hand on his brow and said: "Sleep. I am with you."

And so he fell asleep: with that feeling, with that question, with that quiet, stubborn premonition that did not frighten but simply stood somewhere at the edge of consciousness, like a shadow behind a door.

And in the morning, they saw the sails.

August came up on deck when the sun had barely touched the horizon. The air was cool, damp, with the smell of salt and of something else — something that had not been there yesterday. Perhaps smoke. Perhaps tar. Perhaps simply the presence of others, which you sense before you see.

The sails were far away, in the south. Dark, of an irregular shape, unlike those August had seen on merchant ships. There were several of them — maybe three, maybe four. And they were not sailing along the coast but straight toward them, as if they knew where they were going. Or whom they were looking for.

The captain stood at the helm, and his face was tense but calm. He said not a word; he only watched. The sailors, one after another, came up on deck and watched too. No one spoke. Only the wind, only the sails, only the silence.

Dariush stood at the rail touching his amulet. Hasan stood beside August, and his face, usually alive and open, had gone quiet, gathered, as if the laughter had been taken out of it and something heavy and hard laid at the bottom.

August stood at the rail and watched those distant dark shapes on the horizon. His hand went of its own accord to the hilt of his sword. Not from fear — from habit. His body already knew what would come next, even if his mind had not yet decided.

Then Hasan said quietly:

"It's them."

August did not ask who "they" were. He knew.
