The night passed quietly. He slept by the embers of the fire, wrapped in his cloak, and woke because someone touched his shoulder gently. In the North the first reflex was to grab for the knife, and his hand had already twitched toward the hilt, but the body outran the thought: there was no threat. The touch was light, almost weightless — the way one touches a shoulder when one fears waking someone too abruptly.

He opened his eyes. Above him stood the old man, the same one who had invited him into the village. His face was already lit by the early sun, and the wrinkles seemed deeper than yesterday, as if the night had plowed furrows across them. But the eyes were clear, alive, without the heaviness of sleep.

“Get up,” he said. “It is morning. There is a conversation to be had.”

He rose, feeling how the body had rested through the night. His muscles did not ache, his joints did not creak — three northern winters had taught the body to sleep anywhere and wake ready. He washed from the bucket left by the house. The water was cold, but not icy as in the North — somehow soft, alive. He splashed his face, rubbed his cheeks, ran a wet palm over the back of his head. The water smelled of earth and grass, not of snow.

He followed the old man into his house. Inside, everything was already tidied: the hearth was lit, the floor swept, and on the low table stood two bowls of porridge and two mugs of soured milk. The porridge was thick, with pieces of dried fruit he did not know, and it smelled so good that his stomach reminded him of itself. The old man silently indicated a place by the hearth. He sat. He ate unhurriedly, feeling the hot food spread through his body, restoring strength. The porridge was unfamiliarly sweet, with a tang of some spice he did not know, and the milk turned out to be sour-salty, with a sharp smell; but after the first swallows he grew used to it. In the North they drank sour milk too — only from goats. Here, apparently, from something else.

The old man sat across from him, drinking something from a clay mug — not milk, but a dark, bitterish drink that gave off a herby steam. He waited. He did not hurry. In the North he had learned to value this skill: to let a man finish eating without interrupting, without asking questions, until the spoon clinked against the bottom of the bowl.

When he finished, he set the bowl aside and looked at the old man. The latter took this as a sign of readiness and began to speak. Slowly, with effort, but his southern dialect was understandable enough — August had heard it in the markets of Vistaria and in the ports of the eastern coast, though the words sometimes tangled, and one had to guess the meaning from intonation.

“You are going east?”

“Yes,” said August.

“Why?”

He was silent. Not because he did not know the answer, but because the answer was too large for a short phrase. How to explain to this old man what he was seeking? Not gold, not glory, not a new life. He simply wanted to see. To understand. To hear how the world breathes in places he had never been.

“I want to see,” he said at last. “Everything I do not know.”

The old man nodded, as if accepting the answer, but something in his gaze said he had expected more. Or something else. He drank from his mug and set it down on the table carefully, as if it were fragile.

“It is far,” he said. “There, beyond the steppe, beyond the forests, beyond the rivers — there are people, cities, seas. And farther still. I have not been there. But I have heard.”

“From whom?”

“From traders. They come here once a year, when the caravans come from the eastern shore. They tell of cities of white stone, of sultans sitting on thrones of gold, of gardens where trees grow fruit sweet as honey.” He paused. “And of deserts where there is nothing. No water, no shade. Only sand and wind.”

August listened without interrupting. The old man spoke of a world that sounded like a fairy tale. But in the North there had been tales too, and they had proved true.

The old man fell silent, looking somewhere toward the hearth, where the fire licked at the dry logs. His face in the light of the flames seemed older, the wrinkles deeper, the eyes yet more attentive.

“I do not know who you are, Au-gust,” he said, and for the first time something serious sounded in his voice — not heavy, but weighty. “But you know how to listen when others are silent. And you know how to speak when the strings sound.”

He waited. He felt the old man had not yet said the main thing. In the North he had learned: the most important always comes last, when all the small things have already been said. Bjorn taught it this way: “First the wind, then the rain, then silence. In the silence — that for which all the rest exists.”

“You are going east,” the old man repeated, as if weighing the words. “But there, where the land begins that knows no seas, there are people who are seeking such as you.”

“Why?” asked August.

“They want their voices to be heard. You are one who knows how to hear.”

The old man drew from somewhere behind him, from under the sleeping bench, a small piece of leather on which a map was roughly cut. The leather was old, cracked, stained with grease and pocked with moth-holes, but the lines were visible: rivers, mountains, roads. He laid it before August and pressed his finger on a spot marked with a cross.

“Here,” he said. “Two days' travel. A large trading city. Ask there about the Dead Hill. A woman lives there who knows the way farther on.”

He looked at the map. A few lines, a few marks, one dot picked out by a rough hand. The lines were uneven but interesting: one ran along a river, another across hills, a third crossed something marked with wavy strokes — perhaps steppe, perhaps wasteland. At the edge of the leather, almost at the cut, was drawn a circle with a dot in the center, and an inscription in a language he did not understand.

“What is this?” he asked, pointing at the circle.

“The sun,” said the old man. “That is how they mark cities where a sultan rules. This one is the nearest. It is called…” — he pronounced a word August could not make out, then repeated it more slowly. “Karsan. A big city. Walls, markets, wells. Caravans come there from all sides.”

“And the Dead Hill?”

The old man paused. As if deciding how much could be told.

“The Dead Hill is a place beyond the city. An old one. There was a settlement there once, but it went away. Only a woman remained. She lives alone. The people fear her. But she knows roads that others do not. If you wish to go farther east — to the sea, to the desert, to the sultans — you need her. She will tell you how to pass.”

“Why is she alone?”

The old man looked at him long.

“Because those who know too much usually remain alone,” he said quietly.

August nodded. Not because he understood, but because he accepted. There had been such people in the North as well: the elder in the mountains, who lived apart from everyone because his knowledge was too heavy for everyday company. The shaman who had made the tattoo lived in a cave and came out only when called. Knowledge is not always a gift. Sometimes it is a burden.

“Why do you help me?” he asked.

The old man shrugged — a gesture that could mean anything at all: “because it is the custom,” “because I like you,” “because it is right.” In the North they shrugged too, but there it meant “I don't know.” Here, it seemed, it meant “it does not matter.”

“You gave pelts for food,” he said. “You are not a thief, not a warrior, not a trader. You are one who walks. People who walk need not be stopped. They need to be helped to walk.”

August looked at him. The old man sat straight, without stooping, and there was something familiar in his bearing — the same silent dignity he had seen in Bjorn when the latter stood by the outermost house and saw him off with his gaze. Different languages, different faces, but the same understanding: a man who walks deserves respect simply because he walks.

He rose and held out his hand. August took it and stood. The old man's hand was dry, hard, with calluses that spoke of labor: not the sword, not the pen, but the spade, the rope, the harness. The hands of a man who had lived his life holding on to the earth.

“I do not know your name,” said August.

“My name is Teir,” the old man answered. “The people here call me the Voice. But I am not a voice. I am one who listens.”

He smiled. Not smiled in return, but nodded — with that calm respect which in the North took the place of a smile when words were superfluous.

“I will remember.”

Teir nodded and drank again from his mug, as if the conversation were over and everything that needed saying had been said. August went out of the house.

He gathered his things. Tied the pack, slung the guitar across his back, checked that Bjorn's knife was in place — a habit one cannot shake off: in the North he had learned to check everything twice. His father's sword at his belt, straps tightened, boots on. The hare pelts were no longer needed — he had given them away yesterday, and it had been right. He did not regret it. He took only what he could carry.

Before leaving he stopped at the threshold and turned around. Teir stood in the doorway, holding the mug with the dark drink, and watched him. In the morning light his face seemed less wrinkled than yesterday — rather weary, but calm. Weary of years, but not of life.

“I will ask in the city,” said August. “About the Dead Hill.”

“Ask,” Teir nodded. “And then go on. Do not linger. Cities draw you in. They give food, shelter, talk. But they do not give the road.”

“I remember.”

“And one more thing,” Teir added, a little quieter. “If you find that woman… do not be surprised if she knows more about you than you know yourself. It happens with those who live in old places.”

August looked at him, but the old man had already turned away and gone into the house. The door closed softly, without a creak.

He left the village as the sun was only beginning to rise above the hills. Trees and stones cast long shadows, and the air was still cool, but not frosty — rather fresh, with a light smell of grass and smoke. Silence hung over the village like a dome: not dead, but alive, filled with the breathing of sleeping people, the rustle of mice, the warm snorting of livestock in the pens.

The road east was open, but no longer as empty as yesterday. The earth here was softer, warmer, and things grew on it that he had not seen in the North: bushes with small red berries whose edibility he did not know; low trees with grey, cracked bark, smelling of something bitter and sweet at once; grass that reached to the knee and left small seeds on his boots. It was easy to walk — he did not sink, did not slip, did not stray. His body was adjusting to the new earth, and the earth, it seemed, was accepting him.

An hour later he heard ahead the creak of wheels. The sound was familiar, yet unfamiliar after weeks of silence. In the North there had been almost no wheels — only runners, sledges, drag-poles. Here it was real wheeled transport, and its creak was measured, calm, like breathing. A cart drawn by an old donkey crawled slowly along the road. The cart was loaded with sacks bound with hemp rope, and on it sat a driver — a slight man wrapped in a warm cloak of coarse wool, his face tanned to a dark brown, with eyes that seemed too lively for his weary body.

The driver gave him a look and called out:

“Do you know the road?” he asked in the same broken southern dialect as Teir, but with a different accent — faster, sharper, as if the words were hurrying to burst out.

“No,” August answered. “I am going east.”

“I'm going to the city,” said the driver, without getting down from the cart. “Karsan. You know it?”

“I've heard of it.”

“If you like, walk alongside. You can tell me where you're from and where you're going. I get bored alone. The donkey doesn't talk, and I don't know how to keep quiet.”

August looked at him. In the North he would not have gone — too easily, too quickly, too much talk. But the North was behind him, and here the rules were different. He had to learn them.

He nodded and walked alongside the cart, fitting his step to the measured, swaying rhythm of the donkey. The driver proved talkative, as promised: he chattered without pause, leaping from topic to topic like a bird from branch to branch. His name was Halef. He was carrying grain and dried herbs to the city, hoping to barter them for copper and cloth. He spoke of Karsan as if describing an entire world:

“The walls are high, of clay and stone. There are guards on the walls, but they're not mean if you don't steal. Inside — markets, heaps of markets. One for grain, another for livestock, a third for cloth, a fourth for everything else: spices, knives, clay things, women, if that interests you. But you, I can see, are not that sort. You…” — he narrowed his eyes, studying August — “you're from far away. From the North, yes?”

“Yes,” said August.

“Far off. I've heard of northerners — big, strong, wild. But you're not wild. You're quiet. Hm. Maybe you're not a real northerner.”

“Real,” said August, without offense.

Halef grunted and went on chattering, not waiting for answers. August listened. Not so much to the words as to the rhythm, the intonations, those pauses where Halef would fall silent for half a second and something would slip through his gaze — not fear, not cunning, but something like curiosity mixed with caution. This man was not as simple as he wished to appear.

The road ran over gentle hills, and with every step the land changed. The grass grew taller and stiffer, the bushes thicker, the trees taller. Sometimes dark shapes showed on the horizon — perhaps mountains, perhaps clouds. The wind blew from the east, and it carried the smell of something new: dust, sun-warmed stone, something bitter and sweet at once, like the scent of spices he had once known in the markets of Vistaria, but stronger, denser.

“The wind,” said Halef, noticing how August drew in the air. “Eastern. From beyond the river. There, beyond the river, the real East begins. The steppe. And beyond the steppe — the desert. And beyond the desert — the sea.”

“Have you been there?”

“No,” Halef shook his head. “I'm fine here as it is. But my grandfather went. He said the desert is like the sea, only without water. And it kills the same way, if you don't respect it.”

August nodded. “Like the sea, only without water.” In the North the sea killed too, if you did not respect it. Signe's husband had gone into the sea and not returned. Everywhere there was its own enemy, and everywhere it was the same one — nature, which forgives no inattention.

He did not know what awaited him in the city. But he knew that now there was a direction. The Dead Hill. A woman who knows the roads. And the east wind, which carried the scents of a world he had never seen.

And that was the beginning of the road to the East.
