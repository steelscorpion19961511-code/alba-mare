In the first days he hardly stopped. He walked without choosing a road, keeping to the coast, where the wind was cool and salty and the sand beneath his feet was firm. He did not think. He did not want to think. Every time his thoughts began to drift back to Alba-Mare, to the smoke, the ashes, his father, he quickened his pace.

Sometimes he stopped in the middle of the road and looked at his hands. They were trembling — a fine, unstoppable tremor. Not from the cold. Not from weariness. From the fact that there was nothing inside him that could hold them still. He clenched them into fists, driving his nails into his palms, and waited for the trembling to pass. Then he opened his hands and walked on.

He walked until his legs began to shake, and only then did he sit down on the rocks, take out the guitar and play. He played quietly, almost soundlessly, so as not to hear the voices in his head. He did not allow himself to remember, did not allow himself to feel. Music was his wall — thin, but the only thing he had left.

On the fourth day he met travelers.

An old trader and his daughter, hauling sacks, wooden crates and several barrels on a cart. They had noticed him from far off — he was walking down the deserted road, hunched under the weight of the pack and the guitar, looking as though he had not slept in days. The trader stopped the cart and called out.

"Hey! Are you going far?" he shouted.

August stopped and raised his head. For a while he said nothing, as if he did not understand the question. Then he answered:

"I'm going where the road goes."

"And what will you do there?"

August looked at his case. He ran his fingers over its fabric, as if recalling what lay inside.

"Play."

The trader smiled, and something warm flickered in his eyes — not pity, but recognition.

"Climb up," he said, nodding at the cart. "The road is long, and it's hard alone."

August wanted to refuse. He was used to being alone. But his legs would no longer hold him — he nearly fell climbing onto the edge of the cart. The trader looked at him but said nothing. He only steadied him by the shoulder so he would not fall.

The travelers were two: the man and his daughter — a girl with dark hair and quiet eyes. She did not say a word, only watched as August settled onto the edge of the cart. But her gaze was warm; there was neither wariness nor pity in it. She simply looked — the way one looks at someone who needs a little time.

The trader was loud and talkative. He told of what he had heard on the roads: an earthquake in one town, a fair in another, and in a third a young duke marrying the daughter of an eastern king. August listened with half an ear. It seemed to him that all towns burn sooner or later. He wanted to say it aloud — but the words stuck in his throat. He opened his mouth, clenched his jaw, and closed it again. He could not. Not yet.

The trader did not pry. Now and then he cast August a brief glance — and looked away. He knew when silence was better.

Toward evening they stopped by a small stream and built a fire. The trader brought out an old flask of wine and offered it to August. August refused. The trader did not insist.

"You're from the west?" he asked, pointing to the horizon where the glow still showed.

"Yes," August answered quietly.

"There was a town there?"

"There was."

The trader shook his head, but asked no more. He knew when silence was better. He simply held out a piece of bread, and August took it without looking.

The dark-haired girl came to him when they were all sitting by the fire, and sat down beside him — not too close, but close enough that he felt her presence. She did not stare at him; she looked into the fire. But August felt her attention. She noticed when he flinched, when he fell silent mid-sentence, when his grip on the guitar grew tighter than it needed to be. She said nothing — she was simply there.

"Do you play?" she asked at last. Her voice was soft, almost gentle.

August nodded.

"I heard you walking and singing," she said. "Before we met. I couldn't make out the words, but I thought it was a song about the sea."

"Yes," said August. "About the sea."

The girl was silent, watching the fire.

"May I listen?" she asked.

August looked at the fire, at her face lit by the flames. He had not played for anyone since the day he left home. For seven years he had not played for anyone. But there was no plea in her voice — there was an offering.

He took the guitar and ran his fingers across the strings. They answered softly, with a slight tremor. When he began to play, he forgot there was a fire, a cart, a world. The sound came from the guitar, but it echoed in his chest — in the place that had been empty for seven years. He did not sing loudly. He hardly sang at all. But in the silence of the night his voice rang clear, as though he were speaking not in words but in what remained inside him — the unspoken, the unhealed.

He played of the sea, of the town that had burned, of the mother he had not found. He did not tell them it was true. He simply sang.

No one applauded. No one said a word. In the silence broken only by the crackle of the fire and the sound of the wind, he finished the last note and lifted his hands from the strings.

The girl sat motionless, gazing into the darkness beyond the fire. There was no delight in her eyes — only something like understanding. Slowly, as if afraid to shatter the silence, she turned to him.

"You know," she said quietly, "you have a voice. Not the one you speak with. The one that makes people stop and listen."

August said nothing. She looked at him, and for a moment her hand touched his hand — lightly, almost weightlessly, as if she wanted to say something words could not express. Her fingers were warm, and in that touch there was more understanding than in any words. Then she withdrew her hand and turned back to the fire.

August watched her and felt something stir inside him. He did not know what the feeling was called. He only knew he would remember it.

In the morning he said goodbye to them.

The trader shook his hand firmly, like a man.

"Where now?" he asked.

"I don't know," August answered honestly.

"Then take this," said the trader, holding out a bundle of food. "You'll need it. The road doesn't forgive the hungry."

August took the bundle. He wanted to say thank you — but his throat closed. He only nodded.

He had already turned to leave when he heard footsteps behind him. The dark-haired girl stood a few paces away. She was watching him with the same quiet smile he had remembered from the fireside.

"Your song isn't over yet," she said. "I know."

August looked at her. He wanted to ask how she knew. But he didn't. He simply nodded and walked on.

He did not look back. But her words stayed with him.

He walked down the dusty road, and for the first time in those days it seemed to him that he was not merely fleeing something. That he was walking toward something. He did not know that this meeting would stay with him for a long time — not as a memory, but as a quiet reminder that the world was not over. That it held people who listen. And that his songs were needed by someone.
