Seven years. For seven years he woke before dawn — when the sky had not yet decided to turn grey, and the silence belonged only to the roosters and his own breathing. Pulling on his chainmail, fastening the straps on his chest, checking that the sword had slid into its scabbard with a familiar motion, he stepped out into the city that already knew him by his gait.

He knew every stone in the square — the one that wobbled by the third column, the one that grew so hot by noon that it burned through the sole of his boot, the one that was always wet from a leaking pipe. He knew when the bakers woke — exactly an hour before first light; when the fishermen returned — by the cry of the gulls over the pier; when foreign ships appeared in the port — by the smell of oil and spices that the wind carried half a day before their arrival.

But he no longer waited for them.

August became a good guard. Flawless. He carried out orders without unnecessary questions — questions in general had grown fewer, as if they had been replaced by commands, and he had grown used to answering only "yes, sir" and "at once, sir." He did not argue with his superiors, did not seek adventure, did not look where he was not asked to look. He was reliable — and this was valued.

Old Gregor, who had served in the guard before August was born, spoke of him briefly: "That one won't let you down. Does what's needed." August heard this once, passing by the barracks, and thought then: "I do what's needed. But I don't know what I want." And another thought, which he did not say aloud because he did not want to admit it even to himself: "I have stopped hearing."

He did not know that this was betrayal. Not of Matteo, not of his father, not even of the guitar. Of that part of himself that had once sat on the stones by the old pier and listened as the city spoke to him.

Routine became his skin.

Morning — formation in the parade ground, when fingers grew numb from cold or sweaty from stuffiness, when the sergeant counted the men with a face as if he already expected someone to desert. Weapons inspection — the blade had to shine, but August sharpened his sword not because he was ordered to, but because it was the only hour of the day when his hands were busy and his head was empty. He loved that sound — even, hissing, the same day after day. It calmed him.

Day — patrolling the streets. He walked the market, kept order at the gates, checked the travel papers of merchants who smelled of sweat, leather and money. He looked into faces and stopped remembering them. They flowed past like water — hundreds, thousands of faces over seven years, and not one that he would want to see again.

Evening — changing of the guard, a dry report of incidents: somewhere dockworkers had quarreled, somewhere a sack of flour had been stolen, somewhere a cart had broken. August wrote down numbers and names, but they did not stay in him. He stopped thinking about them the moment the pen touched the paper.

Night — sometimes quiet, sometimes uneasy. On such nights, standing watch on the city wall, he looked at the sea. Dark, indifferent, alien. It had always been here, but he had stopped noticing it. He looked and did not see. It seemed to him that this was what growing up meant — the ability not to feel what had once ached.

Sometimes he was sent beyond the city.

They escorted trade caravans to neighboring villages — across fields scorched by the sun, through forests where the wind smelled of damp and bark, across rivers that in rain became angry and turbulent. They spent the night by campfires, listening to the drivers — they talked a lot, as if afraid of silence. They told of wars in the southwest, of famine in the east, of wonders that someone had seen and someone had invented.

August listened in silence. Almost without participating. It seemed to him that all these stories were about someone else's life, not his. Sitting apart, he cleaned his blade or simply looked at the fire, not trying to make out anything in it. Just fire. Just warmth. Just night.

Once they were sent over a mountain pass. A grain convoy — heavy, slow, dangerous: the road was narrow, a cliff on one side, an abyss on the other. For three days they climbed, two more they descended, and by the evening of the fourth day everyone was dropping off their feet. August remembered how his shoulders ached, how the chainmail crunched with every step, how the salt of sweat stung his eyes. But he remembered this campaign not for the fatigue.

At the camp, when everyone was silent and even the fire crackled lazily, one of the drivers — stocky, with a grey beard — took out an old lute. Not new, not beautiful, with a cracked body and one broken string that he had never replaced. He ran his fingers over the remaining ones, and a sound poured out — low, deep, ancient.

The driver sang in his own language. The words were foreign, guttural, incomprehensible. But the melody… August raised his head before he could stop himself.

It was that very melody. Or not that one, but something in it — an interval, a breath, a pause between notes — struck right into his chest, there where he had closed the door seven years ago and sealed it with stones. He recognized not the sounds, but the feeling that he had once been able to call "home." And it came not as a memory, but as pain.

His hand, resting on his knee, trembled. His fingers clenched on their own — and unclenched, as if trying to repeat a motion they had not made for seven years. He did not notice this. And when he did notice — he clenched them into a fist so hard that his nails dug into his palm.

August shifted his gaze to the darkness beyond the fire. He forced himself to listen to the night, not the music. He forced himself to forget. He almost succeeded. But the melody remained inside — thin, sharp, like a splinter that cannot be pulled out because it has grown into the flesh.

He said not a word. He did not ask what song it was. He did not ask for another. He simply sat and looked into the fire until the lute fell silent, until the drivers began to settle down to sleep, until the fire burned down to embers. And then he lay down on his cloak, closed his eyes, and all night heard that sound in his head — insistent, quiet, alive.

He was twenty-three when he realized that he no longer remembered the voice of his city.

Standing on the wall, he looked at the white stone houses descending to the water, at the azure waves that touched the sand, at the gulls that hung in the air as if someone had drawn them. And he saw it all — but did not feel it. The beauty was here, but it passed through him like wind through a grate. He had become transparent. He had become a place where nothing lingered.

And this, it seemed to him, was not hard. This had become a habit. This had become a way to live on — not to attach, not to wait, not to hope.

He stopped noticing dawns. He stopped hearing the sea. He stopped seeing faces.

But one day — a month after that campaign, when he had almost forgotten the melody — he was returning to the barracks late in the evening. The wind was strong, and the shutters in the old house by the square banged like wings. August was about to walk past, but suddenly stopped.

In the noise of the wind, in the rattling of old iron, in the creaking of wood — he heard it. That very one. Or not it. But something that responded in him with the same strings, the same stones, the same old, forgotten pain.

He did not go further. He stood in the middle of the empty square, under a lantern swaying in the wind, and listened.

And in that moment he understood what he had not understood for seven years.

He had been wrong. Music had not been waiting for him far away. It was not in the guitar he had locked in the wardrobe. It had been here all this time — in the creak of gates, in footsteps on cobblestones, in the voices of merchants at the market, in the breathing of the sea that he had stopped hearing. It was everywhere. He had simply stopped recognizing it.

August stood for another minute. Then turned and went to the barracks. He lay down on his bunk, looking at the dark ceiling, and ran a hand over his face.

Then he did not know that silence does not leave. That it does not end — it simply waits until a person tires of running from himself. And that one day he would return to the guitar not to remember, but to begin again. He did not know that this evening on the empty square was the first step toward return.
