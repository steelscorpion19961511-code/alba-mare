He stayed in that city three weeks.

The tavern keeper let him sleep in a small room in exchange for evening performances. It was narrow, with a low ceiling and a window looking out onto the inner yard. Its only ornament was an old nail in the wall, where he hung the guitar case. The room smelled of dry wood and old dust that had not been beaten out of it in years. But he liked it there.

He played almost every evening. At first timidly, as he had that first time, but by the third night he felt surer. People did not always listen attentively, but they did not leave. Some tossed coins onto the table, some ordered him a mug of warm ale, some simply watched without looking away. He played of the sea, of the town that had burned, of the mother he sought in every song. He never told them it was true. He simply sang.

Sometimes the keeper brought him a plate of hot stew or bread with cheese. August ate in silence, sitting at the far table, and felt his weariness slowly receding. He did not know how long he could stay, but it seemed he was regaining strength he had never noticed spending — while he was spending it on his service.

One evening, when he was already putting the guitar into its case, the keeper came over with a mug of warm beer and set it on the edge of the table. August looked up.

"This is for you," said the keeper. "Not for the playing. For being quiet."

August did not know what to answer.

"You play as if you were saying goodbye to someone," the keeper went on, gazing somewhere past the stage. "That's good. But one day you'll have to play for those who stay."

He paused, then added:

"Such songs are needed not only by those who leave."

August took the mug but did not drink. He simply held it, looking at the warm light of the oil lamp.

The keeper had already walked away when August said quietly, to himself:

"Perhaps one day."

The door opened, and a man came into the tavern. He was older than August — about fifty, with grey at his temples and long fingers that he held the way musicians do. He carried an old lute, rubbed to a shine.

"May I?" he asked, nodding toward the stage.

The keeper waved his hand.

The old bard sat on the edge of the stage and began to play. His fingers moved lightly, almost weightlessly, and the melody he drew out was quiet and sad. It was nothing like what August played — it was older, deeper. It smelled of far roads and long nights by a fire.

August froze. He could not look away.

When the old man finished, he looked at him with a slight smile.

"You're the newcomer," he said, without any question in it. "Been in this city long?"

"Three weeks," August answered.

"A lot. And a little," said the old bard. "My name is Arno. Just Arno will do."

"August."

Arno nodded and slapped the bench beside him. August sat down, not knowing what to say. But he did not have to — Arno began himself.

They talked until morning.

Arno spoke of the roads he had walked for forty years. Of the cities he had seen, of the people he had met. Of how music changes from land to land, and how it stays the same when there is a soul in it. August listened and could not have enough of it. He felt that this man knew more, but did not boast of it.

Arno, in turn, listened to August — about the sea, about the white walls, about how he had lost his father and did not know whether his mother lived. He did not interrupt. He only nodded, and his eyes were warm and calm.

Somewhere in the middle of the conversation, August said:

"Have you heard of the land in the north? The land of the Iron Cliffs?"

Arno nodded:

"I have. We wanderers always hear of such places. It is not a kingdom, not an empire — there they live in clan communities, without rulers, without an army. The land is meager, the winters are long. They say they come south not out of malice, but when their food runs out. I have never seen them myself, but I have heard from others that they are not warriors by calling — only people who do not want to die of hunger."

"My father died at their hands," said August. "But I cannot hate them. Not entirely."

Arno gave him a long look, then said quietly:

"That is right. Evil is easier to hate when it is simple. But people are rarely simple. Remember that, August."

August wanted to ask him about many things, but the words would not come.

"Play some more," he said, nodding at the lute.

Arno smiled and took it up the way one takes up someone familiar.

By dawn August understood that he had outgrown this city. He was grateful for the three weeks — for the warm room, for the plate of stew, for the silence he heard in the spaces between the notes. But there was no sea here. No smell of salt. No wind to carry him farther.

Arno stood in the doorway with the lute on his shoulder and looked at him with a soft smile. When August said he was leaving, Arno was not surprised.

"I felt it," he said. "Yesterday, when you played, I heard the wind in your guitar. It was calling you."

August looked at him and said nothing. He would have liked to say something important, but did not know how. He simply picked up his pack, threw his cloak over one shoulder, slung the guitar case across the other, and headed for the door.

At the very door he turned. Arno stood leaning against the doorpost, watching him, resting on his lute.

"Play my song in whatever place you stop," he said. "Let it live there a little too."

August smiled, but did not look back. He simply stepped into the morning.

Before leaving, he went to the market. The trader — an older woman with a sharp eye — quickly guessed she had a stranger before her and doubled her prices. August had not yet opened his mouth when a voice sounded over his shoulder:

"Don't buy from her. She'll skin you."

He turned and saw a girl — the one who had watched him that first evening. She was looking at him with a faint mockery. It took August a moment to recognize her. She was younger than he had thought — sixteen or seventeen, with dark hair braided into a plait.

"Do you remember me?" she asked. "I listened to you at the Grey Rooster. And a couple of times after that, when you didn't see."

August smiled with the corners of his mouth.

"I remember."

"They're fleecing you like a wool merchant's dream," she said. "Come, I'll show you where to get supplies."

Lis took his hand — without embarrassment, as if they had known each other for years — and led him through the narrow lanes. Her palm was dry and warm, and August did not pull his hand away. He felt her fingers close around his wrist, and there was something simple and trusting in that touch, as though she were telling him: I know you're a stranger here. But you're not alone.

She brought him to an unremarkable shop in a side lane. Behind the counter sat an old trader with kind, slightly weary eyes. Lis spoke with him briefly, and he, glancing at August, nodded.

"Take it, wanderer," he said, holding out a parcel. "Dried meat, hardtack, dried fruit and bread. You'll pay half what the woman on the square asks."

August wanted to ask why, but Lis got ahead of him:

"He knows I won't bring him people who don't belong. And he values honesty more than an extra coin."

August counted out the payment, thanked the trader, and stepped outside feeling that for the first time in a long while he had not had to argue with anyone.

"Thank you," he said to Lis.

"Are you from Greymoot yourself?" August asked as they left the shop.

Lis nodded:

"My father worked at the sawmill at the foot of the ridge. A big log came loose from its bindings. He lived a few more days, but the wound was too grave. My mother works at the Oak tavern, near the market. Now I help her there — I draw the beer, serve the food, sometimes help the old cook everyone calls Stump. I like the work, but musicians rarely come to the Oak. So in my free time I come to the Grey Rooster — to hear the voices and songs that we don't have in our tavern."

"And your father..." August began.

"He didn't play, if that's what you mean," said Lis. "But he loved to listen. He used to say that in a good song you can hear how people live."

August looked at her. She was young — by the measure of this world already grown. Sixteen, the age he had been when he joined the barracks. But there was something different in her eyes — not weariness, but rather the habit of seeing and remembering.

"Thank you," he said.

Lis smiled at him — sincerely, without a shadow of mockery.

She saw him to the city gates by a shortcut, through quiet lanes where there were almost no people. At the foot of the wall, where grass was already pushing through the stones, August stopped.

"I want to play you a song," he said. "A new one. In gratitude."

They sat on a low stone by the wall. August took out the guitar and checked the strings — they answered softly but clean. He began to play. The melody was light, flowing, almost weightless, like wind passing through fingers. He did not sing loudly — he hummed, quietly, for her.

It was a song of parting, of how people's paths diverge — sometimes forever. But even when the roads part, those we meet remain alive, warm, real in our memory. And the gratitude for a meeting stays with us longer than the meeting itself.

Lis listened without moving. When he finished, she was silent for a long time. Then she said:

"I will remember this song."

August smiled and put the guitar into its case. He rose, settled his pack, and looked at the road running toward the horizon.

"I must go," he said.

She did not hold him back. She simply stood, brushed off her dress, and said:

"If you ever pass this way again — tell me what lies beyond the horizon."

"I will," he answered.

But they both knew he would not return.

He lifted his pack, slung the guitar over his shoulder, and walked out of the city, leaving behind him one more day of his life.
