Twenty-four years old. The birthday was quiet. His mother baked a pie with figs and honey, sprinkled with almonds. His father poured amber wine from sweet grapes and made a toast — short, like everything he said: "To my son. To a reliable man." August smiled, raised his cup and drained it in one gulp.

His mother watched him across the table. In her eyes was the same quiet sadness as in childhood, when he returned from the pier full of sounds that no one heard. She said nothing. But he noticed her gaze. And did not answer it.

He rarely visited his parents' house. After entering service he lived in the barracks among other guards, but never felt part of that shared life. He had his own bunk, his own chest, and the silence he carried inside.

At dinner his mother talked about the neighbors: the baker's daughter had married, the old fisherman's boat had broken again. His father was silent, occasionally inserting short remarks. August listened and nodded. He knew they missed him, but did not say so aloud. And he himself did not know how to speak of such things.

After dinner he went out onto the porch. He stood looking at the evening sky, where the first stars were already beginning to emerge through the blue. The sea was calm, almost motionless, and its surface reflected their faint light. Something pricked in his chest — the same feeling as once, sitting on the stones by the old pier. Not pain, but rather the memory of pain that had once been alive and now had turned into a barely noticeable echo.

August walked down the corridor. With each step the air grew more stale, more old. The room that awaited him stood at the end of the corridor — with the same door, with the same handle he had touched as a boy.

He stopped at the door. On the wooden surface — a thin cobweb, almost weightless. His hand froze for a moment. The silence behind the door seemed alive. It was waiting. Like everything in this house.

Then — a slow breath, and he pressed the handle. The door opened.

The room had not changed. White walls, a pitcher on the windowsill — empty, covered with dust. The bed was made with an old coverlet that his mother had once embroidered. The plank floor with a worn middle — the trace of his feet when he walked back and forth, memorizing chords. Everything was in its place. Only he himself had become a stranger.

The wardrobe. The door opened with a creak he remembered from childhood. Among old, forgotten things — a guitar in a case of coarse burlap, with ties at the end. He had sewn it many years ago, when he thought he would wander. The burlap was rough to the touch, with a couple of patches sewn on by trembling hands.

August reached out. And froze.

His fingers, which had not touched this case for seven years, suddenly remembered its weight, its texture, its smell. He stood, not daring to touch. As if afraid the guitar would disappear. As if afraid it would not forgive him for the long silence.

Then he untied the cord. Slowly. Carefully. And took out the guitar.

The wood was warm — as if it had kept his warmth all these years. Dust remained on his skin when he ran his fingers over the soundboard. A thin grey layer that had settled over seven years of silence. He blew it away, and small particles swirled in the air, illuminated by the moonlight from the window. The guitar was breathing. It was alive.

The strings, when he touched them, responded. Quietly, muffled — but they recognized him. Like an old friend who had not been offended at being forgotten. Simply waited. His fingers lay on the neck on their own — without his knowledge. And August understood: they remembered more than he did. They remembered every note he had once searched for.

He wanted to put the guitar back. Return to the barracks, lie down on his bunk and forget this evening, as he had forgotten all the previous ones. But his fingers had already gripped the neck. And he could not stop them.

The sound was quiet, almost soundless. He was afraid to play loudly — afraid someone would hear. But in the silence of the room, where no one could hear him, he allowed himself simply to touch the strings. Not melodies. Just sounds. Abrupt, awkward, timid. But they were.

He did not notice how tears came to his eyes. Did not notice how he wiped them with the back of his hand. He simply sat on the edge of the bed, clutching the guitar in his hands, and felt something inside him thawing.

Before leaving he looked around the room once more. His gaze fell on an old box peeking out from under the bed. He knew what was inside. Those very manuscripts he had hidden under the mattress as a boy. He opened the lid — the sheets had yellowed, the edges were crumpled, the ink had faded. But the words remained the same. Opening one of the pages at random, he read aloud, quietly, almost soundlessly:

"I waited for you on the shore… Though I saw a storm in the distance there…"

He turned the page. And read the next line — the one he had written without knowing about whom:

"And if I return — will you be waiting for me?"

August closed the sheets and put them back in the box. He had no answer to that question. But he took the manuscripts with him, putting them in a satchel next to the case.

Before leaving he stopped once more on the threshold. Turned around. Looked at the room — empty, quiet, flooded with moonlight. The pitcher on the windowsill, the bed with the embroidered coverlet, the rug by the door. Everything was in its place. Only he himself had changed.

He stepped over the threshold and closed the door. Not all the way — he left a crack. As if he wanted to tell the room: "I will come back."

The guitar hung behind his back, pressed against his shoulder blades — a familiar weight, a warmth he had not felt for seven years. The satchel lay on his shoulder, pulling him down together with the manuscripts he had written as a boy.

He walked through the empty streets of Alba-Mare — past sleeping houses, past the fountain in the square where he had once first heard the guitar. And for the first time in many years it seemed to him that he was walking not away from something, but toward something.

August did not know then that this night would be the beginning. That he would not return to this room, would not return to the barracks, would not return to that life in which there were no sounds. He did not know that at dawn, when he woke in his old bed, he would hear the sea breathing outside the window — and in that breathing he would fancy a melody. But he knew something else. He knew the guitar was with him again. And that he would not hide it anymore.
