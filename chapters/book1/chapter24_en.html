He woke in an unfamiliar room.

Through a crack in the shutters came a dim light — thin as a drawn string, trembling in the wind. All around was quiet, and the silence felt thick, almost tangible: only the crackle of firewood in the stove and an occasional footstep beyond the wall broke it. August lay in bed under several woolen blankets, feeling his body filled with heaviness, his head stuffed with cotton so dense that even his thoughts moved with effort.

Opening his eyes, he stared at the ceiling for a few moments, not understanding where he was. The last thing he remembered was the cold, the cart, Mira's voice: 'You're burning.' Then — a gap, a dark emptiness where scraps of sound and sensation flickered. He tried to rise, but weakness pressed him back into the pillow, as if the bed itself were holding him in place.

It took August a while to realize his body was bare: the blanket touched naked skin, and the cool air slid across his chest and shoulders, leaving a light shiver. Someone had removed his clothes while fever burned him. Memories returned in fragments: someone's firm hands, a damp cloth on his forehead, the tart smell of herbs, a quiet voice coaxing him to drink at least a sip.

At that moment the door opened, and a woman came into the room. She was no longer young, with graying hair gathered in a tight bun and warm, attentive eyes that held neither curiosity nor fuss — only calm focus. In her hands she carried a clay mug from which steam rose, and with it a faint herbal scent in which he could make out mint, thyme, and something else, bitter and spicy.

'Lie still, lie still,' she said gently, but there was firmness in her voice that brooked no argument. 'You're not getting up yet.'

August wanted to ask where he was, but his voice would not obey: the words scattered somewhere in his throat, never taking shape. He could only look at her, feeling her sit down on the edge of the bed and hold out the mug. He tried to prop himself up on his elbows to take it — and at that moment the blanket slipped from his shoulders and fell to the floor, baring his body. He froze for an instant, feeling the cool air touch his skin, exposing not only his body but his vulnerability. He wanted to pull the blanket back up, but he had no strength.

Elsa looked at him — not with embarrassment, not looking away, but with the same warm, attentive care with which she had studied his face. Her eyes moved over his chest, his shoulders, his arms — and she nodded quietly, as if appraising something familiar to her: the traces of long roads, scars from the cold, the tension of muscles accustomed to a guitar.

'You are strong,' she said. 'I can see that. Even in illness it shows. You will recover.'

She picked the blanket up off the floor and covered him again — slowly, gently, without disturbing his rest. Her hands lingered a moment on his shoulder, feeling the warmth of his skin, and August felt that touch warm him from within, like a quiet fire kindled in a hearth gone cold. He gave her a grateful look and took a swallow. The brew was bitter but warm, and he felt the heat spreading through his chest, pushing through the numbness.

'Drink,' said Elsa. 'It's herbs. It will warm you and calm you.'

She sat beside him in silence, watching him with that quiet care that needs no words. Her hands were rough, covered in small scars, calloused on the palms — the hands of someone who knows what work and pain are and knows how to deal with them. August noticed this as she straightened the blanket, and felt that these hands inspired trust: in them there was no hurry, only assurance and the habit of doing everything precisely.

He took another swallow, feeling the warmth seep into the farthest corners of his body, and asked quietly:

'Where am I?'

'You're in the village of Old Bridge,' she answered. 'Your companions brought you here three days ago. You were burning with fever, raving. The fever has broken now, but your strength will not return all at once.'

August closed his eyes. Fragments of memory flickered before his inner sight: the cold sea, wind lashing his face, the creak of cartwheels and Mira's voice, calm and firm: 'We will stay here.'

'They left?' he asked quietly.

'They left,' Elsa answered. 'But they left you supplies and a little money. They said if you recover — catch up with them. Or go your own way, as you wish.'

August said nothing. Inside him churned a strange mixture of gratitude and a light bitterness: they had been near him, but they had their own roads, and he could not blame them for it. There was no betrayal in what they had done — only the understanding that each must go where the path calls.

'What is your name?' he asked.

'My name is Elsa,' she answered. 'I am the healer here. And if you want to know, you are not the first to fall to the cold on this road. The North does not forgive those who do not know it.'

She helped him sit up, propping pillows behind his back. August looked around the room: small and clean, with whitewashed walls, a wooden table, and old bundles of herbs hanging from the ceiling. In the corner stood his guitar — they had carried it in and set it against the wall, as if it too were waiting for the moment he could touch it again.

He turned his head and looked at it. It stood in the corner, quiet and calm, its lacquered wood dimly gleaming in the half-dark. He tried to rise to reach it, but weakness pressed him back into the pillow once more. He could not reach it. But he knew: it was waiting.

'I don't know how to thank you,' he said.

'You already have,' Elsa answered. 'You survived. That is the best thanks for one who heals.'

She rose, straightened the blanket, and moved toward the door. At the threshold she paused and, turning her head slightly, asked:

'Are you going north?'

'Yes,' August answered.

'Why?'

'I don't know exactly,' he said. 'But I know that I must.'

She nodded, as if this were an answer she had heard many times, one that required neither explanation nor argument.

'Rest,' she said. 'In a couple of days you will be able to get up. And meanwhile — listen to the wind outside the window. It will tell a great deal to those who know how to hear.'

August was left alone. He looked at the guitar, feeling its silence fill the room until it was almost tangible. He wanted to take it in his hands, feel its familiar weight, run his fingers over the strings, hear the first sound. But he knew that now he simply needed to lie still and wait for his strength to return.

He closed his eyes and listened to the wind rushing against the walls. A foreign wind. Cold. But there was no more fear in it. August realized this with surprise: the fear that had haunted him since the day he fled Alba-Mare had not vanished entirely, but it had drawn back, hidden in a far corner of memory, giving way to something new — a calm readiness to meet whatever lay ahead. And he fell asleep with that feeling, for the first time in a long while not trying to run from his dreams.
