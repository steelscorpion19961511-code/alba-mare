He ran, not feeling his legs.

The air burned his throat, his lungs stung with every breath, but his stride never broke. The trail he had walked that morning now seemed endless. Leaping over stones, stumbling off the dry grass, clutching at branches to keep from falling, he raced ahead. The guitar case beat against his back, the pack tore at his shoulder, but the straps only bit deeper, and he kept running.

When the road opened up, the city came into view. And what August saw stopped him for a moment.

Alba-Mare was burning. Not one house — the whole city. Great tongues of flame rose above the walls, black smoke veiled the sky and swallowed the sunset. Firelight danced on the water, and the sea, always azure, now reflected nothing but red.

August ran on. He burst into the city through the western gates — torn off their hinges. One leaf lay on the ground, charred; the other hung from a single hinge, swaying in the wind. Wreckage lay everywhere — wood, stones, shards of clay pots. Without stopping, he ran down the street toward his parents' house.

His parents' house was not completely destroyed, but it was empty. The door torn off, the windows smashed, ashes in the rooms. He did not go in. He did not want to go in. He looked at what was left of his childhood and could not move.

"Mama!" he cried. "Father!"

Silence. Only the crackle of fire and the howl of the wind.

He ran on. He looked for people — anyone who could tell him what had happened. But the streets were empty. Only shadows. Only ashes.

August stopped in the square. The stone platform where the stranger had once sat with his guitar was scorched and black. The fountain where he had loved to sit as a child was shattered, and water seeped from the crack like a tear. Standing in the middle of the square, he looked down at his hands — in dust, in soot, in scratches. He raised his head to the sky — it was scarlet as blood.

And in that moment he heard a voice. Faint, almost soundless.

He turned and saw a man against the wall — a city guard, gravely wounded. Dropping to his knees, August lifted the man's head and looked into his eyes.

"Who?" he asked, and his voice broke. "Who did this?"

The guard opened his eyes. Young — younger even than August. Once he had stood watch with him at the western gates.

"They came from the north," the man whispered. "Many. Armed. We didn't understand what they wanted. They didn't speak our language. They simply walked toward the gates; we tried to stop them — and they began to kill."

"How many were there?"

The guard shook his head, struggling to remember:

"More than us. First they rained burning arrows on the city — from the hills, straight onto the rooftops. The houses caught fire in an instant. We didn't even have time to understand what was happening. And then they burst through the gates while we were fighting the flames. They took captive everyone they could seize. The rest they cut down."

August clenched his teeth:

"Where is my mother? My father?"

The guard closed his eyes for a moment, as if gathering strength.

"Elian held the eastern gates," he said. "He understood they would not stop, and he ordered us to fall back. I don't know what became of him. I never saw your mother from the very start. But I heard they led several women away. I don't know whether she was among them."

August stood up. No fear, no pain, no weariness — only emptiness. He walked toward the eastern gates.

He found his father at the very edge of the city. He lay face down, in his old chain mail, gripping the shaft of a spear. The spear was broken — it had split at the head; only the stub remained in his hands. Bodies of the northerners lay nearby. There were many of them. August understood: his father had held out to the last, fighting while the others retreated.

Dropping to his knees, he turned his father over. Eyes closed, face at peace. At the belt hung a scabbard of rough leather with a worn copper fitting. The sword was whole — his father had not even drawn it; the spear had been the better weapon against overwhelming numbers.

August unclasped the scabbard. He drew the blade — straight, a hand-span longer than his own, with a narrow matte edge. A plain hilt, the leather of the grip worn smooth by years of service. He had seen this sword in his father's hands a thousand times — on patrol, at the gates, when his father taught him to hold a weapon.

He slid the sword back into its sheath and buckled it to his belt. The blade settled against his hip, heavy and familiar.

He knelt beside his father for a long time. Then he understood: he could not leave him here, among strangers. He lifted the body into his arms and walked to the shore. It was hard, but he felt no weight. He walked until he reached the place where the sea was clean and the sand was white.

He remembered how people were buried in Alba-Mare. No graves, no monuments — and no long speeches. They were burned on the shore, so the wind could carry the ashes over the water, so the soul could depart with the waves. August took off his cloak, wrapped his father's body in it. He stacked dry branches and planks and set them alight. The fire leapt up — warm and quiet.

The flames lit his face as he knelt and watched. Sparks rose into the darkening sky; the sea breathed softly at his feet, keeping the silence. He knew no words, no prayers. He simply stood and watched the fire.

The pyre burned long. August sat on the sand, looking into the flames, and did not know how much time had passed — an hour, three, the whole night. He did not count. He simply watched as the fire consumed his father's body, as the sparks climbed into the dark sky.

The wind died down. The moon rose high, and its light trembled on the surface of the water.

The pyre was burning itself out. When only ashes remained of the body, August stood up. He stood on the shore, gazing into the distance, and felt the weight that had dragged at him all his life finally lift.

Now he was no longer a guard in armor — he was a wanderer, carrying only what could be carried: a guitar in its case, a pack of manuscripts, his father's sword at his belt. And ashes on the wind.

He turned to the sea and took a step — the sand beneath his feet was damp and cold.

A second. A third.

He did not know where he was going. He did not know whether his mother lived. He knew only that there was no road back.
