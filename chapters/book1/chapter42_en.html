The winter stretched long — so long that time seemed to lose its usual shape and became not a succession of days but a single dense current in which every morning resembled the one before yet repeated itself not for an instant. Frost bound the earth, but life in the settlement did not freeze — it only learned to move differently, more quietly, husbanding its strength.

August stayed in the settlement. He had not planned it, but the days gathered into weeks, the weeks into months, and at some point he stopped counting them. He helped Bjorn hunt, went out onto the ice with the fishermen, learned to set nets and mend boats. At first his movements were clumsy: he fumbled the knots, did not understand what this or that tool was for, and even a simple knife seemed foreign to him. But Bjorn did not hurry, did not scold — he simply showed it again and again, until August's fingers began to remember the necessary movements before his head could catch up with them.

His body grew used to the cold — not the way one grows used to something unpleasant, but rather it learned to come to terms with it. He knew at what moment the frost began to worm under his clothing, and he tightened the straps in advance, rolled up his sleeves, checked whether the laces had frozen over. His hands learned to do what had once seemed impossible: tie strong knots, true harpoons, whittle thin pegs for traps. He no longer stumbled in deep snow, no longer feared the bright sun reflected from the whiteness — now he knew how to shield his eyes and how to walk so as not to sink into the drifts.

He learned the language, memorizing the words people spoke by the fire. At first they were simple phrases — “thank you,” “give me water,” “which way.” Then he began to understand their talk, to catch intonations, to distinguish when weariness sounded in a voice and when pride in a successful hunt. After a few months he could already say several sentences in the northern tongue without stumbling, and once, without noticing it himself, he answered a question without even translating the words into his native language in his head. People no longer looked at him with suspicion — now they looked with curiosity and approval, at someone trying to become part of their world and doing it not for gain but because he could no longer do otherwise.

By the end of winter he already knew how to read tracks in the snow, telling animal from human. From a barely visible dent he could determine whether a wolf had passed an hour ago or a day, and from the direction of the track guess where it was going. He knew when a blizzard would begin by the way the wind changed direction, turning sharp and prickly, as if testing the air for strength. He could light a fire even in damp weather, when the wood seemed frozen through and refused to catch: he knew which splinters to take first, how to lay them so that the heat gathered within instead of escaping into the frosty air.

He became almost unrecognizable — not only outwardly but within. His face was weathered and tanned by frost, his skin grew coarser, his movements more economical, without needless fuss. He ceased to be the boy who had fled Alba-Mare, driven by fear and resentment. Now he was a man who could look the cold in the eye without looking away, because the cold was no longer an enemy — it had become a teacher.

But the most important thing happened on one of the nights when he went out of the house to check whether snowfall had begun. The frost was fierce, almost ringing, but the sky was clean and transparent, and the stars burned in it so bright that they seemed to have drawn near the earth to examine it more closely. And then the lights began.

Green and blue rays streamed across the sky, shimmering like cloth that the wind stirs above one's head. They moved and pulsed, now gathering into dense currents, now scattering into fine threads, as if the sky itself were learning to breathe. They were alive, breathing, and August stood looking at them, feeling something spread through him that he could not name. It was not happiness and not peace — it was the sense of a connection, deep and ancient, that had existed long before him and would exist after.

He stretched out his hand toward the sky, as if trying to touch that light, and for a moment it seemed to him that he truly felt it — not as warmth but as a vibration passing through his bones, through his blood, through his memory. It was not simply a phenomenon of nature — it was something no words could explain. In that light he heard the voice of Alba-Mare, the voice of his father, the voice of the mother he had left in the south. But he felt no pain. He felt connection: as if all those voices were not calling him back but blessing him to go forward.

August stood there until the lights began to fade, slowly dissolving into the darkness like a dream one remembers only by its feeling. And when the last green gleams vanished, leaving only the stars behind, he understood that his journey was approaching its end — not because he had nowhere further to go, but because he had finally found what he was seeking. He had not been seeking answers — he had been seeking understanding. And now he knew that he carried it within himself, and that he needed to go nowhere else to find himself.
