August woke before dawn — from the habit developed over years of service. His body rose from the bunk on its own, without waiting for a command. The house was quiet. His father was still asleep, his mother must have been busy in the kitchen, but no sounds could be heard. Lying on the old bed and looking at the ceiling, he felt a strange excitement rising inside him. Where had it come from? Perhaps from the fact that the guitar lay beside him, pressed against the wall. Perhaps from the fact that for the first time in many years he did not know what he would do today.

He got up, dressed quietly so as not to wake anyone. Packed his satchel. Inside he put a leather waterskin, bread baked by his mother, a handful of dried apples, a piece of cheese wrapped in cloth, and a flint — just in case. He put the manuscripts on top so they would not be crushed on the road. He hung the guitar in its case on his shoulder.

At the threshold he stopped. His gaze fell on his father's old cloak hanging on a nail by the door. Dark blue, of coarse wool, with worn elbows and a faded buckle at the collar. August took it down and threw it over his shoulders. The fabric was heavy but warm — it smelled of wood, smoke and something else he could not name. Perhaps simply of home. He did not know why he took it. Perhaps to feel that his father was still near. Or perhaps to keep for himself a part of the life he was leaving.

He left the house when the sky was just beginning to brighten. The city was still asleep. The empty streets returned his steps dully from the stone walls. The square where he had once first heard the guitar greeted him with silence — only pigeons were pecking at something by the fountain. The port was empty: the fishermen had not yet gone out to sea, and the boats rocked on the calm water. The road led to the western gates.

Beyond them the journey began. The path wound among low hills overgrown with tough grass and sparse bushes. After an hour the city disappeared around the bend — and around him remained only silence, not the kind he was used to, but the kind that is heard only beyond human dwellings.

August had almost disappeared behind the crest of the hill when he stopped and turned around.

Alba-Mare lay below — white walls reflecting the first rays of the sun, the azure sea that had not yet fully awakened, a light smoke above the roofs. The city that had raised him. The city he was leaving. He stood like that for a minute, examining the familiar landscape, noting every detail: the Tower of the Council, the pier where he had once worked, the old pier where he had first heard the voice of the city. He tried to remember everything, though he did not know why. Simply his chest suddenly grew tight — as happens when you feel that there will be no return.

Then he turned and walked on.

He walked unhurriedly, listening to his steps, to the wind, to the breathing of the earth. The path led ever higher, and with each step a new view opened: now a cliff where the sea broke against the rocks, now a valley flooded with morning light. It seemed that the boundary between sea and sky was disappearing, and the wind sang in a language inaccessible to words. The road led to where the air became more transparent and filled with anticipation.

He knew this place.

The cove hid behind a low cliff. It could not be seen from the road — only a narrow path winding between stones and bushes led down. He had found it by accident several years ago when he lost his way while escorting a caravan. He had not returned here since. But he remembered.

The path narrowed, branches whipped at his face. With each step the air grew more humid, the salty smell mingled with the smell of wet stones and seaweed. Somewhere below the surf could already be heard — still distant, but clearer with every moment.

August slowed his pace. He was in no hurry. As if he felt the silence penetrating inside, filling him with a lightness forgotten over the years of service. Soon turquoise water appeared in the gaps between the rocks. The waves touched the sand. He descended to a small ledge and stopped at the very edge of the cliff to breathe this air, to feel that he was here.

The cove was round as a bowl, surrounded by rocks overgrown with moss. The sand — golden, almost white. The water — transparent to the very bottom, and at the depth one could make out rounded stones and seaweed swaying in time with the current. The waves rolled in slowly, lazily, their whisper was the only sound. Not a soul.

He dropped the satchel, took off the guitar and sat on the sand by the very water. He looked at the horizon line where the sea merged with the sky, and could not remember when he had last felt so calm.

Untying the cord, opening the case, he took out the guitar. The strings had loosened — he began to tune them slowly, testing each sound. He was in no hurry. He listened to how the strings responded, and remembered how he had once found the right note with his eyes closed. His fingers moved timidly, but quickly remembered what the years had not forgotten.

He closed his eyes. And suddenly understood that he was not searching for a melody — he was simply letting it be. As at eight years old. When there was nothing to prove. When one could simply sit on a stone by the old pier and listen as the city spoke to him. His body remembered this sensation before his mind did. His fingers lay on the strings on their own, and they responded — not with words, but with what was deeper than words.

He played a few chords. Simple, almost clumsy. But they sounded. And he smiled — for the first time in many years.

He played all day. He recalled melodies invented by the boy, songs heard in the square, and his own, written on sheets of paper. He did not think about time, about service, about orders. He simply played.

When the sun rose high, he put the guitar aside, lay down on the sand and closed his eyes. Waves, warmth on his face, a strange lightness in his chest. He did not think about his mother. He did not allow himself to. Every time her face surfaced in his memory — hands kneading dough, the look when he returned from the pier, the voice that said "don't lose this" — he drove it away. As he knew how. But in the silence of the cove, between the waves, it seemed to him that he heard this voice again. Opening his eyes, he looked at the horizon. And smiled.

He thought about how life was arranged. All this time he had not been himself. He had been the one others wanted to see: a reliable guard, an obedient son, a proper man. He did what was needed, not what he wanted. He lived someone else's life.

Opening his eyes, he looked at the sky, at the sparse clouds. Perhaps it was not too late to change everything. Perhaps he could still become the one he wanted to be. Perhaps he still had time.

He did not know that almost no time remained.

August dozed off on the warm sand. He dreamed of the sea — as azure as in childhood. And the voice of the old merchant: "Learn to play, boy. Not for me, for yourself." He wanted to answer, but could not find the words.

He woke from the cold. The sun was already leaning toward sunset. The air had grown colder — and something inside contracted, as before a storm, when the air becomes too dense, and the silence — too deep. Sitting up, he ran a hand over his face, brushing off the remnants of sleep.

He could not explain what exactly was wrong. Just a feeling — sticky, anxious — rose from his chest to his throat.

August looked at the water, raised his eyes higher — and saw above the hills, in the direction where Alba-Mare should be, thick black smoke. It rose into the sky in a wide column, lit from below with orange, and the wind carried it toward the sea, covering the sunset.

He jumped up, not believing his eyes. Orange reflections were mirrored in the sky. This was not a campfire. This was a fire. A big one. Destructive.

Thrusting the guitar into its case, grabbing the satchel, he ran. Path, roots, stones — he did not choose the road. His father's cloak flapped behind him, the case beat against his shoulder blades, but he kept running. He ran faster than ever, almost falling, but not stopping. He did not feel fatigue. Only the pulse in his ears and the heaviness in his chest.

He ran because he knew: his home was there. His city. And he had to make it in time.
