The guitar became his shadow. He took it with him to the shore, to the field, to the roof of the old warehouse by the port, where no one disturbed him. He did not know notes, nor chords — he simply searched. His fingers found sounds that resonated in his chest, and he memorized them, inventing his own names: "wave", "dawn", "loneliness". August did not speak of them aloud — this was his, secret.

He played everywhere. On the deserted shore, where the wind drowned out false notes. In the abandoned garden outside the city, where no one went. On the roof of the old warehouse, from which the whole port was visible. Once, when he was sitting on the stones by the pier and plucking the strings, an old fisherman mending a net a few steps away raised his head. August froze, ready to apologize for disturbing him. But the fisherman said nothing. He only nodded — briefly, barely noticeably. And returned to his work. August remembered that nod for the rest of his life. No one had ever listened to him like that — without words, without judgment. Simply accepting.

During the first year he learned to play simple melodies that he heard in his head. In the second year he began to match sounds to words — first to others', then to his own. He wrote them down on scraps of paper that he hid under the mattress. By the end of the third year he had a whole stack of sheets. In it were songs that no one had heard.

But time passed. And Matteo's ship did not return.

Every morning August woke up and looked out the window. Not at the sea — at the horizon. He searched for white sails the color of ivory. At first — with hope, almost with pain. Then — with habit, when his heart no longer skipped at the sight of any ship. Then — with emptiness, when he stopped noticing that he was looking. He often went out to the pier, stood by the very water and peered into the line where the sky met the sea. But there were no white sails. And after a year he simply stopped waiting. Not because he forgot. But because he understood: there was nothing more to wait for.

He was sixteen when his parents spoke of the future. His father called him into the study after dinner. The conversation was short, without unnecessary words.

— You are no longer a boy, — he said. — It is time to think about the trade. You will go into the city guard. I have made arrangements.

August was silent. He knew this day would come. But he did not know how hard it would be to stay silent.

— And the guitar? — he asked quietly.

His father looked at him, and in his gaze there was no anger. Only weariness.

— Music is good, August. It is even beautiful. But it is not life. Life is when you have a roof over your head, work and confidence in tomorrow. You will not play on the street when you have a family. You will work. Like me. Like your grandfather.

August wanted to say something. He wanted to say that music is life. But he could not find the words.

His mother stood in the doorway, listening, not interfering. She too knew this day would come. She had never forbidden him to play, but she had not protected him from his father's decisions either. Perhaps she was waiting for him to say something important himself. But he was silent.

That night August did not sleep. Sitting on the edge of the bed, he looked at the guitar. His mother came into the room, sat down beside him and, without saying a word, laid her head on his shoulder. He felt her breathing, her warmth — calm and even. She did not ask him to stay. She did not tell him what he should do. She was simply there — for the last time, when he could still be a boy, when his father did not yet know what decision he would make tomorrow. August closed his eyes and felt her fingers squeeze his hand.

In the morning he took the burlap case, carefully laid the guitar inside and put it away in the wardrobe, behind old things that no one wore. He said not a word. He did not cry. He simply closed the door and left the room.

That same day he was sent to the barracks — thus began his service, thus began his new life. In Alba-Mare, sixteen was considered the age of majority, and he became a guard, as his father wanted.

For seven years he did not touch the guitar.

He learned to wear armor, to stand on watch, to carry out orders. He learned to look at the sea without thinking that it had once sung to him. He became what they wanted him to be — reliable, calm, without unnecessary thoughts.

But sometimes, at night, August woke up because he dreamed of strings. Sitting up in bed, he listened to the silence. It seemed to him that he heard it — distant, muffled, but still alive.

Then he did not know that silence does not leave. That it does not end — it simply waits until a person tires of running from himself. He did not know that the guitar in the wardrobe, covered with dust, keeps every note he had once found. And that one day he would return to it — not to remember, but to begin again.
