He had been walking north for four days when the land finally changed. At first the snow lay only on the shaded slopes of the hills, as though nature were trying the colour on and hesitating to cover everything with it. But with every hour there was more of it: it settled on the path, filled the hollows, rested on the branches of low-growing trees, turning them into fragile, whitened sculptures. August walked on, and behind him stretched a line of footprints that the wind slowly erased, wiping away his path as if to say that here a road exists only in the very moment you walk it.

He spent the night in a gorge, beneath an overhanging rock that sheltered him from the wind. He lit a small fire with the flint he had bought in Old Bridge from one of the traders. Heimrir, the old man from the tavern, had told him always to carry flint and a small kettle with him: “In the north, fire is your closest friend. Without it you will not warm yourself, not cook, not survive.” August had nodded then, without fully understanding how true those words would prove. Now he felt their weight.

He set the kettle on the fire, filling it with snow. When the snow melted and the water boiled, he threw in a pinch of the herbs Elsa had given him. Steam rose above the kettle, and a bitter but soothing scent spread through the air — warm, spiced, with a faint note of dried mint and something elusive that reminded him of her home. He drank in small sips, feeling the warmth spread through his body, warming him from within, driving out the chill that had settled in his bones since the day he left Old Bridge. Beside the fire lay pieces of dried fish and meat; he warmed them over the flames until they grew soft and edible. The food was simple but filling, and August felt his strength return, drop by drop, sip by sip.

In the morning he woke to the cold and to a silence that wrapped the world beyond his shelter. This silence was not empty but dense, saturated, as though the earth itself were holding its breath. He crawled out from beneath the rock and froze.

Snow was falling in large, soft flakes. It came down thickly, soundlessly, and the world around became a different one — soft, white, almost weightless. August stood with his face raised to the falling snow, feeling his breath turn to steam, his cheeks chill under the touch of snowflakes, his lashes seal together with tiny frozen drops.

He caught snowflakes on his palm and watched them melt, leaving only small drops of water — like little secrets that managed to whisper something to him before vanishing.

He had never seen snow like this. In Alba-Mare there had been rain, there had been sea mist that settled on stones and sails, at times fog — but snow falling like this, covering the ground in a fluffy blanket, turning everything white and quiet as a frozen lake, until even the wind seemed to lose its voice — that he had never known. It was as if he had seen a colour that had never existed before, or heard a note that had been missing from his songs.

He stood so long that a layer of snow gathered on his shoulders and hood, and he felt something strange spreading inside him — not fear, not anxiety, but wonder before something new he did not yet know. In this world where everything was white and cold, there was something majestic, almost solemn, as in an ancient temple where words are unnecessary. The snow did not simply cover the earth — it changed its voice, its smell, its rhythm. And August listened to this new rhythm, trying to memorise it, so that later, if he had to, he could play it on the guitar.

August gathered his things: lifted the kettle from the stone, covered the embers with earth so that not a single spark, not a single threat to the forest would remain, slung his pack over his shoulders and moved on. The snow crunched underfoot; walking grew harder, each step demanding a little more effort, but he felt his body adjusting to the new rhythm, his muscles learning this weight, this slow, sure tread across the white land.

He walked through a forest where the trees stood bare and black, and the snow lay on their branches in white caps, as though someone had dressed them for a festival no one would ever see. At times it seemed to him that the forest was watching him, that these silent trees knew more about him than he did himself. But they kept their knowledge in silence, and August was grateful to them for that.

Travellers crossed his path now and then; some were heading south, hurrying away from the approaching winter. Their faces were tired, their eyes watchful; they looked at August with curiosity, noticing his travel-worn clothes, the guitar, the resolve in his bearing. But no one asked questions, and he offered no conversation. Here, among snow and wind, words seemed superfluous, and silence more honest.

Towards the end of the fifth day the road began to climb, and the air grew thinner, clearer, as though with every step he was rising not only above the land but above the familiar world. The mountains on the horizon drew closer, their dark summits visible against the low sky, sharp as blades, and August understood that he was approaching the Land of the Iron Cliffs.

He stopped at the pass, looking out at the valley beyond which an unknown land began. The wind whistled around him, carrying cold and the smell of a distant sea — salty, sharp, almost wild. The smell was like the voice of the northern land itself, saying: “You have come. Now go on.”

August adjusted his cloak on his shoulders, checked that the sword was fastened securely, touched with his fingers the little pouch of herbs hidden against his chest, and stepped forward, into this snowy land. He did not know what awaited him, but for the first time in a long while he felt ready to meet it. The snow creaked beneath his feet, and every sound was a promise: he was walking, he was alive, and the road went on.
