He walked two more days before he saw the first houses. The village clung to the slope of a hill, sheltered from the wind by a tall crag, as though it had sought out that shelter of its own accord. The houses were low, built of dark stone, with flat roofs bearing a thick layer of snow — it sat there like a heavy, motionless cap. Smoke curled from the chimneys, mingling with the grey sky and rising unhurriedly, as though it knew that haste was not the custom here. White emptiness stretched all around, and only by a few houses could fenced pens for livestock be seen — their walls dusted with snow, and inside them the dark shapes of animals moved quietly, seeking warmth.

As August drew closer, he noticed that he was being watched. People looked out from behind their doors without stepping outside, studying him with curiosity but without hostility — rather with that careful attentiveness with which one observes an unfamiliar beast: is it dangerous, is it hungry, does it bring trouble? He felt no threat in their gazes — only a guarded expectation: now he would take a step, say a word, and by that step, by that word, they would understand who he was.

He stopped before a large house with a signboard on which a ship was crudely painted — its outline simple, almost naive, yet carrying an obstinate will to stay afloat through any storm. Inside it was warm and almost dark: light filtered through a small window under the ceiling, falling in a slanted rectangle on the floor and at once dissolving into the half-dark. It smelled of rancid butter, smoke and old wool — the smell of a dwelling that had survived more than one winter.

The innkeeper, seated behind the counter that divided the guest half of the tavern from the kitchen, was wiping a wooden mug. His movements were unhurried, deliberate, as though he were not simply cleaning the wood but returning to it its memory of warmth. He raised his eyes, took August in from head to foot, lingered on the pack over his shoulder, on the guitar, on his tired but unbroken eyes — and only then nodded.

“A room for the night?” asked August.

The innkeeper gave him an attentive look. His eyes were light and sharp, the eyes of a man used to noticing details: not only what is visible but what hides behind a gaze, behind trembling fingers, behind an effort to stand straight. He looked over his clothes, his guitar, his hands — and only after that nodded.

“There is. A room upstairs. You pay for the night and for the food.”

August counted out the coins — they clinked dully against the table, an everyday sound, and in it there was something soothing: a simple transaction, rules that could be understood. He climbed to the second floor. The room proved small, almost bare, with a narrow bed and a little window looking out on the snowy yard. The snow lay in an even, untouched layer, and only a thin line of footprints left by August stretched from the road to the door — the only living line in this frozen landscape.

He set the guitar in a corner, took off his cloak and sat on the edge of the bed, looking at his hands. His fingers trembled slightly from the cold, but otherwise he felt steadier than before: not because the fear was gone, but because now he knew what to do with it. He looked at his palms, at the lines, the scars, the calluses, and thought that these hands could still play, could still hold a staff, could still warm someone with their heat.

When darkness fell, he went downstairs. The main room was almost empty — just a few people along the far wall, talking quietly, not raising their voices, as though afraid to frighten away the warmth. August ordered hot soup and bread and sat in a corner where the shadow was thickest and where he could watch without drawing attention.

The soup was simple: thick and hearty, with pieces of meat and root vegetables, seasoned with dried herbs. The bread was dark, dense, with a crisp crust. August ate slowly, feeling the warmth spread through his body, his strength returning, each spoonful not merely food but a small act of returning to life.

As he was finishing, a short man in a warm jacket came up to him. He took off his cap, and August saw his face — plain, with soft features but a firm line of jaw that betrayed a habit of making decisions.

“You are from the south?” he asked.

“Yes,” August answered.

“So am I,” said the man. “Six years here, in this village. I trade. This village is called Holmstad. The locals are used to strangers. But you, I take it, are not simply a trader?”

August shook his head.

“I am looking for the way. I want to understand what the Land of the Iron Cliffs is.”

The man smiled, but not mockingly — rather approvingly, as though he had heard what he hoped to hear: not “I came for gold,” not “I came to hide,” but “I want to understand.”

“Then you have come to the right place,” he said. “The paths of traders and refugees meet here, and those who are simply seeking themselves. If you like, I can show you the village and tell you what is what.”

August was silent, studying the stranger. He carried himself calmly; his voice was even, without haste and without any wish to please. He did not know whether to trust him, but he felt that there was no hidden meaning behind the offer — only human sympathy, born of knowing what it is to be a stranger in a harsh land. He nodded.

“My name is August.”

“And mine is Ulrik. Trader in furs and stories,” Ulrik smiled.

They sat until late in the evening. Ulrik spoke of the trails one could take, of traders who came from the south, of families who had lived here for years and spoke two languages. And of the fact that in this land the main thing is not to fear the cold and the silence.

“Here,” he said, “if you make no noise, they leave you be. And if you can play, as they say — they will find a way to listen to you. Not for merriment, but to remember that somewhere there is another world, where not only blizzards sing.”

August listened, and hope was kindling in his mind that this harsh land might become for him not an end but a new beginning. Not a place where he would hide from the past, but a place where the past would find a new meaning.

The next day Ulrik told him:

“Today — the bathhouse. A real one. Not what you are used to, but one where the road is washed away.”

He led August through the snowy streets to a small wooden structure clinging to the rock as though it had grown out of it. Thick steam rose from the chimney, mingling with the frosty air and forming a light white cloud that hung above the roof, refusing to disperse. It smelled of wet wood, smoke and heated stone. Boards lay on the floor, and wooden tubs of water stood upon them. In a corner were stacked wooden buckets and dippers, darkened by time and water.

“Undress,” said Ulrik, taking off his jacket. “No one is shy here. Here people survive.”

August took off his clothes and hung them on a nail by the entrance. His skin broke out in goosebumps from the cold air at once, but inside, behind the thick log walls, warmth was already felt, coming from the stone stove that had heated the stones to a red glow. He entered the steam room after Ulrik — low, narrow, barely room for four. Water hissed on the hot stones, raising billows of thick, damp steam. It smelled of juniper and birch twigs, which hung on the walls as though waiting their turn.

He sat on a wooden bench, feeling the heat wrap his body, the muscles cramped by cold and road begin to loosen, the tension accumulated over weeks dissolving in the warmth. Around him sat men — silent, with coarse hands and weathered faces. No one stared at him, but all felt his presence, and in that silent acknowledgement there was more acceptance than in any words.

August closed his eyes. He remembered the Imperial Baths in Vistaria — the marble, the oil lamps, the silence broken only by the murmur of water. There the silence had been artificial, created for comfort. Here the silence was real — heavy, dense, filled with the crackle of wood, the hiss of water and the breathing of people who knew the price of warmth.

He took a wooden dipper, scooped cold water from the tub and poured it over the hot stones. Steam rose with a hiss, scalding the skin, and for a moment the world shrank to this sound, this heat, this sensation of being alive. He breathed out, feeling the sweat run down his chest, his back, his legs — washing away the salt, the fatigue and the heaviness he had carried within.

Beside him sat a man with a thick fair beard and broad shoulders covered in scars. He looked at August, at his body — grown thin but strong, with fine white lines of scars on his sides and arms. The man said nothing. He only nodded, as though he had seen such bodies a hundred times: the bodies of people who had walked, fallen, risen and walked again. In that nod was a silent acknowledgement: you have walked your road, and I respect it.

When the heat became almost unbearable, Ulrik rose and nodded to August:

“We go out.”

August followed him out of the steam room and headed for the door, expecting they would go to dress. But Ulrik flung open the outer door and stepped into the snow — naked, without clothing, unafraid of the cold, as though he were on familiar terms with it. August froze for an instant, feeling the cold air touch his heated skin, the world become piercingly bright and sharp. Then he stepped after him.

The snow crunched under bare feet. The frost struck his face, his chest, his shoulders, penetrating under the skin, making his heart beat faster. August stopped, feeling the cold enter his body, steam rising from his skin into the frosty air and forming a thin haze. Ulrik stood beside him, looking at the sky, at the heavy, low clouds that promised fresh snow. Without a word, he bent down, scooped up a handful of snow and began to rub it over his chest, his arms, his face. The snow melted on his skin, turning to water that ran down his body, washing away the last of the sweat and heat.

August did as he did. He scooped up snow, and the cold burned his palms, but when he rubbed it over his chest, he felt the blood rush to the skin, his body waking, a strange, almost unbearable clarity spreading within him. The snow melted on his shoulders, his arms, his face, and he stood in the midst of the white world, naked, shivering — but alive. They stood like that for a minute, letting cold and heat meet within them and forge that northern tempering which makes a person stronger.

Then Ulrik clapped him on the shoulder and said:

“Now you know how people wash here.”

They returned to the dressing room, and the cold no longer seemed so piercing — it had become familiar, almost kin. August dressed, feeling his skin burn from the snow and the steam, every fibre of his body singing with life. He was clean — not as after a bathhouse in the south, but differently. More deeply. More truly.

Ulrik looked at his footwear — old, worn leather boots with thin soles. He shook his head.

“Those are not for the north,” he said simply. “You will freeze in them the moment you step beyond the village. Tomorrow morning I will take you to a man. He makes boots of reindeer hide with fur inside. In those, feet do not freeze even in the longest frosts.”

August looked at his boots. He felt the cold of the floor penetrating them, and remembered how his feet had ached on nights by the fire, how he had pressed them to the flames trying to bring back the feeling of life. He nodded.

“Good,” he said.

The next morning Ulrik brought him to the leatherworker — an old man with hands covered in scars and calluses, with eyes that had seen so many winters they had stopped counting them. He looked over August’s feet, measured them without a word, as though words were superfluous and only the shape and size mattered, and how the foot held the ground. He went into the next room and returned half an hour later with a pair of high boots of coarse reindeer hide, with thick soles and dense fur inside.

“Try them,” he said.

August took off the old boots and put on the new ones. They were heavy, but when he had them on, he felt the fur softly embrace his feet, protecting them from the cold, as though the northern wind itself had decided to become an ally for a time. He stood, stamped his feet — and felt the cold cease to touch his soles, felt the warmth remain within, not scattering into the frost. For the first time in many weeks his feet were truly warm.

He paid, thanked the leatherworker and stepped out onto the snow. Fresh snow crunched underfoot, and a quiet confidence spread through his chest. He was ready for winter. Not because he had become invulnerable, but because he now knew: the cold can be met, can be understood, can be tamed. And in that knowledge there was strength.
