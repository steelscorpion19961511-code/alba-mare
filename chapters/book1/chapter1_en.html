When they talk about paradise, they rarely specify that it is located on the shore of the azure sea. But the one who has seen Alba-Mare knows: that is exactly what it looks like.

The name of the city from the old language literally meant "White Shore". But there was a second one — more lyrical, which only the locals knew: "Light born of the sea". They said that the first settlers chose this place not by chance. They were looking not just for land by the water — they were looking for a place where the sky meets the sea so close that the border between them disappears. And they found it.

Here the sunrises were so long that it seemed as if the sun was not rising, but sprouting from the water. First — a thin golden thread on the horizon, then — the slow, almost lazy birth of light, which spilled across the sky like honey. And at this hour the sea became transparent to the very bottom, and one could see fish gliding between the stones, not believing that the night had passed.

The air here was so transparent that on a clear day one could see the mountains on the other side of the bay — blue, ghostly, as if painted on a canvas of haze. They did not know winters at all, and autumn was only a light shadow of summer. It came not with cold, but with transparency: the air became a little more ringing, the sea — deeper, and the roses, it seemed, bloomed even more luxuriantly, as if saying goodbye to the warmth until next year. Leaves fell from the trees rarely and without haste, and they were not burned, but left to lie on the paths so that they would rustle underfoot until December itself.

Life in Alba-Mare flowed not by the clock — it flowed by the tides. The fishermen knew when the sea was generous and when it was simply breathing. Children woke up not from the ringing of bells, but from the cry of seagulls and the smell of bread rising from the bakery on the corner. In the evenings, oil lamps were lit in the port, and their light, reflected in the water, trembled like a living thing.

People here were in no hurry. They knew that tomorrow would be the same as today — and that did not frighten them. There was no boredom in this, there was confidence. Confidence that the world would not collapse, that the sea would not rise above the walls, that tomorrow would bring nothing but the same sun, the same waves, and the same smiles.

Sometimes foreign ships entered the port. They brought overseas fabrics with unusual patterns, stories of distant kingdoms, and smells that had never been heard in Alba-Mare. But these ships never stayed long. It seemed to them that it was too quiet here. Too calm. Too real.

Alba-Mare had its own voice.

It began long before dawn, when the sea was still breathing in its sleep, and the city lay motionless, like a stone warmed by the sun during the day. The first to wake were the seagulls — their cries were sharp and short, like blows on the water. Then somewhere in the port a boat creaked, and this creak carried along the shore like a reminder: the night is over, you can begin.

By noon the voice of the city became dense. It mixed the sound of footsteps on cobblestones, the clinking of dishes in taverns, the voices of merchants calling to buyers, and the distant ringing of bells from the Council tower. It was not noise — it was a pulse. Alive, warm, a little hurried, but without alarm.

By evening the voice changed. It became deeper, quieter, almost pensive. The waves ran onto the shore with an even, lulling whisper. Oil lamps were lit in the windows, and their light, reflecting in the dark water, seemed to continue the conversation begun in the morning. The city did not fall silent — it simply switched to another language, understandable only to those who knew how to listen.

August knew how to listen, for he had grown up among these sounds — and did not know that it could be otherwise somewhere. It seemed to him that silence was when the sea fell silent for a moment between waves. And in these moments he heard something else — as if the air itself vibrated, folding into a melody that no one played.

For the first time this realization came to him at the age of eight.

It happened on one of those days when his father had gone to work, his mother was busy in the house, and August had nothing to do. Then he went to the old pier, where fishermen were mending nets and it smelled of tar and wet boards. Sitting on a sun-warmed stone and dangling his legs towards the water, the boy stared at the horizon, where the sea merged with the sky in one pale line.

How long he sat like that — an hour or two — did not matter. His eyes followed the seagulls circling over the boats, the light ripples running towards the shore, the clouds that slowly changed shape. And suddenly he heard it.

Not voices. Not cries. Not splashing.

Something else. Something that was not there, but that sounded inside him, as if someone was quietly singing in a language he did not know but understood. When he closed his eyes, he realized: the city itself was talking to him.

August did not know how to explain it. He did not know that it was called music. He only knew that it was his. That he wanted to listen again and again, until he remembered every note, every breath of this sound.

The sun was already beginning to lean towards sunset, and the shadows were growing long. His fingers, resting on his knees, trembled slightly, as if already plucking strings that did not yet exist.

His mother found him by the very water when it began to get dark. She stood on the pier, arms folded across her chest, and looked at him with an expression he could not understand then — a mixture of anxiety and some quiet, almost sad pride.

— August, — she called softly. Quietly, as if afraid to wake him.

He did not turn, continuing to look at the water.

— August, — she repeated. — Let's go home. Dinner will get cold.

Slowly turning his head, the boy looked at his mother. His eyes were clear, but something new had appeared in them — something she had not seen before.

— I heard it, — he said quietly. — Do you hear it too?

She froze for a moment. Then she came closer, sat down next to him on the stone — not on the pier, but on the very stone where he sat. She put her hand on his shoulder.

— What did you hear? — she asked.

— I don't know, — August answered. — But it was there.

His mother was silent. She looked at the sea, at the evening sky, which was already beginning to darken. And she said what he remembered for the rest of his life:

— You hear what most people stop hearing when they grow up. Don't lose it, August. Even if others say it's empty.

Of course, he did not understand her words then. But he remembered them. And the warmth of her hand on his shoulder. And the evening light that trembled on the water like a living thing.

They returned home when it was completely dark. His father sat at the table, gloomy, and glanced at August with slight displeasure.

— Where were you? — he asked shortly.

— On the pier, — said August.

— Sat and looked at the water, — his mother added calmly, placing a plate of hot soup in front of her son. — It's good for him.

His father wanted to say something, but changed his mind. He only shook his head and returned to his mug.

August ate in silence. But his fingers — under the table, where no one could see — were still trembling, memorizing a movement that did not yet exist.

From that day on, he began to come to the pier every day.

At first — just for a few minutes. Then — for whole hours, when other children were playing tag or helping adults. He sat on the same stone, closed his eyes, and listened.

Sometimes it seemed to him that he heard a melody — not entirely, but only its edges, its breath. As if it were somewhere nearby, around the corner, but not yet daring to show itself. He waited. Endured. Learned to listen so as not to frighten it away.

August did not talk about it with anyone — not with friends, not with his father, not even with his mother. Only with her did he sometimes exchange brief glances: she looked at him through the kitchen window when he returned from the pier, and he saw in her eyes the same quiet, understanding light. She said nothing. And he was grateful to her for that.

Then, at the age of eight, he simply sat on the stone, looked at the sea, and listened to how the world breathed. And the world, it seemed, breathed back — slowly, deeply, like an old friend who is in no hurry to speak, but is always ready to listen.
