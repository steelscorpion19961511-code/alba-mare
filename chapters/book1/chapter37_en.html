He left Holmstad at dawn on the fourth day. The snow was dense, packed down overnight, and walking on it was easier than on loose snow — his boots did not sink but only slightly pressed the firm crust, and every step sent through his body a measured, calm confidence. The sky was clean and cold, and low in it hung a pale sun that gave almost no warmth but at least gave light — silvery, sharp, as if chiselled out of ice.

August walked north, toward the place where the Land of the Iron Cliffs began beyond the hills, and with every step he felt the air grow drier and sharper, as if each breath were cleansing him of something superfluous. The road was empty. Sometimes he came upon tracks — deer, wolf, human — but there were no people. The settlements grew rarer and the mountains closer: they rose from the horizon in heavy, angular masses, as if reaching themselves toward that cold sky.

He walked, listening to the crunch of snow under his boots and to his own breathing, which burst from his mouth in clouds of steam and dissolved at once in the frosty air. It seemed to him that he was alone in this world, and from that solitude a strange, almost tranquil clarity spread through him — not emptiness, but the sense that all needless anxieties had been left behind, in the warmth and noise of the southern cities.

Toward evening on the second day he came out to the sea. But it was not the sea he knew. In Alba-Mare it had been azure and warm, smelling of salt and sunlight, and its waves had caressed the white sand. Here the sea was different — dark, almost black, with white caps of foam that the wind tore from the waves and carried through the air like little snow spirals. The shore was stony, and along it stretched cliffs — high, sharp-ridged, with dark veins that resembled old scars on the body of the earth. When a wave struck the rocks, the sound was muffled and lingering, as if the land itself were breathing in that rhythm, heavy and unhurried.

August stopped and looked at the water for a long time. Far off on the horizon he saw the dark silhouette of a ship — a fishing vessel, perhaps, or a warship. He did not know. But for some reason he felt that this shore was not simply a place on the map. It was a place where something important began, something that would change him the way Alba-Mare had changed him at the beginning of his journey.

He walked along the shore, keeping close to the rocks so the wind could not knock him down. The snow lay unevenly here, blown by the wind into strange patterns like frozen waves, and walking grew harder, but he did not stop. He knew: somewhere here there had to be people. And he wanted to see them — not through other people's tales, not through the contemptuous words of southerners, but with his own eyes.

On the second day he noticed smoke. It rose from behind a cliff in a thin thread, almost invisible against the grey sky, but to August it was a sign of life — quiet, restrained, and unmistakable. August quickened his pace and, rounding the rock, saw a small settlement. The houses were built of dark stone, their roofs turfed, and nets hung drying around them on wooden stakes. By the shore stood boats — narrow, light, with high prows like those he had seen in Heimrir's tales. Narrow, curved, with carved dragon heads at the bow, they looked less like ships than like children of this harsh world — as if the rocks themselves had begotten them to glide over these cold waves.

August froze, looking at them. He felt a strange agitation rise in his chest — a mixture of curiosity and caution. He did not know whether to go further. This was his first meeting with those who did not know the southern tongue, with those whom southerners called brigands and murderers. But he no longer believed those words. He wanted to see them himself, to hear their voices, to understand what life flowed through these cold lands.

He repeated to himself the words Einar had taught him: “Heim-fjord…” — and took a step forward.

A man came out of one of the houses. He was tall and thin, with a long fair beard and hair plaited into fine braids. His clothes were rough, of wool and leather, and a long knife hung at his belt. He looked at August without surprise but with an attentive, measuring gaze — the way one looks at the trail of a beast, trying to tell whether it is dangerous, whether it is hungry, whether it has come for prey or has simply lost its way. For several moments they stood in silence. August felt the wind chill his face and heard gulls crying somewhere far away — their cries sharp, almost alarming, yet familiar to this place.

“Heim-fjord,” August said, trying to say it the way Einar had taught him, putting into the words not only sound but respect for a foreign land.

The man held his gaze for a moment, and in his eyes flashed something like surprise — not so much at the words themselves as at the fact that a stranger had tried to speak their language at all. He did not answer, but he did not walk away and made no threatening gesture. He simply stood, looking at the man who had come from the south, weighing him — not by his clothes and not by his weapon, but by how he carried himself, by how he looked, by how he breathed this cold air.

“Heim-fjord,” August repeated, taking a step forward and spreading his arms wide to show that he had no weapon, that he had come not for battle but for meeting. The gesture was simple, and it needed no words.

The man nodded slowly. His face remained severe, but something flickered at the corners of his eyes — perhaps curiosity, perhaps respect for the fact that the stranger was trying to speak in his tongue rather than demanding an answer in his own. He stepped aside, clearing the way, and said a short phrase that August did not understand.

August shook his head.

“I don't understand,” he said in his own language, hiding nothing, not pretending to know more than he did.

The man looked at him and, after a pause, pointed his hand toward one of the buildings, where by the door stood an elderly woman with a face creased as if all the winds and all the winters of this shore had been carved into it.

August nodded and went toward her, feeling a quiet gratitude spread through him — that he had not been driven away, that he had been given a chance, that even in this harsh land there was room for cautious trust.

He entered the house and was met by the smell of smoke, dry grass and dampness. It was the smell of a northern dwelling, to which he was already growing accustomed — a smell with none of the coziness of southern homes but with reliability in it, as if the walls and the smoke themselves guarded against cold and wind. The woman showed him a place by the hearth and set before him a clay mug with a warm drink that smelled of honey and of herbs, slightly bitter, northern. August took it, feeling the cold slowly release his fingers, and allowed himself for a moment to close his eyes. He did not know what he would say to these people. But he knew he was where he was meant to be, and that this path, however hard it might be, was leading him toward something important.

When he opened his eyes, the woman was sitting across from him, watching him with curiosity that held no fear. A shadow of a smile crossed her lips — not broad, not joyful, but restrained, like that of those accustomed to sparing their warmth and their words.

“Name?” she asked in broken language that he understood.

“August,” he answered.

She nodded, as if testing whether the name settled on her ear.

“Av-gust,” she repeated, savouring the sounds, tasting them the way one tastes unfamiliar honey.

They sat by the fire, and the house was warm. Snow began to fall again, covering the world in white silence, and beyond the window nothing could be seen any longer — only a white veil in which the rocks, the boats and the tracks on the shore dissolved. August felt that he was not alone on this journey — and that the north he had feared so much was, in truth, simply a land where people breathed the same air he did, sought warmth the same way, guarded their fires the same way, and tried in the same way to understand the strangers among them.
