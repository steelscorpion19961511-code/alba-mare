For the next few days August stayed with Einar. They sat by the fire, ate dried fish and hot stew from the kettle, listened to the wind and watched the snow fall upon the rocks, settling in layers, as though writing into this land the days lived in silence. Einar spoke little, but every word was weighed, as though he measured it out like a grain of salt — enough to last, and never spoiling the taste.

“Snow here is not simply water,” he said, nodding at the drifts overhanging the rocks, heavy, ready to come crashing down. “It can kill you. If you do not know where to shelter, if you cannot feel how the wind is changing — you will freeze. The first winter here is a lesson not everyone survives.”

August watched his hands — coarse, scarred and calloused, accustomed to the axe, the rope and the wind — listened and memorised. He tried not merely to hear the words but to see behind them what Einar left unspoken.

“Look: if the wind blows from the sea — expect a blizzard. If it blows from the mountains — it will be dry, but cold. You must learn to read this. Not with your eyes. With your skin.”

He showed him how to light a fire even in wet weather: how to peel the outer layer of bark from frozen branches to reach the dry heartwood, how to stack the wood so the first gust would not smother the flame, how to find dry twigs beneath the snow by reading the sparse bushes and ledges of rock. August learned, feeling his body adjust to a new rhythm — do not rush, do not panic, trust what you see and hear. Here a single mistake could cost you warmth, and warmth was dearer than gold.

On the second evening, when the snow paused for a moment and the sky opened a crack, revealing a scatter of sharp, cold stars, Einar said unexpectedly:

“You don’t know their language.”

“Whose?” August asked, though he had already guessed.

“The northerners. The people of the Iron Cliffs,” Einar answered. “If you want to understand them, you must at least be able to greet them. Otherwise they will not even stop to hear you out. For them, gesture and word are a boundary. Cross it wrongly — and you do not exist.”

August looked at the fire in silence. The flame was reflected in his eyes, and it seemed to him that in those reflections he saw not only warmth but also the invisible wall that separated him from the people of this land.

“Teach me,” he said.

Einar smiled — the same quiet, almost imperceptible smile that made his face not stern at all, but somehow gentler than this land allowed.

“Then listen,” he said. “Repeat after me. Do not hurry.”

He pronounced a short phrase in the northern tongue. The sound was rough, guttural, with deep vowels that seemed to be born somewhere in the chest rather than on the lips, and in it one could hear the noise of wind and the weight of stone. August repeated it, stumbling, and the sound came out foreign, brittle, like a branch someone tries to bend without warming it first.

“Again. Slower. Do not drag the vowels out — let them fall, like a stone into water.”

“Heim-fjord…” August tried.

“Better,” Einar approved. “But do not rush. This tongue does not tolerate haste. The more surely you speak, the better they will understand you. Here a word is like a step on ice: if you hurry, you fall through.”

So they sat by the fire, and August learned to pronounce simple words: “greeting,” “thank you,” “I am a friend,” “I wish to speak with you.” Einar explained not only the sounds but the meaning of the words: that each carries weight, that they are spoken only in certain situations, that northerners do not waste words on the empty.

“Their language has no word for ‘perhaps,’” he said one day. “There is ‘yes’ and there is ‘no.’ And there is ‘I will think on it.’ Everything else is not for them. If you say ‘perhaps,’ they will think you do not know what you want. And not knowing is dangerous here.”

August memorised it all, feeling the language open a new world before him — not as a set of sounds but as a way of seeing differently, of noticing what had earlier remained in shadow. He repeated the words again and again until they began to sit more easily on his tongue, until they acquired their own weight, their own truth.

By the third day he could say a short phrase of greeting almost without error. Einar listened attentively, head slightly bowed, as though he were listening not to the words but to how they sounded in this place, whether they suited the wind and the stones. At last he nodded.

“Good. If you say that to the first northerner you meet, he may not kill you at once. He may even ask why you are here.”

August smiled, but there was no mirth in the smile — only relief mixed with unease.

“I will remember.”

They sat in silence, watching the fire. The snow began again, large and sparse, and every flake, as it fell, flashed for an instant in the light of the fire before melting. August felt himself beginning to grow used to this rhythm: the crackle of wood, the whistle of the wind, the silence that here was not the absence of sound but something living, almost tangible.

“Why are you teaching me?” he asked quietly, when the silence had grown so deep that he could hear his own thoughts within it.

Einar was silent a long time, looking into the flame, as though searching it for answers he could not pronounce at once. Then he threw a branch on the fire, and the sparks rose, swirling like tiny fireflies before vanishing into the dark.

“Because I have seen men go north and not return. Not because they were killed — because they did not understand where they were. They walked as through a foreign song and could not hear its rhythm. And you — you hear it. I see it in the way you look, in how you listen to the wind, in how you remember small things. That is worth teaching.”

He paused, then added, a little quieter, as though entrusting to the fire what he would not wish the echo of the rocks to hear:

“And also because I am tired of seeing fear where understanding might be. If one man comes to know this land, its people, its language — perhaps he will tell others that beyond the Cliffs live not monsters, but those who seek the same things we do: warmth, a home, a place where someone waits for you.”

August said nothing. It seemed to him that any words now would be superfluous, that they would only break the thin bond that had formed between him and this harsh land, between him and Einar. He looked at the fire, at the dancing tongues of flame, and felt something within him settling into place, as though he had finally found the right note in this northern song.

The snow fell, the fire crackled, and the silence between them grew deeper still — that kind of silence which does not demand to be filled, because everything is already in it: the past, the present, and what is yet to come. The feeling of safety born by that fire would stay with him for a long time — not as a weakness, but as a strength he could draw from memory when he found himself alone once more among cliffs and wind.

And when night finally settled upon the earth, wrapping it in a dense blanket of darkness, Einar said quietly:

“Tomorrow we go on. I will show you the trail the locals use. The one where the wind does not knock you down — it whispers the way.”

August nodded. He felt neither joy nor fear — only a calm readiness. He was ready to listen.
