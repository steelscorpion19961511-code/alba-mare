The road to the village took two days.

The path wound between hills covered in wild shrubs. It was quiet all around — only the wind stirring the grass, and the air smelled of dry earth and unfamiliar flowers. August walked unhurried but steadily, feeling each step bring him closer to something important. A strange feeling was growing in his chest: not simply hope, but almost certainty, as if the road itself were leading him, and he could not turn aside from it.

The village turned out to be small — fifteen or so houses scattered down the slope of a hill. The roofs were tiled, the walls whitewashed with lime, and fishing nets and gear hung drying everywhere. The locals turned to look at the stranger with curiosity, but without hostility. Here there was none of the bustle of the big ports, and even their gazes seemed gentler — as if the people were used to strangers coming and going, and saw no threat in it.

August stopped by the first house. A woman hanging out laundry paused for a moment, then smiled at the corner of her mouth and pointed him toward the street leading to the sea:

"The refugees live at the far end. Go straight, you'll see it."

The little street narrowed, winding between olive trees and lavender bushes. His heart beat faster than usual, but his step stayed even. The way seemed endless, though in truth it took only a few minutes. Each turn seemed to hold back the moment that was to change everything.

A low house with a stone fence stood almost at the water's edge. The door was ajar. Through the gap August saw a woman sitting on the porch. She was sorting dried herbs — grey-haired, weary, with that particular carriage of the shoulders that could not be mistaken for anything else. Her hands moved softly, familiarly, as if touching something living.

August stopped at the gate. His heart was beating somewhere in his throat, and he could not take the step. He looked at her and could not believe this was really happening. She was here. She was alive. She sat on the same porch, with the same herbs in her hands, as if nothing had changed, as if Alba-Mare had not burned, as if his father were not lying by the eastern gates with a broken spear in his hands.

He drew a breath. Opened the gate. Stepped into the yard.

The woman raised her head and froze. For a moment he saw everything in her eyes — astonishment, pain, hope, a quiet, almost unbearable joy. Then she rose, stepped toward him, and her hand touched his cheek — slowly, as if checking whether he was real, whether he had not been dreamed once again.

"August," she breathed.

He stepped forward and sank to his knees before her. She embraced him, and her hands trembled on his shoulders. There were no words — they simply sat on the porch, holding on to one another, and the silence between them was fuller than any conversation. In that silence was everything: the farewell to what was gone, the gratitude for what remained, and the promise that now they would not lose each other again.

When they came back to themselves, his mother held his hand between her palms, and they talked — haltingly at first, then more freely, as if each spoken word made it easier to breathe.

"I buried him on the shore," August said. "The way it's done among us."

She closed her eyes and exhaled slowly. There was pain in that breath, but no questions.

"Father held the gates to the last," August went on. "He gave you time to get away."

His mother nodded without opening her eyes:

"I knew it. He was always that way. And I knew you would come."

"How did you find me?" she asked quietly.

"I searched," August answered. "I asked people, showed them your portrait from memory. An old fisherman in a port town told me he had seen you. That you were asking about a man with a guitar."

"And you believed him?"

"I chose to believe," he said. "You always told me that faith is not knowledge. It is a choice. I made my choice."

His mother squeezed his hand and said nothing. But in her silence there was more than in any words.

He told her everything: how he had come back to the guitar, how he had fled the city, how he had found his father's body and given it to the fire on the shore of the bay of farewells. He spoke of the road, of meeting Arno, of Liss, of the ship and the fighting in the strait. Sometimes she would squeeze his hand, sometimes smile — when he told of the songs, and of playing for the sailors. In these small things she recognized her son, the boy who had once hidden his hands behind his back, afraid they were too clumsy for the strings.

Then she told him her own story.

She had not been born in Alba-Mare. She had grown up here, in this village, by this sea. Her mother, old and grey-haired, still lived in the next room. She had left these places young, when she met August's father, and had spent her whole life with him in Alba-Mare. And now — she had returned.

"I thought I would never come back," she said, looking out at the sea. "But when everything fell apart, I understood: there is only one place I could go. Not because it is safe there. But because there, they know me."

August said nothing. He looked at her and saw in her not merely a mother who had lost her husband and her city, but a woman who had come home. For the first time in a long while he understood: she had not simply survived — she had found a place where she could breathe.

The next day he met her mother. An old, bowed woman with eyes that had seen more than they could say. She sat by the hearth, warming her hands, and studied August with a long, measuring gaze.

"You look like him," she said quietly. "Like your father. The same look."

August found no words. He simply nodded and sat down beside her.

He stayed in the village for several weeks. He worked from morning to evening. He mended the roof that had leaked through the winter. He carried water from the well, chopped firewood, laying in enough to last until spring. He fixed the old fence the wind had toppled and dug over a plot of ground so his mother could plant vegetables in the spring. He did it all silently, without needless words, and his mother watched him with the same expression as in his childhood — a mixture of pride and quiet sadness.

She had found a place in a small school at the local temple — teaching children to read and count. The learning she had gained through her years on the Council was needed here more than anywhere. In the evenings she told him about her pupils, about how they were getting used to letters, and August saw light come into her eyes.

And in the evenings he took the guitar and played for them — for his mother, for the old grandmother, for the silence that no longer weighed. He played old melodies he remembered from childhood, and new ones born on the road. The old woman sometimes closed her eyes, sometimes nodded in time. And once, when he finished, she said:

"You've taken after him. After your father."

August raised his head.

"Was he like that?"

The grandmother chuckled, showing her few teeth.

"He was quite the adventurer. In his youth — never a day in one place. Out the door the moment a road appeared. Your mother barely caught up with him. She traveled in those days too."

She paused, looking at the fire.

"He only settled down when you appeared. When he understood he had a home now. Not because he wanted to — because he had to."

August was silent. He looked into the fire and for the first time saw his father not just as a guardsman who had held the gates to the last, but as a man who had once searched for something beyond the horizon too. And had stopped — for their sake.

August smiled. He did not know what it meant. But it warmed him to hear it.

One evening, when the sun was sinking into the sea and the air had turned cool and clear, his mother took his hand and said:

"I would like you to stay. But I know that you cannot. You are like your father. Not because you are stubborn, but because you are searching. And that is right."

She fell silent, and a quiet fullness hung between them. Then she added softly:

"You do not have to stay here. You came. You found me. But your life is not in this village. I always wanted you to find your own path. You have found it. It is not here. It is out there — where the wind and the songs are."

For a long time he sat motionless, looking at the sea. Then he answered:

"I know."

In the morning he gathered his things — the sword, the guitar, the pack holding his songs on crumpled scraps of paper. Fresh bread, dried fruit, and other provisions for the road, carefully packed by his mother. His mother stood at the gate, but he did not turn around, though he felt her gaze on his back. He knew she would stay here. That this place was no accident. That she had returned to where her life had once begun. And he knew he could write to her — through traders, through wanderers, through those who traveled from south to north.

He walked along the path leading out of the village, and in his chest there was not emptiness but lightness. Not the lightness of forgetting, but the kind that comes after a long-awaited thing has come true. He had found his mother. He had seen that she was alive. And he had let her go — as she had let him go.
