On the fourth day a stir began in the settlement — the kind that comes when winter yields, however briefly, to human will, and people decide to defy it. From morning they came out of their houses wearing their best clothes: woollen jackets, embroidered belts, fur caps kept in chests for special days. From the sheds they brought out boats, checked nets and spears, tapped wooden mallets against the fastenings to make sure everything held fast. The air smelled of smoke, dried fish and something else — juniper, burned at the thresholds to drive off evil spirits and summon luck. The smoke crept low, mingled with the frosty air, and seemed to bind the settlement together with an invisible thread.

August stood at the porch of Signe's house and watched the settlement prepare for the feast. Everything around was filled with movement, voices and some ancient, living rhythm in which there was no bustle — only clear, confident action. He felt himself a stranger, but not an unwanted one: no one pushed him away; they looked at him with that calm curiosity with which one studies a young tree that has grown up among old stones.

The old man he had met at the rune stone came up to him, leaning on a staff but standing straight, as if he himself were one of those stones.

“Today is the day of the Great Hunt,” he said. “We go out to sea and to the mountains. Those who return with quarry will feed us all. And in the evening — a fire, songs and tales. You must be with us.”

August did not know what to answer. He could not hunt, did not know the local customs, and did not want to be a burden — afraid his inexperience would let down those who had trusted him a place beside them. But the old man, as if reading his thoughts, added:

“You will watch and listen. That is part of the feast too. Here they value not only the strength of the arm but also the keenness of the eye and the quiet of the heart.”

August nodded, and in that nod there was not merely consent but the acceptance of someone else's world — without any attempt to remake it, without any wish to appear as someone else.

He went with them to the shore, where the boats stood. The fishermen checked their nets, secured their oars, felt every knot with their fingers, as if on it depended not only luck but life itself. The women gathered baskets of food for those going out to sea: flatbreads, dried meat, flasks with warm herbal broth meant to keep warmth in the icy waves.

Children ran between the grown-ups with sticks in their hands, playing at hunters, and their laughter rang like the first drops of melting water breaking through the ice.

All of it was filled with movement, voices and that same ancient rhythm which, it seemed, had lived in these lands long before people and would remain after them. August stood apart, feeling himself part of that current — not its main stream but a rivulet flowing into the greater river.

The old man came up to him and held out a small stone, polished to a shine, as if it had been kept for years in someone's palm, warmed and given part of their strength.

“Take it,” he said quietly, almost in a whisper, so as not to startle the moment. “It brings luck to those who go on the hunt with us for the first time. Let it remind you that you are not alone.”

August took the stone, feeling its smooth, cool surface which, despite the cold, seemed to hold the warmth of many hands. He put it in his pocket, close to his heart, and, glancing at the sea, thought that this day might become one of the most important of his life — not because he would perform a feat, but because he would witness something real, ancient and alive.

When the boats set off, August remained on the shore. He watched them recede, growing smaller and smaller, until they merged with the grey expanse of the sea. The wind carried to him voices, laughter and songs born on the water, and these sounds mingled with the roar of the waves and the cries of the gulls, becoming a single song of the shore. He sat down on a rock, laid the guitar across his knees and began to play. Its sound did not drown out nature but became part of it: it wove together with the rumble of the surf, with the cries of the birds, with the breathing of the wind. It seemed to him that he could see the boats going into the distance, the wind playing with their sails, and in it there was something primal and real — something that cannot be faked and cannot be forgotten.

The return was loud and joyful. The boats came back with their catch — several large fish and even one young seal, carried on deck, its dark hide gleaming like wet stone. People poured out onto the shore, helping to unload the boats, and their laughter carried over the water, as if driving off the last shadows of the winter cold. August stood apart, trying not to be in the way, but one of the hunters came up to him and held out a piece of fresh fish, still warm, smelling of the sea and of smoke.

“Eat,” the man said, smiling so that little wrinkles gathered at the corners of his eyes, as if he had spent his whole life laughing at storms and beating them. “You are part of this day too. Without those who wait on the shore, the hunt is never complete.”

August took the fish, not knowing what to say. Words seemed too small for the moment. It seemed to him that he was touching something no words could explain — the very essence of life in these lands, where every piece of food is a victory over cold and emptiness, and every person beside you is a shield against loneliness.

By evening a great bonfire was lit in the square. It burned brightly, throwing orange light across the faces of the people, making them look like heroes of ancient legends. The flame crackled, sending sparks up into the dark sky, and it seemed that those sparks carried away with them every fear and every doubt. The women set food on the tables, the men poured drinks into wooden mugs, and the children ran around laughing, throwing dry twigs into the fire so that the flame would burn brighter still. Everyone was preparing for the long night, when songs would sound and the old ones would begin to tell legends, passing them on to those who did not yet know them but had to hear them.

At some point someone clapped August on the shoulder — a man with a broad smile and a fair beard plaited into tight braids. He was still young but already conspicuous among the others, and his voice was loud and rolling, like the surf of the sea.

“You must play today!” he exclaimed, and there was no command in his voice — only a sincere wish to share the joy.

August looked at the fire, at the faces of the people, at the dark sea beyond them that breathed in time with the bonfire. He nodded and took up the guitar. In the silence that fell around, as if the whole world had held its breath, he began to play. The music was born from the very air, from the roar of the waves and the crackle of the wood, from the smell of smoke and sea, from the warmth of human voices. And it was about the journey, about the cold, about how people warm one another in the harshest lands by sharing what they have, even if it is only a piece of fish or a place by the fire. It seemed to him that he was not merely playing — he was speaking to them in a language they understood without words.

When the music died away, silence came. But not the kind that frightens — the kind that gives warmth and peace: the silence in which one hears the beating of the heart of the world. Someone clapped, someone laughed, someone said a few quiet words in their own language, and August understood that his voice had been heard. He sat by the fire, looked at the stars burning above him, scattered across the sky like sparks that had failed to fly away, and felt truly alive — not because he had survived, but because he had found a place where his music had become part of a common song.
