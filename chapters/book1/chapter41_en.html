They came down to the elder's dwelling toward evening, when the sun no longer struck the eyes but only touched the peaks of distant ridges with thin pink rays, as if testing whether that light would suffice to hold back the night. The descent proved far more treacherous than the climb: the path wound between boulders, in places turning into a barely visible strip of stone, and one had to walk sideways, pressed into the rock, feeling the ice crumble under one's boot. Bjorn moved ahead, neither hurrying nor slackening — he walked as if he knew every crack in this path, every footing where a foot could be set. August tried not to look down, remembering the hunter's advice, and instead studied the stone, trying to guess which ledge would best take his grip.

When they finally reached the house, twilight had already settled over the earth in a dense blanket. The house was built of dark stone almost indistinguishable from the cliffs, and it seemed part of the crag itself — as if not people had built it but the mountain had allowed itself to take the form of a dwelling. The narrow door stood ajar, and warm air drifted from it, smelling of smoke, dry grass and something ancient that was hard to name in words but that at once answered in the chest with a familiar feeling of comfort.

August followed Bjorn inside and stopped at the threshold, not knowing how to behave. He felt too large for this low doorway, too noisy for this silence, too southern for this place. Inside, a hearth burned, and its light danced on the walls, picking faces out of the darkness — people sitting by the fire. There were few of them: several old men and two women, who talked quietly among themselves but fell silent at the appearance of the guests — not with alarm but with that calm attention with which one listens to the first drops of rain after a long drought.

The elder sat by the fire, closer to the flame than anyone — not to warm himself but to see in it what others could not. He was older than anyone August had met in the north: his face was furrowed with wrinkles like the bark of an old tree, and his hair was white as the snow beyond the window. But his eyes remained clear as water in a mountain stream — in them there was neither weariness nor irritation, only endless patience, as if he knew how to wait as naturally as he breathed. He looked at August for a long time without a word, and August felt almost transparent under that gaze, as if the old man saw not only his clothes, his boots and his sword but all his doubts, fears and hopes hidden deep within.

Then the elder slowly opened his palm and laid on his knee a piece of old wood with runes carved into it. The wood had darkened with time, but the lines remained distinct, as if they had been cut yesterday and not long years ago. He ran his finger along one of them, as if recalling the touch of the hands that had once made this pattern, and began to speak in a voice like the rustle of dry leaves that the wind carries over frozen ground:

“From the south?” he asked — not so much to learn as to hear how August would say the word.

“Yes,” August answered, trying to speak calmly although everything within him trembled with strain.

The elder nodded, as if he had expected that answer, as if he had known of August's coming long before he appeared at the door. He turned his gaze to the fire, and for a moment the flame was reflected in his eyes, making them almost golden. And then he said the words that would ring long in August's memory, like an echo in a deep cave:

“South and north are two sides of one body. Neither can live without the other. When the south suffers, the north feels the pain. When the north freezes, the south loses its warmth. We are not enemies. We are continuations of one another that have forgotten this.”

August sat unmoving, afraid to startle the moment away. These words entered him deeper than any cold, deeper than the pain in his worn feet and aching muscles. There was no consolation in them — there was truth, severe and simple as these mountains. He looked at the elder, feeling his breathing grow steadier, and the silence in the house seemed fuller to him than any words, as if all the stories ever told at this hearth lived within it.

“You are seeking answers?” the elder asked again, and there was no curiosity in the question — only the wish to understand what this stranger had come carrying.

“No,” August answered after a short pause, because he understood that he was indeed not seeking ready-made solutions. “I am seeking understanding. I want to understand why everything is arranged this way and not otherwise. I want to hear the voices of these lands so as not to be a stranger to them.”

The elder looked at him for a long moment, and in that silence August felt that his words had been heard and accepted. The old man reached out and touched the hilt of August's sword, lying at his feet. The metal was cold, but the elder's touch seemed warm, as if it carried not only physical force but the memory of generations.

“You carry your father's blade,” the elder said quietly, almost in a whisper, so that these words would reach August alone. “So you have memory of what has been lost. And you have music as well, which comes to you even in silence. I heard you play by the fire in the settlement. There were more words in your melody than in some speech. These two things are your path. One protects; the other heals. Do not lose either. Let the blade guard your life, and the music — your soul.”

He fell silent. In the house there was only the breathing of the fire and the soft crackle of the wood, as if the hearth itself were listening to these words. Then the elder rose, moving slowly but without effort, as if his body knew how to husband its strength, and took from the shelf an old, yellowed manuscript. The leather binding was battered, its corners rounded by time, and on the cover a barely distinguishable pattern could be seen, resembling the intertwining of roots. He held it out to August:

“Take it. These are the stories my forefathers told. There are no maps in them, no exact dates, but there is what matters more: memory of how we lived, what we feared, what we rejoiced in. I can keep them no longer — my hands no longer hold the pen, and my eyes no longer see the letters as clearly as before. You may carry them away, and they will become yours. Let them tell you what I will not live to tell.”

August accepted the manuscript, feeling its weight settle into his palms. It was not heavy, but dense, as if every page had absorbed years and winters. He did not know what was written in it, but he understood: this was not simply a book. It was a key to this world — to its secrets and its pain, to its beauty and its severity.

Bjorn, standing a little apart, inclined his head slightly, as if confirming that this was how it should be. The women by the wall exchanged quiet glances and smiled — not broadly, but barely perceptibly, the way one smiles upon seeing that justice and order have been restored.

When August left the house, the snow was still falling, but no longer so thick as before: the flakes had grown larger and fell more slowly, as if the sky had decided not to hurry. He stopped at the threshold, looking at the snow-covered mountains, which now seemed to him not merely stone masses but living creatures keeping their secrets. The wind touched his face, and in that touch he heard not only cold but the promise of warmth that would surely come, if one did not give up.

He understood that his journey continued. But now he walked not simply forward — he carried with him not only the sword and memories but another's wisdom, another's pain and another's hope. And in that burden there was no heaviness: there was strength.
