He walked three more days.

The road wound between the hills, sometimes climbing toward the rocks, sometimes descending to shallow streams. He stopped to drink, to eat a piece of the bread the trader had given him, and walked on. The guitar hung at his back — now and then he touched it, just to be sure it was still there.

On the fourth day he saw the city.

It was Greymoot. It stood at the foot of the Grey Ridges, on the very edge of the Deepwood. The forest was coniferous — tall sequoias and dense firs stretching dozens of meters toward the sky. Timber was cut here and sent overland to Seacliff, a port town on the eastern coast, and from there by ship to the capital. The resin gathered in these woods was prized above all: it was used to seal ship planking and ropes, and no vessel in these parts could do without it. The air smelled of pine, of resin, of wet bark and sawmill smoke. The city was grey not from poverty but because all its stone and timber had been hewn from grey rock and dark trunks.

Greymoot was smaller than Alba-Mare. Its walls were grey, of rough stone, without the familiar whiteness. The gates stood open; people came and went without a backward glance.

August stopped at the entrance, looking down the streets. He did not know what he wanted from this place. But he knew he could not walk on without stopping.

Inside it was noisy and cramped. Narrow streets — people, carts, dogs. Wagons loaded with logs crawled toward the gates, their heavy creaking blending with voices and the hammering of mallets. Workshops lined the streets where craftsmen labored: joiners, wood-carvers, carpenters.

August paused at one of the workshops. Behind the counter sat a man carving patterns into a wooden board. Finished pieces lay alongside: carved spoons, bowls, boxes, children's toys. The wood was pale and smooth; the air smelled of fresh shavings. August ran a finger over one of the boxes — it was warm and sleek.

A little farther on, the coopers were at work — the masters who made barrels and casks for wine, oil and pickles. August stopped at their shop and watched two men deftly bend the hoops and fit the staves so they pressed tightly together. He had never given thought to how much labor went into a simple barrel. Here it was an art.

The smells here were sharp and foreign: roasting meat, leather, dung, smoke. Nothing here was like Alba-Mare. No sea, no white stone, no quiet. But there was life flowing in its own rhythm, and August felt that this city would not ask him who he was — it would simply take him in for a while.

He passed the craftsmen's row and came out onto a small square. An old stone fountain stood there, dry and overgrown with moss. Benches around it; old men talking; by one of the benches a woman selling her wares. August stopped by the fountain.

He did not notice when the staring began. First one, then another. People whispered and pointed. August caught fragments of speech: "a wanderer," "from the west." He did not understand what was so unusual about him, but he felt himself being sized up.

He was about to leave when a young man with red hair and quick eyes came up to him. He nodded at the long narrow case on August's back.

"What have you got there?" he asked.

"An instrument," August answered shortly.

The man grinned:

"Don't fret, I won't take it from you. Can you play it?"

"Yes," said August.

"Then go to the Grey Rooster," the man said, pointing down the street. "They always need musicians there. Tell them Ren sent you."

August wanted to ask who Ren was, but the fellow had already gone, vanishing into the crowd.

The Grey Rooster. The name seemed strange, almost absurd. But he felt he had nothing to lose. He walked up to the tavern, pushed the door and went in.

Inside it was dark and noisy. People sat at the tables, drinking, talking. In the corner was a small stage, empty. August approached the counter, where the keeper — a heavyset man with a grey beard — was wiping mugs.

"I'm a bard," said August.

The keeper raised his eyes and gave him a long look.

"Ren sent you?"

August nodded.

The keeper grinned, showing yellow teeth:

"Well then, show us what you can do. If we like you — you stay."

Climbing onto the stage, he felt every eye upon him. His hands trembled slightly as he took out the guitar. His palms were damp with sweat; there was cold in his chest. A strange feeling — part fear, part something else he could not name. He did not know what to play. He did not know what to sing. He looked at the room bathed in the yellow light of oil lamps, at the unfamiliar faces — and understood: this was his first stage.

He ran his fingers across the strings. They answered softly, almost inaudibly. A voice that had not sung for many days stirred inside him, and he began.

No one applauded.

But no one left.

Someone stopped talking. Someone turned around. August did not know whether they liked what he played. He did not know what he was doing here. But he went on, until his voice ran dry, fading with the last chord into the darkness of the tavern.

When he finished, he searched the faces — waiting for a smile, a question, a gesture. Nothing happened.

But then he noticed.

In the doorway stood a girl with dark hair. Simply dressed, quiet, almost unnoticed. And she was watching him with the same quiet smile he remembered from the fireside. She said nothing. She simply looked, then turned and slipped out onto the street, leaving only the swing of the door and a faint noise.
