He did not go into the village right away.

August stopped at a small inn on the edge of town. It was old and nearly empty — just the owner, his wife, and one other lodger who slept without taking off his boots. But it was quiet here, and that was the main thing. He took a room for the night, paid with a coin earned aboard the ship, and climbed to the second floor.

The room was small, but it had a bed with clean linen, a wooden chair, and a basin for washing. August set the guitar in the corner, took off his cloak and sword, and sat on the edge of the bed. He sat staring at the wall, listening to his own heartbeat. He wanted to sleep, but he was afraid that if he closed his eyes, he would see smoke rising over Alba-Mare again, and his father on the broken spear-shaft. And that raider he had pushed overboard would look at him from the darkness — rasping, throat cut, his gaze refusing to let August go even when he tried to think of other things.

He lay like that a long time, sleepless, watching the crack that crawled across the ceiling — thin, branching, like the bed of a dried-up river. Now and then footsteps came from the street, the creak of cartwheels, a distant dog's bark. Life went on beyond the walls, and August envied that simple, untroubled bustle. He turned on his side, pulled the pillow toward him, and closed his eyes, trying to think not of the past but of what lay ahead. The village to the east. The woman from the north who was searching for her son. A man with a guitar. It could be him. And it might not be. He would not let himself believe it, because belief is the thing that hurts most to lose.

Toward morning he finally fell asleep, and no dreams came. Just darkness, deep and empty, like the bottom of a well. When he woke, dawn was already breaking outside, and in that silence, for the first time in a long while, he heard no sound of waves. Only quiet. And it frightened him more than any storm.

Then he got up and went to the bathhouse.

It stood at the end of the street — a low stone building with pipes from which steam rose. Inside, it smelled of wood, wet stone, and herbs. August took everything off, left his clothes in a basket, and stepped into the changing room.

It was crowded there — in one corner sat a family, a man with two sons who, laughing, doused each other with water from a wooden ladle. Along the far wall, several women talked among themselves, paying no mind to the men. Somewhere a baby cried, its voice blending with the hiss of steam and the splash of water. The door to the hot room stood open, and heat came from within. People came and went without shame about their nakedness — here it was natural. Like breathing.

August froze for a moment, looking at the scene. It seemed to him he was looking at something ancient — at something that had existed long before him and would exist long after. Here there was no shame, no division — only body and warmth, only cleansing and life. He entered the hot room, sat down on a wooden bench, and closed his eyes. The steam burned his skin, and he felt the muscles, clenched through weeks on the road, begin to loosen.

All around sat people — men with rough hands, women with tired faces, children playing with water, splashing in a wooden trough. The voices merged into a single warm murmur. Someone laughed out loud, someone spoke in half-tones, someone simply sat silent, as he did.

August watched them, feeling his own body — roughened, thinned, covered with scars he did not remember — stop being a stranger to him. Sweat ran down his chest, his stomach, his back, washing away salt, weariness, and the weight he carried inside.

When he came out of the hot room, he took cold water from a bucket and poured it over himself from head to foot. It ran down his shoulders, his chest, his face — washing away not only the dirt but the shadow that had settled in his gaze since the killing. Beside him stood the same man who had been laughing at the trough with his sons. He turned his head, looked at August, at the scars on his body, and gave a short nod, without asking where they came from. He simply nodded — like a man who knows the body keeps its memory and that it needs no explaining. August nodded back, feeling his breathing grow even and light.

Leaving the bathhouse, he stopped in the street and breathed deeply. The air was warm, almost sweet, and smelled like nothing in any of the towns he had passed through: flowering bushes by the roadside, dry grass warmed by the sun, and somewhere in the distance — smoke from ovens where bread was baking. He stood and breathed, and it seemed to him that with every breath something heavy left him — something he had carried since that night in the strait.

When he returned to the inn, his skin was fresh, his breathing even. He put on an old shirt — grey linen, faded and worn thin at the elbows. His trousers, brown and coarse, were rubbed through at the knees, and a thread dangled at the waist where he had sewn them up again and again. He looked at himself in a clouded mirror and realized his cloak, the old fisherman's one, heavy and smelling of smoke, was barely holding on to his shoulders. He decided: today he would renew everything he could.

He went out and made for the market.

The market was loud, motley, alive — unlike any he had seen in the north. The air smelled of spices, fried meat, honey, and something sweet and unfamiliar. He walked past stalls of fish — silvery, fresh, laid out on leaves. Past women traders with baskets full of apricots, plums, and round little fruits like small suns that he did not know.

On the way he stopped at a stall with hot food. An old woman in a patterned headscarf was frying flat breads on a stone slab, brushing them with oil and sprinkling them with herbs. The smell struck him, and he suddenly realized how long it had been since he had eaten anything but dry rations and salted meat. He bought two flat breads, hot and soft, and ate them as he walked, burning his fingers. The dough was thin, with a taste of smoke and salt, and the herbs were sharp and fresh. It was the first real food in many days, and it made his eyes sting, as if his body remembered what it meant not merely to be fed, but to eat.

He finished the bread, wiped his hands on the edge of his cloak, and went on to the stalls with cloth and clothing. An old trader with a grey beard and lively eyes looked him over and asked:

"What are you looking for, wanderer?"

"Everything," August answered. "A shirt, trousers, a cloak."

The trader took out some things. He handed him a shirt — light grey, linen, long-sleeved, with a plain collar. August ran his fingers over it: the cloth was soft and pleasant to the touch. He held it against his shoulder and knew it would fit well.

The trousers were dark, nearly black, of denser cloth than his old ones. Sewn simply and firmly, with nothing superfluous. He took them without hesitation.

He chose the cloak at length. He needed one that would not weigh on his shoulders and would shield him well from sun and wind. The trader handed him a light cloak — grey, of fine wool, with a hood that covered the neck.

"This one won't soak through in fog," he said. "And it won't let you freeze at night. And it breathes in the sun."

August draped it over his shoulders. It was light and soft, and when he fastened it he felt that this was exactly what he needed. He took out his coins, counted the right amount, and thanked the trader. The man nodded, but before August could go he called out:

"Wanderer. Are you going far?"

"East," August answered.

The trader frowned, as if recalling something.

"There's a village beyond the hills. A quiet place. I've heard people from the north have come there. Refugees. Perhaps you're looking for someone among them?"

August went still. His heart clenched again. He nodded, not trusting his voice.

"Then go in peace," the trader said simply. "The road there is no more than half a day's walk."

August thanked him and was already leaving when he noticed a basket of fruit on a neighboring stall. He came closer and saw figs — dark purple, with beads of juice showing on their skin. He took one, gently broke it open, and a smell came over him that carried him back to the days when he was still a boy. The fruit seller held out a few more, and for an instant their fingers touched.

She smiled at him — simply, without words, as though she understood he was not just buying fruit but returning to something old and warm.

Figs grew in Alba-Mare too. There was a tree by the old wall where August used to sit in summer, listening to the wind stir the leaves. His mother would sometimes bring him ripe fruit in a wooden bowl, and he would eat them sitting on the warm stones while she stood beside him and smiled, watching the juice run down his fingers. She would say: "Eat slowly. Figs are sweet when they're given time."

Now he suddenly felt something stir inside him and answer with an old, forgotten warmth. He bought several, wrapped them in a cloth, and put them in his pack.

Back at the inn, he folded his old things into a corner. They were too worn to carry any farther. He left them on the bed — perhaps the owner could pass them on to someone in need. In their place he put on the new shirt, the new trousers, and the new cloak. They were plain, almost poor, but clean and strong.

He sat on the bed and took out the guitar. Not to play — but to make sure it was all right. He ran through the strings, tightened the pegs, passed his fingers along the neck. The guitar answered evenly, purely, as if it too had rested. He put it back in its case and looked at himself in the small mirror on the wall. On his shoulders — a light cloak. At his belt — his father's sword. In his hands — a guitar.

He was ready to go on.
