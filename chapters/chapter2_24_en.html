He came out from under the awning feeling satiety spread through his body — warm, heavy, pleasant, as if the food had not merely filled his stomach but settled into every muscle, every joint, returning what the island had taken away. The sun stood high, and the square was already filled with people to its very edges: traders called to buyers, stretching out their vowels as if singing; camels stood chewing and gazing past everything with that philosophical calm found only in animals that had seen more deserts than a man would see in a lifetime; children ran between legs, crying out something bright and unintelligible, and their voices dissolved into the general hum like drops in a fountain.

August passed through the crowd, and no one touched him, jostled him, noticed him — he was a stone in a river, around which the water flows without stopping. He stopped by an old stone building with wide steps and a carved wooden door darkened by sun and time. It was a caravanserai — a place where merchants and travelers stopped, where goods were stored and camels changed. The building was old, warm, built of light stone that had drunk in the sun over many years and now seemed to give it back, even in the shade. Above the door hung a sign with Arabic script that August could not read, but he felt: this place was a crossroads, a point where roads from east, west, south, and north converged, and everyone who passed through it left something of their own here and took away something foreign.

Three steps up. The guitar on his knees. Without thoughts of what to play. Simply — to be heard.

The first chord — and the square began to fall silent.

Not at once, not entirely — like ripples on water spreading in circles from a thrown stone. First those standing nearest fell silent: a trader who cut off his "buy, the best wares, finest…" in mid-sentence and never finished; a boy with a tray of flatbreads, stopped mid-step, as if his feet had decided on their own that here he must stand. Then the silence went further, taking in new people, and each who fell silent passed that silence to his neighbor as one passes a torch in the dark.

Within a minute around the steps stood that peculiar silence that comes when many people are silent together, and in that silence there is not emptiness but attention, thick and warm.

August played. Quietly at first, almost timidly, as though testing: would the city accept his sound, would it drown it out, would it drive it away. Then the sound grew fuller, deeper, and the melody was born out of what he had seen in these days: out of the morning at the market, when the first rays touched the minarets; out of the park, where the water whispered something to old trees; out of flatbread and sauce, out of pilaf that tasted like home, though before this he had not known that home could have a taste. Light but deep, like the water in the canal he had noticed that morning. The music flowed through the crowd, flowed around people as water flows around stones, and each stone — a person frozen for a moment — felt its coolness.

People slowed their step. They stopped at the foot of the steps, sat on neighboring stones, leaned against the caravanserai wall, and on their faces was the expression August already knew: not curiosity but recognition, as if in this music each had heard something of his own, long forgotten or long sought. A young merchant with a bundle of cloth froze, forgetting his wares, and his hands, accustomed to sorting silk, hung at his sides as if they too were listening. An old woman in a dark kerchief stopped a few steps away and closed her eyes, and her lips moved as if she were praying or singing along, but no words could be heard. A soldier in dusty boots, with the weary face of a man who had walked too many miles, leaned against the wall, folded his arms on his chest, and his face smoothed as if the music had erased the weariness the way water washes away dust.

August sang in a language they did not know. The northern language, the language of snow and cliffs, the language in which Bjorn spoke of the road and Signe sang of the stars. But beyond the words they heard what was more important: not meaning, but what lay beneath meaning — pain, longing, hope, quiet joy. Music needed no translation: it translated itself, as water and wind translate, which speak every language and none.

The last chord faded. Silence hung for a moment — full, warm, like the air before dawn, when the world still sleeps but already knows it will soon wake. Then — the chime of coins on stone. One more, another. Someone laid on the step a ripe fig wrapped in a leaf — not money but a gift, and that made it warmer. August did not look at the gifts. He looked at faces: at the old woman, who opened her eyes slowly as if returning from far away; at the merchant, who took up his bundle again but now carried it differently, lighter, as if the music had lifted part of its weight from him; at the soldier, who nodded — briefly, simply, the way one nods to those one respects.

The music was becoming part of this city. It rooted into the stones, was caught by the wind and carried over the rooftops, over the minarets, over the harbor, where ships rocked on the waves and their rigging rang as if echoing the melody. How much longer he would play, he did not know. But this was his voice. And he had found those to give it to.

The sun leaned toward evening, and the light turned golden, thick as honey, and the stones of the caravanserai in that light seemed alive, warm, as though they kept within them the warmth of all the days that had passed through them. August rose. The people were dispersing, but some came up to nod or say a short word. The language was unfamiliar, but the glances were comprehensible: in them was what needs no translation — gratitude, and quiet respect, and something else that has no name but is felt when two strangers for a moment become closer than they were.

The coins gathered, the kerchief tied in a knot. The guitar pressed to his chest, his steps toward home. The evening city sounded different from the morning one: quieter, softer, as if the day was tired and had lowered its voice. Children's laughter came from the lanes, bright and distant; the little bells on camels clinked, measured and calm; the cloth awnings over the taverns stirred in the evening wind, and in their rustle was their own music, their own melody, their own silence. The day remained in fingers that still remembered the music, in a body that still kept the warmth of sun and food, in eyes that still saw the faces of people who had stopped to listen.

By the window, in a small room that smelled of wood and herbs, August took the guitar and played a new melody — the one born in the square when the first chord made the trader fall silent and stopped the boy with the flatbreads. Quiet, warm, like the air of Al-Sharan, like the stone of the caravanserai, like the evening wind that stirs the awnings and carries within it the smell of spices and the sea. A melody that tried to prove nothing, tell nothing, explain nothing — it simply was, like a day that had been lived, like an evening that comes, like a night that will come and carry everything away with it except the sound living in the wood and in the fingers, and in what lies between them — in the silence that is music itself.
