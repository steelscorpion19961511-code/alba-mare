The ship had been sailing along the southern coast for over a week. The sea was calm, the wind even, as though the element itself had decided to keep in step with them without testing their strength. August had grown used to this rhythm: the morning began with the first rays sliding over the wet planks of the deck; then — work: hauling ropes, checking knots, helping raise the sails; evening — the time of the guitar and quiet talk, when voices did not contend with the noise of the waves but merged with it; night — stars that here seemed larger and closer, as though wanting to tell something.

He already knew all the sailors by name and by habit: Jorgen grumbled in the mornings until he had drunk a mug of hot tea, and only then became a human being; Lars hummed softly when he thought no one could hear — old fishing songs, drawn-out and a little sad; young Willy, who had barely set foot on deck in this voyage, was still afraid of storms but tried not to show his fear and seized any work, if only to feel needed. August knew when they needed help and when it was better simply to stand nearby, without disturbing the silence. That silence on the ship was special — it did not press but held, the way strong rigging holds a sail in a storm.

On the eighth day the captain announced that in a few hours they would reach Windholm. A small port town — to take on water, buy fresh bread, give the crew a respite. At the sound of that name August felt something stir inside him, like an old wound reminding of itself for a moment — but without pain; rather as a sign that he still remembered. Windholm. He knew this place. He had been here once — long ago, in another life, when he was a different person: younger, more lost, with eyes in which the panic had not yet settled that the world was too large and too alien.

The shore rose out of the fog slowly, almost reluctantly, as though the city was in no hurry to reveal itself, testing whether the guest deserved to see it. And when the fog finally parted, Windholm appeared just as it had remained in August's memory: wooden piers darkened by salt and time; old lamps whose copper sides had long since grown a green patina; the cries of gulls, sharp and insistent, like a reminder that the sea gives no gifts for free; the smell of wet fish and seaweed, dense and heavy, soaking into clothing and skin. Everything was in its place. Only August himself had changed.

The ship docked at the pier. August came ashore and paused for a moment, feeling how the boards gave slightly under his boots — that familiar, almost forgotten response of wood underfoot was like a handshake from an old friend. The wind was damp and piercing; it crept under the cloak, stirred his hair, smelled of iodine and salt. Gulls cried behind him, barrels boomed somewhere close, someone argued loudly over the price of fish, and that noise, so ordinary and yet so dear, wrapped around him like a warm blanket after long cold.

The crew scattered through the town: some hurried to the shops, some to the tavern, some simply wanted to walk on solid ground and feel its stillness. And August went along the familiar lanes, studying the houses he had once passed by. The stones of the pavement were the same — grey, worn by the soles of hundreds of feet; the shutters on the windows the same — only now one of them hung from a single hinge and creaked softly in the wind; even the smell was the same: a mixture of smoke, fish, spices and dampness that never leaves a coastal town.

That day came back by itself, without effort, as though someone quietly opened a door into the past. Then he had set foot on this shore for the first time, come ashore from the Northern Dawn, and the ground beneath him had seemed motionless, almost alien, as though he had forgotten how to walk on land. He had been young, lost, had not known where to go. He had walked these streets with a bag holding only bread and cheese, had looked at the fishing nets drying in the wind, heavy and grey as storm clouds. He had stopped at the market, where it smelled of seaweed and dried fish, had bought bread and cheese — and his hand had found the right weight in the purse by habit, as though the body remembered how to be an ordinary person.

And then he had seen an old man in a faded cloak, sitting by the water with a long stick — a staff or a fishing rod, one could not tell. August had sat down beside him without asking permission, simply because he did not know what else to do. They had looked at the sea in silence. The waves rolled onto the pebbles and rolled back, as though counting something important, and the man, without looking at August, had said: "Do not seek — the road will find you by itself." And those words had then sounded like a verdict, like a renunciation of hope. But now August understood that there had been no despair in them, but wisdom: sometimes one must simply sit and watch the water, and then the world itself will show where to go.

Now August sat in the same place. He looked at the grey, heavy water. It had none of the azure transparency he remembered from the southern seas of his childhood; here the sea did not smile. It breathed evenly and heavily, like an old beast not used to pitying anyone but wishing no evil either — simply living its own life, vast and indifferent. The wind, damp and piercing, stirred his hair, and August shivered and pulled his cloak tighter. Somewhere behind him sailcloth flapped, a chain clanged against the hull, voices called to one another, and these sounds were like an anchor, keeping him from drifting too far into memories.

August sat and looked at the water, and in his chest it was quiet and calm. Then he had been lost. Now he knew where he was going. That knowledge did not make the road easier — on the contrary, it added weight to every step, because now he answered not only for himself but for his choice. But it made the road comprehensible. And in that there was its own strength.

The sun was sinking toward its setting, staining the sky in dull shades of gold and ash, when August returned to the ship. Matteo waited by the side, leaning on the rail, looking now at the town, now at the sky, as though trying to read something important in it. Seeing August, he raised an eyebrow slightly.

"Have you been here before?" he asked calmly, without curiosity — simply to know.

"Yes," August answered. "Long ago. Then I was only beginning my road. Or thought I was beginning. In truth, I was simply running away."

Matteo nodded, as though that were the most ordinary confession he had heard a thousand times.

"And now do you know where you are going?" he asked, looking straight at him, without condescension and without judgment.

"Now — yes," said August confidently, and in that "yes" there was no bravado, only firmness tested on the road. "And that is the main thing."

"A good place?" asked Matteo, turning his gaze again to the town, where lights were already being lit, reflected in the dark water in trembling golden paths.

August looked at Windholm, at its old houses, at the narrow lanes climbing upward, at the lamps that would soon burn all night, guarding the town's sleep.

"A quiet one," he said. "And wise. It does not shout about its strength, but you feel it in every stone, in every creak of the boards. It has seen so many stories that it has learned to keep silent and simply hold them within itself."

They stood together, watching evening Windholm. Tomorrow they would put out to sea again, and the town would remain astern, another point on the map of his road. For now one could simply stand and watch the lights reflect in the dark water, as night slowly drew its soft cover over the shore. Somewhere a chain clanged, somewhere sailors returning from the shore laughed, and that laughter was warm, alive, real.

August listened to those sounds and felt how this small, stern town became part of his memory. Not as a place where he had been, but as a place that remembered him. As though the stones of the pavement, the boards of the pier and even the wind walking the streets whispered: "We saw how you came here lost. We see how you leave, knowing. And we will remember both of those men."

And in that there was something consoling: not all roads end where they begin, but all of them leave traces. And Windholm was one of those places where his trace remained not on the earth but in the heart.
