On the tenth day of his stay in Old Bridge, August entered the tavern for the first time.

He stood by the door, feeling the cold air touch his back and the warm air his face. He did not know whether he would enter this circle of strangers, whether he would become part of their quiet. The boundary between the street and the hall seemed almost tangible: here, beyond the threshold, lived their own habits, their own voices, their own stories, and he was a guest among them. But he drew a deep breath, as if drawing courage from the very cold, pushed the door open, and stepped over the threshold.

The tavern was called 'By the Old Bridge' — plainly, without flourish, like everything in this village. Inside it was cramped and warm, the air thick with smells and conversation. In the middle of the hall a fireplace hummed, with an iron cauldron hanging above it; from time to time the hostess came over and stirred it with a long wooden spoon. It smelled of stewed meat with fragrant herbs, fresh bread, and hops. Local folk sat at the tables — they talked in low voices, sometimes laughed, but not loudly, with restraint, as if even laughter here did not disturb the general calm, did not trouble the winter silence.

August sat in a corner where the light from the fireplace fell in soft patches, and ordered a mug of warm ale and a bowl of stew. He was in no hurry. He simply sat and listened, taking in the sounds of the place: somewhere beyond the wall firewood crackled, somewhere a woman spoke quietly with the innkeeper, a floorboard creaked, someone gave a soft chuckle at a joke. Everything was simple as breathing, and this simplicity, unexpectedly, was soothing.

He had nearly finished eating when the tavern door opened and let in a gust of cold that ran along the floor and for a moment mixed the smells of street and warmth. An old man came in — tall, slightly stooped, with a thick gray beard and eyes that regarded the world with a warm, faintly wry wisdom. He carried a staff in his hands, but leaned on it more from habit than necessity. His hands rested on the staff — rough, with dark traces of veins, with the calluses that remain on those who have held an oar or an axe all their lives.

He surveyed the hall, habitually taking stock of who was there, and noticed August. For a moment his gaze rested on the unfamiliar face; then, smiling to himself, he approached the table.

'Is this seat free?'

August nodded. The old man sat down across from him, ordered a mug of warm beer, and was silent for a long while, studying him. When the mug came, he drank a little, closing his eyes briefly as if listening to the taste, and said:

'You're not from here.'

'No,' August answered. 'I'm from the south.'

'I guessed as much,' the old man said with a smile. 'You have the look of someone seeing real cold for the first time. Not the kind that nips your cheeks, but the kind that slips under the skin and makes you look at the road differently. I was like that myself once. From southern parts.'

August raised his head and looked at him more closely. There was something dependable in this old man, like in old trees that have lived through many winters.

'And now you live here?'

'I do,' the old man answered. 'For many years now. My name is Heimrir. I know these places, I know the roads, the winds, and the people who travel them. And you, I hear, are being treated in Old Bridge. At Elsa's.'

'How do you know?' August asked, surprised.

'In a small village everyone knows everything,' Heimrir said, and a spark flickered in his eyes, a kind and slightly sly one. 'Elsa said you are stubborn, and that a long road awaits you. Is that true?'

August nodded.

'I want to go north. To the Land of the Iron Cliffs.'

Heimrir looked at him thoughtfully and, leaning slightly forward, said quietly:

'Then what you need is not merely someone who knows the road. You need someone who knows this land. And who can explain it to you.'

He drank a little more beer and looked into the fire, as if gathering his thoughts, choosing his words so that they would lie evenly and not crumble.

'I have been there. More than once. And I will tell you this: it is unlike anything you have seen. There are no kings there, no cities. People live in communities there. Clan communities, harsh ones, surviving on bare rocks by a cold sea. They know no other way of life, and they count as their strength what southerners call cruelty. But in truth it is simply survival. It is not malice, but the habit of guarding every crumb of warmth and strength.'

'I have heard that they make raids,' said August. 'That they plunder and kill.'

'They raid, yes,' Heimrir did not deny it. 'Sometimes southward, sometimes into neighboring lands. But they are not brigands in the sense you mean. Where the land yields no harvest and winter lasts almost half the year, the only way to survive is to take what your own land does not give. For them it is not villainy. It is necessity, heavy as a stone on the shoulders.'

August listened in silence. It seemed to him that a world was opening before him that he could not previously have even imagined — a world where kindness and cruelty are woven together so tightly that they are hard to separate.

'If you go there,' Heimrir went on, 'you will face colds that spare no one. Blizzards in which you can see only a step ahead, and the wind screams so that you cannot hear your own thoughts. People who do not smile at strangers, because a smile is also warmth, and warmth is saved for one's own. But if you can pass through all this, you will see a land that forgives no weakness, yet gives strength to those who endure.'

He raised his mug and looked at August over its rim.

'But first you must recover. And prepare for what awaits. Winter here begins early, and it does not love those who are not ready.'

'I am ready,' said August, and in that 'ready' there was more hope than certainty.

Heimrir smiled, as if he had heard exactly what he expected:

'Then perhaps you will play for us this evening? They say you have a guitar and songs. We rarely hear a true southern wind around here.'

August smiled in return and took up the guitar, feeling a light warmth spreading through his chest, like the warmth that came from the fireplace.

That evening the tavern filled with music. He did not play for pay, nor for glory — he played to share with them what he had. Songs born on the road, about the sea, about the journey, about the people he had met. People listened; some joined in the singing, some watched the fire in silence, and it seemed that this small hall, lost among snows and hills, grew a little warmer for a moment, as if the music were holding the cold outside the walls.

When he finished, one of the women — young, with dark hair braided in a tight plait and light, attentive eyes — came over to him. She stopped beside him, and he felt the warmth of her breath before she spoke. She laid her hand on his hand, still resting on the neck of the guitar, and he started at the unexpectedness — her fingers were warm and dry, with a slight roughness, like those of a person used to work.

'You play as if you knew what winter is,' she said quietly.

August looked at her. In her gaze there was neither challenge nor invitation — only a quiet, calm curiosity, a wish to understand something important.

'I am only learning,' he answered.

She smiled, withdrew her hand, and without looking back dissolved into the half-dark of the tavern. August remained sitting by the wall, feeling on his skin the trace of her fingers — warm and light, like the memory of something that might have happened, but did not.

He sat at the edge of the stage, watching the people make their way home, and felt that for the first time in a long while his music was not merely a voice — it was a shared breath that needed no words. And this feeling stayed with him, warming him even when the hall had emptied, the fire in the hearth had grown quieter, and the cold had once more announced itself beyond the tavern walls.
