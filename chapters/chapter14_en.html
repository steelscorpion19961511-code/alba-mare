The ship reached open sea on the third day.

The shore vanished, and nothing remained but water — grey, calm, endless. August stood on the forecastle, searching the line of the horizon, and for the first time in a long while did not know what to look for. The wind swept his face, and it seemed to him that he was sailing not on a ship but inside that wind.

The Northern Dawn was cramped, but August quickly found his place. He helped the crew: hauling sacks, scrubbing the deck, mending rigging. No one asked him needless questions. The sailors were simple folk, used to strangers who appeared for one voyage and disappeared. August made no attempt to grow close to anyone, but he felt how common work made them one crew. He knew that aboard a ship everyone must be useful, or they would not take him on the next time.

But at night he was alone.

When the crew went below to the forepeak, and the captain shut himself in his cabin, August sat on the forecastle and watched the sea. Black water, sparse stars, the noise of the waves. He took out the guitar and moved his fingers over the strings, listening to their sound melt into the night. He did not play for anyone. He simply listened to how the guitar answered the motion of the ship.

In the mornings, when the August night gave way to a cool morning, August touched the pegs first of all. The gut strings — soft, pliant, so sensitive to any dampness — hummed in unison with the morning fog. He already knew how to read their mood: humid air made them stretch lazily, and the tension had to be adjusted, listening to how each string answered his fingers. When he played of the sea, the strings answered him with a deep, velvet sound, as if they too belonged to that shore.

In such mornings he almost forgot he was sailing anywhere. It seemed to him that he had stayed in place, and the water moved beneath him.

The captain sometimes came up to him. He said not a word — he simply stood beside him, watching the water and listening. Once he asked:

"Do you always play like that?"

"Only when I'm alone," August answered.

The captain nodded and walked away. But after that conversation he left him a little more bread, and more often let him sit on the forecastle late.

On the fifth day of the voyage August noticed that the crew had begun to treat him differently. He was used to their silent attention when they saw him at work. But now they came up to him in the evenings, sat down beside him and listened to his playing. He did not know how to respond to it. He simply played — of the sea, of the wind, of what he had seen in his life.

Once he played a melody he had composed in the cove, the day before the fire. It was quiet and sad. There were no words in it, but there was something that made his listeners freeze.

One of the sailors, an old mariner with a weathered face, came to him afterward and said:

"You're playing about something that matters. I don't know what — but I hear it."

August looked at him and said nothing. It seemed to him that the sea was still speaking to him — only now it was the voice he heard in the silence, when his hand touched the strings.

Toward the end of the week, as the ship ran along a rocky shore, August suddenly felt almost at peace.

He lay on the forecastle, watching the sky, and thought that his life, whatever it had been before, now belonged to this ship. And it seemed to him that the sea no longer frightened him. Ahead, beyond the horizon, a new shore awaited — and a new meeting.

And then came the thing that finally made him part of the crew.

In the middle of the sixth night the wind rose — not a storm, but strong and gusty, so that the deck swayed underfoot and the rigging sang in different voices. August woke because the guitar nearly slid from under his hand: it rocked with him. He got up, threw on his cloak and came out on deck.

Sailors were already bustling there, lashing down everything that could tear loose. The captain stood at the helm, calm and firm, as if he himself were part of the ship. Seeing August, he called out shortly:

"Hold this line until we've made it fast. And hold it tight!"

August seized the wet rope, feeling it tremble under strain like a living string. He pulled, braced his feet against the slippery boards, and in this simple, heavy labor suddenly felt what he felt in music: a rhythm that must be held. Not for beauty, but so that everything stays whole.

When the wind eased a little and the sky began to grey, the captain came up to him and nodded:

"Thank you. You didn't lose your head. That matters."

August only shrugged, but inside him something fell into place. Now he was not merely a companion allowed aboard. He was one who holds the line.

After that the sailors began to call him by name more often, instead of hailing him: "Hey, musician!" One evening, as August sat again on the forecastle, that same old mariner came and sat beside him. In silence he held out a piece of dried cod and a mug of hot tea, smelling of thyme and smoke.

"This is for you," he said simply. "We don't care for many words here. But if you play and we listen — then we are together."

August nodded, taking both the food and these quiet words. He bit into the fish, drank the tea, and unhurriedly took out the guitar. This time he did not seek a special melody. He simply played a few simple chords, as if tuning not the instrument but something within himself.

The sailor listened with his head slightly bowed, nodding now and then. And then he added quietly:

"You know, we have a song in the north. They sing it when setting out on a long voyage. I don't know whether it will suit you... but in it they sing of how the sea remembers everyone who stepped upon its waves."

And he sang a few lines quietly, almost in a whisper. His voice was hoarse, worn by the winds, but there was such an ancient, calm strength in it that August froze, afraid to scare away the moment.

When the mariner finished, August quietly repeated the tune, fitting his fingers to it, and added to it a shade of his own — the one that had lived in him since childhood, since his father taught him to listen to the silence between the sounds.

They played that song together — unpolished, quiet, but as if this were the whole truth: not in perfection, but in two people hearing each other amid the noise of wind and waves.

From that night on, August noticed more and more how the sailors slowed their step as they passed, if they heard him playing. Sometimes someone would stop, sit down beside him, and in those minutes the silence was no longer empty but full.

And one morning, when the sun had barely risen over the water and gilded the sails, the captain came to August and said:

"We'll put in at a small port in two days. There we can take on supplies and give the crew rest. If you like, you may go ashore."

August raised his eyes as if hearing these words for the first time. The chance to go ashore. To return to his search. But he looked around — at the deck, at the sailors, at the captain standing with his hands on his hips, waiting for an answer. And suddenly he understood that for the first time in a long while he did not want to run anywhere.

"I'll stay," he said quietly. "For as long as you hold this course."

The captain smiled faintly — the way people smile who know the worth of simple, firm words.

"Good," he nodded. "Then get ready: ahead lies a stretch of strong winds. We'll need every hand."

August nodded. He felt that in his chest, where only longing and haste had lived before, something new had settled. Not peace — no. But certainty: he was in his place. If only for a time. If only for this voyage.

The days went by, and the sea changed. Now it grew smooth as glass, and it seemed the ship sailed across a reflection of the sky. Now it heaved in waves, the deck swayed wildly, and the wind shouted so loud it drowned out any words. But August had already learned to tell the voices of the sea apart. He heard how it whispered when it grew weary, and how it roared when it raged. And in each of these voices he found something of his own.

In the evenings he still sat on the forecastle. The guitar lay on his knees, and his fingers found the familiar chords on their own. But now he sometimes paused and listened not to himself, but to what sounded around him: the creak of the masts, the lapping of water against the sides, the distant cries of gulls. And it seemed to him that this music itself — the noise and the silence, the wind and the waves — was asking to enter his songs.

He did not know whether this road would lead him to his mother. He did not know whether he would ever see Alba-Mare again. But now, on this old ship, among these silent, sturdy people, he felt for the first time in a long while that he was not lost. He was sailing. He held his course. And even if ahead lay only the unknown, he was ready to meet it.
