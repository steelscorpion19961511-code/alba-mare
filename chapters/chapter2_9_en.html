In the morning August woke from the light slipping through the cracks in the shutters. He lay a while, listening to himself: the body, at last, was resting, as if for the first time in long months it had shed the weight of the road. The hammam had done its work — the muscles had grown soft, the breathing even, and in his chest there appeared a rare sensation of peace, almost forgotten.

He gathered his things, went downstairs and ordered breakfast. The tavern owner silently set before him a plate of stewed vegetables, a hot flatbread and a mug of tea — astringent and spiced, with a smoky tang. August ate unhurriedly — not for the sake of fullness, but for the rhythm itself: a swallow, a bite, a pause. He listened to the voices around him. At the neighboring tables people spoke several languages, and fragments of phrases reached him — about prices, goods, distant roads. In that noise there was something reassuring: the world went on turning, and in it there was always a place for a traveler who simply wanted to eat and gather his thoughts.

After breakfast he went out into the street. Shaan-Tar lived its ordinary life — noisy, colorful, spiced. The air was thick with the smells of spices, fried dough and dust. August wandered the market, looking over the stalls: fabrics that shimmered as if the silk had drunk in the sunsets; spices heaped in neat cones that made the nose tingle; ceramics with fine painting; metalwork heavy and cold in appearance, yet with delicate chasing. Everything was foreign but beautiful — and in that foreign beauty he suddenly felt a little less lost.

He found a weapons trader's shop — small, cramped, smelling of oil and metal. The trader, a stocky man with narrow slitted eyes, sat by the entrance cleaning a dagger, running a finger along the blade as if he were testing not its sharpness but the memory of the metal. August laid before him the trophy blades and knives — those he had taken after the skirmish in the desert.

The trader examined the weapons without haste, as if reading in the nicks and scratches a whole story. He weighed the blades in his hands, grunted without raising his eyes, and named a price — without haggling, calmly, like a man who knows what steel is worth, and what the weariness of the one who brought it is worth. August did not argue. He took the coins, put them in his pouch and was about to leave when the trader called out:

“You're not local. Where from?”

“From the North,” said August.

“Then you ought to know,” the trader nodded toward the east. “A day's walk from here there is a port. A big one. Ships sail along the shore, far to the south. If you want to leave — leave. There is nothing for you here.”

August inclined his head slightly in gratitude. In those words there was neither threat nor pity — only the plain practicality of a man who had seen hundreds of travelers and knew which of them stay and which leave because they cannot breathe this air.

He went out into the streets again and questioned several more people — some answered in broken phrases, some turned away, some simply shrugged. But from the fragments, the glances, the gestures he understood the main thing: the port was alive, ships sailed often, and there hands were always needed. He did not need to know the names of the ships or the captains. He needed the sea.

Water. The ocean. Waves. The smell of salt.

The feeling rose from within — quiet but stubborn, like a tide that asks no permission but simply comes. August had not seen the sea for a long time, had not heard gulls crying over the piers. The last time he had stood on a northern shore was when he left the North. Since then there had been deserts, mountains, dust and foreign cities. And now — the sea again, quite close.

He spent the rest of his coins on provisions: hardtack, dried meat, water, a couple of flatbreads. Everything simple, dependable, things that would not spoil on the road. Then he left Shaan-Tar through the eastern gates and strode toward the sun.

The wind that blew against him was warm, but the salt could already be felt in it — light, barely perceptible, like a promise. August walked, and with every step the promise grew a little louder. He did not know what awaited him in the port, did not know on which ship he would sail or where the road would lead afterward. But for the first time in a long while he walked not because he needed to flee or to hide, but because ahead of him lay the thing he wanted.
