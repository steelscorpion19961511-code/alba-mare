The city met him with noise.

August entered through the southern gate, and at once he was enveloped in the smell of fried meat, fresh bread, flowers, and horse dung. It all mingled into one thick, living air that made his head swim. He stopped for a moment, feeling that this city — unlike all the previous ones — did not frighten him, but rather absorbed him, let him in. Or, at the very least, paid him no notice.

The narrow streets were full of people — they walked, hurried, stood, laughed, argued, haggled. Somewhere music was playing, somewhere a street vendor was crying his wares, somewhere a horse stamped its hoof on the stones. The houses were tall, with tiled roofs, and a line with drying laundry was strung between them. Jasmine bushes bloomed along the walls, their scent mixing with the aroma of smoke and spices.

August walked slowly along the pavement, drinking in this chaos, studying faces, memorizing the colors of houses, the patterns on shutters, the curved lamps over doorways. The city was alive and enormous. It did not seem to him merely a place where people lived — it was like a great, noisy organism that breathed, moved, and spoke in dozens of voices.

He passed a market where heaps of fruit, fish gleaming in the sun, and great rounds of cheese lay on the counters. The clink of coins, children's laughter, the murmur of voices — it all merged into a continuous current that caught him up and carried him onward. Carts with horses rolled past, leaving clouds of dust and a faint smell of leather behind.

He walked until he found himself in a great square.

It was spacious, paved with light-grey cobbles that had warmed through the day and now gave back their heat. In the center of the square stood a large stone fountain — water streamed from a bowl wrapped in carved stone fish and fell with a quiet, soothing sound. People sat around the fountain, talking, reading; some dozed. The square was full of people but not crowded — there was room for everyone.

August walked around the square, choosing his place. He needed the sound not to get lost between the houses, but not to strike into emptiness either. He stopped by the fountain — where the noise of the water created a light echo that deepened the voice and gave the sound more body. He took off the guitar, set the case on the cobbles beside him, and opened it.

The people around paid him no attention — they were busy with their affairs, their conversations, their shopping. But August was not waiting for attention. He sat on the rim of the fountain, laid the guitar on his knees, and closed his eyes for a moment, listening to how the water fell into the bowl, how the wind touched his face.

Then he began to play.

Softly at first, testing the strings, listening to how the sound settled over the square, how the wind carried it aside. But gradually he fell into the rhythm and began to sing. It was a song of the sea, of white walls, of how a ship goes into the fog. The melody was soft and lingering, like the waves he knew so well.

For the first several minutes no one stopped. But then one person lingered, listened, dropped a coin into the case, and went on. Then another. Within minutes a handful of people had gathered around August. They stood, not crowding one another, and listened. Someone closed their eyes, someone held a child by the hand, someone simply fell into thought.

One woman with a child in her arms stopped at the very edge of the case. The child, who had been fretting, suddenly went still when August began to play. The woman looked at him for a long moment and nodded quietly — as if she understood more than she could say. She did not drop a coin. She simply stood and listened, rocking the child, and left only when August finished the song.

He played a long time, nearly two hours. He played everything that had gathered in him over these years: songs of the road, of meetings, of partings, of silence, and of what it means to be alive. His voice grew tired, but he did not stop. It seemed to him he was not playing for money — he was playing to tell this city: "I exist. I am here. And I have something to tell you."

When he finished, the square was no longer so loud. The sun was leaning toward sunset, and people were making their way home. August crouched down, carefully took the guitar off his shoulder and laid it on the cobbles beside him. He undid the strap of the case, turned it over, and shook out onto the ground what had gathered in it over the evening. The coins poured out with a muffled ring — copper, silver, a few small coppers. He counted them, feeling a pleasant weight settle into his palm.

There was more money than he had expected. Enough to take a room, eat a full supper, and even set a little aside for the road.

He swept the coins into a leather purse at his belt, shook the case out thoroughly so that not a single coin remained, and only then carefully laid the guitar inside. He cinched the strap and lifted it to his shoulder. The fatigue no longer seemed so heavy. Something warm was spreading inside him — something he had not felt for a long time.

He walked across the city, feeling fatigue spread through his legs, and at the same time feeling a warmth spreading inside him that he had not known in ages. It was a strange sensation: he was at once empty and full. Tired and alive.

August took a room in a small inn not far from the square, where they made up a clean bed for him and offered a warm supper. He ate it, feeling the food restore his strength. But his body still remembered the road — the tension that had not let go of him for weeks. He recalled the bathhouse in the port town, that close, warm place full of voices and steam, and understood that he needed to feel that warmth again — but this time differently.

The innkeeper told him where the best bathhouse in Vistaria stood.

"The Imperial Baths," he said with respect. "Only those who can afford it go there. But if you want to wash the road off yourself — you'll find no better place."

August nodded and went out into the street. He walked through the night city, and in the lamplight the vistaria on the walls seemed even more alive — the lilac clusters swaying in the wind as if breathing with the city.

The Imperial Baths stood at the end of a broad street clad in stone and light. It was a majestic building of white stone, with tall columns and bronze doors carved with ornament. Inside, August was met by a scent — not simply herbs and steam, but something refined, almost sweet. Clean floors of warm tile, the soft light of oil lamps set into niches.

He paid at the entrance and went in.

There was none of the crowding and clamor of provincial bathhouses here. Silence ruled here — broken only by the murmur of water and rare footsteps on stone. A great hall with marble basins filled with clear, faintly steaming water. Along the walls — wooden benches draped in white sheets. In a corner — a masseur with strong, warm hands, working on someone in the half-dark.

August undressed and, leaving his clothes in a stone niche, entered the hot room.

The steam was thick, almost impenetrable. He sat on a wooden bench, feeling the heat sink into his muscles, feeling it release what had been held inside him for months. He closed his eyes, allowing himself simply to be.

And then his gaze fell on a woman a few meters away.

She lay on a wooden bench in the hot room — on her back, unclad, serene. Her chest rose and fell slowly with her breathing — deep, calm, like waves rolling in and drawing away. The warm steam clung to her body, leaving beads of moisture on her skin. They ran down her face, her neck, her collarbones, lingering in the curves of her body before sliding onto the wood.

She did not look at him. Her eyes were closed, and she seemed part of this place — like a statue come alive in the steam. August let his gaze linger on her for a moment, feeling warmth rise not only from the stones. It was not desire — it was admiration. Beauty without pretense, without play. Simply a body, free and alive, like everything he had once known in Alba-Mare.

But almost at once he looked away. He did not want to disturb that stillness with either a thought or a movement. He closed his eyes again and tried to return to the calm he had begun to feel even before he noticed her. And in that effort — in not looking, in not clinging to the image — he suddenly felt the tension sink even deeper, as if washed away not only by the dust of the road but by the weight of chance impressions.

When he came out of the hot room, he took cold water from a basin and poured it over himself from head to foot. It ran down his shoulders, his chest, his face — washing away not only the sweat but the light heaviness that that glance had left. He dried himself and went through to the hall with the basins, where he sank into the hot water up to his shoulders, closed his eyes, and let himself simply dissolve into the warmth.

He lay in the water a long time, looking at the vaulted ceiling where the glimmer of oil lamps danced in the half-dark. It seemed to him that he could hear the voice of Alba-Mare — not as a cry but as a whisper that had followed him unremittingly. But here, in this vast hall, in this city of flowers, the voice sounded different. Calmer. Almost like a farewell he had come to accept.

While he lay there, thoughts came and went like light waves. He remembered his mother on the porch, sorting herbs. He remembered the carter who had never become a bard but had learned to listen. And suddenly he understood: his path lay not in forgetting all that had been, but in carrying it with him, without letting its weight break him.

When he stepped out of the Imperial Baths, the night air of Vistaria felt cool and fresh. His skin was clean, his breathing light. August returned to his room, undressed, and lay down on the bed, feeling his body finally let go of its tension. He had done something important. Not for anyone — for himself. He understood that he could live by this — his playing, his voice. Closing his eyes, he felt the weight he had carried in himself for months, and even for long years, slowly loosen its hold. For the first time in a long while, he fell asleep without dreams.
