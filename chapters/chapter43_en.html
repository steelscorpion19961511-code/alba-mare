He left at dawn. The snow still lay in a dense layer, covering the earth like an old, reliable blanket, but in the air one could already feel the breathing of spring — light, barely catchable, like a promise that does not yet dare sound aloud. The sun rose higher than in winter, and its rays, not yet hot, already slid over the roofs, making the rime melt and trickle away in thin streams.

August stood at Signe's house, adjusting the straps of his rucksack. Inside lay provisions, a notebook of songs, the elder's manuscript and a little dried meat for the road. The guitar was on his shoulder, his father's sword at his belt. Everything he had brought with him from Alba-Mare, and everything he had gained here, was with him. It was not a heavy burden — it was his world, gathered into one sack.

He paused for a moment and looked at his hands. They were rough now, calloused, with thin scars from ropes and ice. These were not the hands he remembered when he fled Alba-Mare. Then they had been smooth, almost without marks of work, as if they did not yet know what real labour was. Now they told a story — of cold, of patience, of how he had learned to survive, step by step, knot by knot. He clenched them into a fist and opened them again. They no longer trembled. They were ready.

Signe came out onto the porch, adjusted her kerchief and looked at him for a long time. She did not weep, but in her eyes was that quiet heaviness belonging to those who know they are parting for a long time — perhaps forever.

“Will you return?” she asked in her own tongue.

August looked at her, at the snow-covered roofs, at the mountains that were already beginning to darken on the horizon, baring grey rock where the snow settled and gave way beneath the sun. He did not know the answer. And he did not want to lie.

“I don't know,” he said. “But I will remember this house.”

She nodded, as if she had been waiting for exactly these words — not a promise but the acknowledgment that this place would stay with him even if he left. Then, after a pause, she held out to him a small leather pouch tied with a cord. August took it, and for a moment she covered his fingers with her own — warm, dry, with the same roughness as his own. Her touch was brief, but there was more in it than in any words.

“This is soil from your doorstep,” she said. “So you will know where you left your mark.”

He slipped the pouch into the inner pocket of his cloak, next to his heart, and for a moment held his breath, memorizing the warmth of her fingers.

He walked through the whole settlement. People came out of their houses, stood silently at their gates, some raised a hand in farewell. He recognized their faces — those with whom he had shared bread, those who had patiently corrected his knots, those who had nodded silently when at last he did something right. The children, who had once watched him with curiosity, now stood beside their parents and watched him with respect, as one who is going on a long journey. He walked through the settlement, feeling their gazes upon him. They said not a word — they simply watched him go. He knew that this day would remain with him forever, as would their silent blessing.

At the last house he met Bjorn. The old hunter stood leaning on his staff and watched him with the same silent firmness with which they had gone into the mountains several months before. August stopped opposite him. They did not embrace, did not clap each other on the shoulders — there was no need. In their silence there was something greater than friendship: respect forged by cold and road, a quiet that speaks louder than any words.

“You survived,” Bjorn said. “Now you know how this world breathes.”

“Thanks to you,” August answered.

Bjorn nodded, then said:

“You are leaving not as a stranger. You are leaving as one who has understood. Go, south wind. Carry us with you.”

August looked at him, at the mountains, at the sea that was already beginning to darken, as if waking from a long winter's silence. It seemed to him that all of this would remain with him forever — not as a memory but as a part of himself.

He slung the guitar over his shoulder, adjusted the rucksack and stepped onto the road leading south. The snow crunched under his feet, and his tracks stretched away like a thread binding him to this place. Somewhere behind him the wind carried a voice — not loud, but clear. Someone said:

“August Fjord…”

He did not turn around. But he felt the name settle into him like a seed into soil — warm and cold at once. His. Foreign. His own.

He walked on, repeating the words to himself: “August Fjord.” They were new, yet no longer foreign. He did not yet know what they meant to others, but he knew that this was now his northern name. Behind him lay the winter; ahead, spring was beginning — and he was ready to meet it.
