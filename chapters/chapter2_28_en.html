On the following morning August came to Matteo earlier than usual. The sun was only just rising over the roofs of Al-Sharan, gilding them a pale gold, and the house was quiet — that special morning quiet when the city has not yet woken but only stretches, tasting the air. Matteo sat at the table with a mug of tea, sorting through some papers, rustling the parchment so softly that the sound was almost music. When August came in, Matteo looked up — and understood at once: something had changed. Not in the room, not in the city, but in August himself. In his walk, in the way he held his shoulders, in the way he looked straight ahead, without his habitual wariness.

Matteo set the papers aside and leaned back in his chair, watching him expectantly. In that gaze there was no question, but there was expectation — like that of a man who has long known that one day this conversation would come.

August sat down opposite him. He was silent for a moment. Gathered his thoughts, trying to fit into words what lay churning inside, like a heavy stone that had to be lifted so it would not stand in the way of breathing. Then he said it straight:

“Did you know that the North is divided?”

Matteo was not surprised. He slowly set the mug on the table, ran his palm over his grey beard as though to shake from it not dust but time, and sighed — not heavily, but like a man who has finally received what he had long thought about and never said.

“I knew. I thought you knew too.”

“I knew only what I had seen,” August said, and his voice came out a little duller than usual, as if he were ashamed of that ignorance. “The eastern northerners. Their language, their ways. I thought they were all like that. I thought the North was one world.”

“No,” Matteo answered calmly. “The North is two lands, divided by a ridge. On one side — the eastern ones; on the other — the western ones. They almost never speak to each other. The ridge is too high, too cold. No one can cross it. In winter the snow lies in layers, like the pages of a book, and no one can turn them. In summer the stones weep from the heat, and in winter — from the cold. It is a wall that cannot be broken and cannot be gone around.”

He took a sip of tea, warming his throat, and went on:

“The eastern northerners are open. They are used to outsiders. They trade, they know our language. Sometimes they come to the southern villages, buy grain and iron, pay with furs and amber. They are not feared. But the western ones are different. They live closed off, they trust no one. Their land is poor, the winters are long, and the summer is short as a breath. They take what they can carry when hunger presses them against the wall. It is not malice. It is necessity.”

August was silent, feeling how Matteo's words settled into his memory like stones into an old wall: even, tight, forever. Each stone — a new piece of knowledge, a new facet of what he had thought simple.

“Have you been there?” he asked quietly.

Matteo looked at him for a long moment, then turned his eyes to the window, to the morning light that filled the room and turned every mote of dust to gold. As though searching in that light for something he had lost long ago.

“I have. Once. It was long ago, before I met you in Alba-Mare. I was younger then, bolder, and it seemed to me there was no land that could not be won over with trade. I had good goods — things they needed: grain, dried fruit, iron tools. They needed them desperately. I thought they would see me not as an enemy but as one who could help them.”

August waited, not interrupting, not hurrying him. He knew that such stories could not be told quickly.

“They would not engage,” Matteo went on, and in his voice there was not resentment but the weariness of a man who once made a mistake and remembered it all his life. “They came down to the shore with spears, silent, without a single word. No shouts, no threats, not even a glance one could call hostile. They simply stood and watched. I tried to speak with them, to show my goods, to smile. I spoke their language as best I could, tried to show that I had come in peace. But they looked at me as though I had come not with peace but with war. As though my very kindness were suspicious to them. I went away with nothing. And I understood that these people live in another world. In a world where trust is not the beginning but the end. Where it costs too much.”

“Why did you not try again?” asked August.

“Because I understood: they do not want to trade. They want to survive. And when they have no other way out, they come south and take what they need. It is not malice. It is necessity. And if they do not want to speak — then there is nothing to speak about.”

August looked at his hands, lying on the table. At the calluses, the scars, the lines that formed a pattern only he could read. He felt something rising in him that he had tried to stifle in Al-Sharan: not anger, not pity, but understanding — heavy and bitter as wormwood.

“Do you think they might accept me?” he asked quietly, almost in a whisper, as though afraid to frighten off hope.

Matteo smiled, but not mockingly — with the warm irony with which elders look at the young when those want to do the impossible.

“You know their language. You lived by their laws, ate their food, slept under their roofs. You are not a southerner who came for profit. You are a Fjord. A name the wind gave you. That means more than any trade. For them a name is not a word. It is a track. And your track they will recognize.”

August sat motionless. He felt Matteo's words settle into him like seeds into the earth: quietly, imperceptibly, but deep. And somewhere inside, something was already sprouting that would soon become a decision.

“I will go there,” he said quietly, but firmly, so that even the silence in the room seemed to nod in answer.

Matteo looked at him with a long, warm gaze. Then nodded, as though he had been waiting for these words from the very beginning, as though he knew that August could not stay while there was a question in the world without an answer.

“I knew you would say that. You always go where no one is waiting for you.”

“Will you come with me?” asked August, and in the question there was not only the wish to have a reliable man beside him, but the fear of standing alone before what waited beyond the ridge.

Matteo smiled, shaking his head.

“I am old, August. My time for going into the unknown has passed. But I can take you there in my own ship. And I can give you everything you will need. You will not be alone — not until the very shore.”

He reached out and squeezed August's shoulder — firmly, like a father, so that the gesture held more than any words: support, blessing, and a farewell that had not yet come.

“And there… there you will be on your own.”

August did not smile in return. He only nodded. And in that nod there was more than in any words. In it was a promise: he would go. He would learn. He would find.

And somewhere deep in his soul he knew: this was not merely a road to an answer. It was a road to himself — to the August who had once stood at the gates of Alba-Mare and watched his world burn. Now he was going to where that fire had begun. To understand whether it could be put out. Or at least to understand why it had blazed.
