Three days later they entered the port of Sea Cliff.

The town was larger than Windholm, noisier, more alive — as though here the very earth did not know how to stand still. Ships crowded along the piers — trading vessels, fishing boats, warships, with tall masts and heavy hulls darkened by salt and time. The shouts of loaders, the clanging of chains, the creak of cranes, the smell of tar, fish and cheap wine — all of it struck the face like a hot wind, like a reminder that the world does not know how to wait and does not love those who linger. Here there was none of the quiet melancholy that had remained on the piers of Windholm. Here life seethed, stern and unhidden, like open fire on a windy day.

August stood at the side, studying the familiar outlines, and felt the past gently knocking at his chest, as though asking to come in. He knew this port. It was from here, many years ago, that he had set out on his first sea voyage. Then he had been almost a boy still, with his father's sword at his belt, with a guitar on his back and with a hope he could not explain even to himself. He had signed on to the Northern Dawn, an old worn ship with faded sails and creaking decks, where every knot seemed a test and every dawn a promise. The captain had said then: "Show yourself — and I will take you on to the eastern shore." And he had shown himself. He had worked as though his life depended on every motion. And perhaps it did.

The crew went ashore — some for drink, some for women, some simply to stretch their legs and feel again how the ground does not rock beneath one's boots. August walked along the pier, studying faces, listening to voices. The port taverns hummed; in them people laughed and argued, mugs clinked and throats tore themselves in song, as though trying to outshout the sea itself. He went into one of them — the one standing at the very edge of the embankment, with a leaning signboard and the smell of fried cod soaked into the boards. He sat at a table, ordered hot soup, bread and a mug of warm beer.

Everything was as it had been — and yet different. The same walls, the same boards, even the smell the same, as though time here had frozen in amber. But he had changed. Then he had been a guardsman, a fugitive, a man without a home, clutching at every possibility like at a straw. Now he was August Fjord. He knew his name. He knew where he was going. And that knowledge did not make him calmer, but it made him stronger: he was not afraid to look back, because he was not ashamed of a single step.

At the next table two sailors argued about the price of furs; a third — old, with a weathered face and grey beard — sat in silence, looking into his mug as though trying to read something important in the dark liquid. August looked at him, and something inside contracted like a string stretched to its limit. He recognized him. Not at once — the features had smoothed, the eyes grown a little duller, the shoulders bowed under the weight of years. But when the old man raised his head, his heart froze for a moment. It was that same captain. The one who had once taken him aboard the Northern Dawn.

August rose and came to his table. Stopped, not daring to sit, as though afraid to frighten off the memory.

"Do you know me?" he asked quietly, but his voice sounded firm.

The old man raised his eyes. He looked for a long time, screwing them up, as though trying to gather a whole face out of separate features. Then suddenly he grinned, showing his gap-toothed jaw, and in that grin there was so much sea and wind that August almost heard the creak of decks and the whistle of the storm.

"You're that lad with the guitar," said the captain. "Ran from the raiders in the strait."

"Ran," August smiled, and the smile was not of joy but of relief: the past had not turned away from him. "And stayed alive."

"I see," the old man nodded. "Not lost. That's the main thing."

"How is life?" asked August, sitting down opposite him.

"Long," the captain answered, and in that word there were enough stories for a dozen books. "But I still sail the sea. And you?"

"The same," said August. "And I still play."

They talked briefly — of the sea, of the wind, of old times when every storm was a lesson and every calm a test of patience.

The captain told how the Northern Dawn had long since been struck from the lists, how her planks had gone for firewood and her sails for patches. Now he sailed a different vessel, smaller but sturdier. He told how the war in the south had died down but not gone away — merely lain low, like a snake in tall grass. August listened, nodded, and felt how past and present converged in this port, how meetings that had seemed accidental proved the only ones possible. As though the road had led him precisely here, so that he would see this old man and understand: he had not forgotten a single one of the days that had made him what he was.

When August came out of the tavern, the sun was already sinking to its setting, staining the water in dark-orange tones, as though someone had spilled molten gold into the sea. The port town clamored, glowed with lights, smelled of smoke and fried meat, and that smell was the smell of life — coarse, real, unembellished. August stood on the embankment for a while, watching the ships, the water, the sky that was gradually darkening, revealing the first stars trembling over the horizon. He did not know whether he would ever come back here. But he knew this town was part of his road. As was Windholm. As was Alba-Mare. As every pier, every wind, every dawn he had met on this road.

He returned to the ship when it was already dark. The deck was quiet; only the water softly lapped the sides, singing its eternal song. Matteo sat on deck, smoked a pipe, and its smoke rose in thin rings, dissolving into the darkness. He looked at August, nodded, and said nothing — he did not need to. August sat beside him. They were silent, listening to how the night embraced the sea, how the stars grew brighter, and the town gradually fell silent, yielding its place to quiet.

Tomorrow they would put out to sea again. The wind would be different, the waves different, the horizon different. But the road would remain the same. And August would walk it, knowing that every step was not merely a movement forward but a conversation with the past, which had finally ceased to be an enemy and had become an ally.
