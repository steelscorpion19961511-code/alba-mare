They spent two more days together. Einar showed August how to find dry wood beneath the snow — not merely by touch but by sound: a damp branch gives back a dull knock when tapped, while a dry one rings like a thin string. He taught him to pitch a shelter from his cloak between the rocks so that the wind could not bring him down: choosing natural cover, stretching the fabric at an angle so the gusts slid over it instead of tearing it. He showed him how to tell the tracks of animals — not only by the shape of the print but by how the creature had set its paw, how long ago it had passed, whether it had hurried or moved calmly. He taught him to read the clouds: heavy, low ones with ragged edges meant a blizzard; thin threads stretched by the wind meant a clear but icy day. He explained how to know when the snow would turn to stinging grains and the wind would grow so fierce that even the rocks would groan under its blows.

“Everything here is connected,” he said, gesturing at the sky, the mountains, the sea, as though drawing invisible lines in the air that bound the elements together. “You cannot survive by a book or a map alone. You must feel it. And when you learn to feel, you will stop being afraid. Fear comes when you do not understand what is happening. When you understand — only the work remains.”

August listened. He asked no needless questions, but every word settled in him like a seed in the earth, and sprouted not at once but later, when he was alone with the road and the wind. He memorised not only the techniques but Einar’s intonations, the pauses between phrases, the way he narrowed his eyes looking at the horizon, as though trying to make out there not merely the outlines of cliffs but the changes in weather to come.

On the third morning the snowfall ceased. The sky was clean but cold, piercingly blue, the kind of blue there is only on the frostiest days, when the air becomes almost glass. The sun shone so brightly that the snow dazzled the eyes, reflecting the light in millions of tiny blades, and August had to squint to see the road. Einar stood by the fire, his staff over his shoulder. The flame had all but died; only smouldering embers remained, giving off a light steam that mingled with the frosty air.

“Now it is time for you to go,” he said calmly, without haste, as though simply noting the next step of a long road. “North, through the pass, you will find a village. There you can find lodging and food. People live there who speak your tongue — traders, former southerners. They will take you in.”

August rose, fastened his cloak, adjusted his pack and the guitar. His fingers trembled slightly as he buckled the strap — not from the cold but from the weight of the moment. He looked at Einar and felt that he did not want to leave. It seemed to him that here, by this fire, he had finally found a place where silence did not press but sheltered. But he knew he had to go on — not because he was driven, but because this peace was not his home, only a temporary refuge that had let him gather strength.

“Thank you,” he said quietly but firmly. “I will remember this fire and this snow. And your voice. I will remember how you said that truth lives not in words but in deeds.”

Einar smiled with his eyes alone — in that smile there was no mockery, only a warm, barely visible spark of understanding.

“You too left something in this fire,” he said. “I do not know what exactly, but I feel it. Perhaps a particle of southern warmth, perhaps a melody the wind caught and carried off. Go, bard. And remember: in this land they do not look for truth in words — they look for it in deeds. So simply walk. Listen, look, sometimes be silent. And you will understand everything.”

August nodded, turned, and moved ahead into the snowy distance. The snow crunched under his feet, familiar and almost tender, as though the earth were whispering to him: “I know your step.” His footprints stretched away toward the horizon, growing smaller and smaller, until they merged with the whiteness, dissolving in it like a note that sounds for one more instant after the string has stopped vibrating.

Einar stood by the fire and watched him go, until August’s figure vanished in the white haze, leaving behind only the faintest line of tracks that the light wind had already begun to erase. He sat a long while by the dying fire, listening to the wind, to its endless conversation with the rocks. And when it carried him a distant sound — as though someone were softly singing in a foreign tongue, a song about the sea and far shores — he only smiled into his grey beard and lowered himself onto a stone, knowing that August would not perish. In him was the thing the north respects above all: the ability to listen.
