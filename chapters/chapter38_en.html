August stayed in that house for several days. He was taken in without extra words — the way one takes in a traveler when a blizzard is raging beyond the windows and the roads have vanished: not as a guest who must be entertained and showered with questions, but as a person who simply needs warmth and quiet. The woman, whose name was Signe, proved to be a widow. Her husband had gone out to sea three winters ago and had not returned. She lived alone, but her house was always open to those who needed warmth — not out of showy kindness but because here in the north they knew: today you let a stranger sit by your fire, and tomorrow someone will let your son sit by theirs.

August felt it — not in words but in how she added wood to the hearth, how she set hot food before him, how she nodded silently when he took up the axe and went out to the yard. He helped her about the house: carried water from the ice hole, split firewood, laying the logs in even rows by the wall, mended an old net he had found in the shed — its meshes stretched thin, its threads worn, but he patiently tightened the knots, returning strength to it. Signe watched him with mild surprise but said nothing. Perhaps she was not used to having anyone help her. Or perhaps she simply did not want to disturb the silence that had settled between them and was as natural as the crackle of wood in the hearth.

On the second evening, when the wind was howling beyond the windows and the house smelled of bread, dried fish and smoke, Signe sat down beside him at the hearth. Her movements were unhurried, as if she were afraid to startle the warmth away. Her words were simple, and her language was halting, but August grasped the meaning. She told of the sea — of how it could be different: gentle, when one could go out in a boat and catch fish for supper, and cruel, when it takes those who trusted it. She told of her husband, of how he used to set out on his voyages, always saying: “I will return when the wind is kind.” And of how people live here in the north — not in cities but in houses pressed against the rocks, hiding from the storms. Of how they wait for spring, how they rejoice at the first warmth that stirs barely audibly in the air, and how every year they hope it will be better than the last.

August listened, and it seemed to him that he was hearing the same voice he had heard in Alba-Mare — only in another language. There the voice had been sunny and warm, filled with laughter and the cries of traders; here it was cold, restrained, chiselled from wind and silence. He raised the guitar and softly, almost weightlessly, began to play a melody that had no words but spoke of something important. It was translucent, like the air over a lake on an early morning, and in it one could hear the same silence that sounded beyond the windows — only now it did not frighten; it embraced.

Signe listened without moving, as if afraid that any movement would break this fragile bond between her memory and his music. When he finished, she said quietly:

“Your music is like the memory of a summer this place never had.”

August did not know how to answer. He smiled — not broadly but barely perceptibly, so that the smile lived only in his eyes — and nodded. That was enough.

The next day he decided to walk through the settlement. The houses stood close together, almost leaning on one another, as if holding on to each other to withstand the wind. Between them wound narrow, snow-covered paths, and the snow here lay not in an even blanket but in fanciful waves moulded by the wind. People looked at him with curiosity, but no one showed hostility. Some nodded, some looked away, as if embarrassed to examine a stranger too closely, but there was no malice in their gazes — only careful attention. August noticed that his music, which he had played at the hearth in Signe's house, had already reached them. Perhaps he was the first southerner who had come here not with a sword but with a guitar, and they did not yet know how to take it — but they were no longer afraid.

He stopped at an old stone pillar on which runes had been carved. They were unfamiliar, but he felt that they told a story — not in letters but in lines, each of which was like a scar, like a trace left by time. Someone came up behind him — an old man with grey hair and dark, deep eyes in which all the northern light was reflected.

“You see the runes?” he asked in broken language, but with such intonation that the question was not about sight — it was about the ability to see the essence.

“Yes,” August answered. “I want to understand them. Not the words — what stands behind them.”

The old man nodded, as if he had heard what he wanted to hear. He ran his finger along one of the carved lines — along a smooth edge polished by wind and rains — and began to tell. His tale was slow and deep, like the breathing of the sea: how each rune is a word that the wind carries, and how one who can read them may hear the voices of his ancestors if he falls silent and learns to listen. He spoke of how these signs helped not to forget: who you are, where you came from, whom to remember in the long winter night.

August listened, and it seemed to him that he was touching something that time could not touch. This was not a history that could be written down but a memory that could only be felt. He did not know what would come next, where the road would lead him, but in that moment he felt himself part of something vast — something that had existed long before him and would remain after.

He stood at the stone with the runes while the wind played with his hair, tugging at the collar of his cloak, as if it wanted to say something. And within him an astonishing calm spread — not emptiness but fullness, the kind that comes when you have finally found a place where you can simply stand and breathe. In that moment he understood: he had not merely passed through these lands — he had begun to hear their breathing. And that mattered more than any words.
