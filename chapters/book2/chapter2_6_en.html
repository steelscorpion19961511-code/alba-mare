At dawn August stood at the southern gates of Trade Cliff. The air still held the night's coolness, but one could already feel that it would not last — the sun was rising from behind the horizon slowly, heavily, almost grudgingly, and with each moment its light grew hotter, drier. August stood and looked at what awaited him beyond the gates: several camels loaded with bales, and four drovers — swarthy, silent men in long robes the color of scorched earth. The camels were tall, with long lashes casting shade over large dark eyes, and they moved unhurriedly, heavily, as if each step for them was not an effort but a decision they were deliberating.

August had never seen such animals. He watched them chew their cud — slowly, rhythmically, with some ancient calm — and felt the world grow larger. In the North he had known every sound, every smell, every shadow on the snow. Here everything was different: warm, foreign, alive in another way. The camels did not smell of beast, but of something dry and bitter, like old leather that had lain in the sun. Their coats were the color of sand, and in the morning light they seemed part of the desert that waited beyond the gates.

“Surprised?” asked Kane, coming up at his side. His steps were quiet, soft — he moved on stone as if born on it.

“I did not expect it,” August admitted. “I didn't think they were so… big.”

“They do not fear the heat,” said Kane, and in his voice there sounded not an explanation but the respect people show not their own kind but those who can do what they cannot. “They know how to wait. They can go days without drinking. They know when to walk and when to stand. There is something to learn from them.”

August looked at the nearest camel. It turned its head and looked at him — calmly, without curiosity, without fear, as if weighing: will this man go with us or not?

The caravan set out when the sun had only just risen over the horizon. August walked beside the camels, and his steps seemed too quick, too sharp beside their measured, swaying gait. The ground quickly grew drier, stonier, and the air hotter and heavier. First underfoot was the trodden stone of the road, then pebbles, then sand — fine and coarse-grained, which ground between the teeth and worked into the crevices of his boots.

They walked all day. August was used to the long road — in the North he could walk for hours without slackening his pace, feeling every stone underfoot, every root beneath the moss. But here everything was different: there were no trees, no water, no shade. Only stone, sand and sparse bushes clinging to life with thin, thorny branches, as if they were not growing but holding on with their last strength. The sun climbed ever higher, and its light ceased to be light — it became pressure, heavy, tangible, as if someone had laid a hot palm on the back of his neck and would not remove it. Sweat ran down his face, down his back, soaked his shirt, and the clothing clung to his body, wet and heavy, yet it did not cool — only made him feel more keenly how the heat came from the ground, from the stones, from the air, which was so dense it could almost be touched.

The drovers walked in silence, neither slackening nor quickening their pace. They moved like their camels: evenly, sparingly, spending exactly as much strength as was needed, not a drop more. August tried to imitate their rhythm, and by midday he understood: here one must not hurry. To hurry in the desert means to burn up before you arrive.

At the rest stop Kane untied a sack with flatbreads, cheese and dried meat. The flatbreads were dry, hard, but the salty cheese and meat made them edible. August ate slowly, feeling the food draw moisture from his body, and washed it down with water from the waterskin — warm, with the taste of leather.

“Sit,” said Kane, and August sat by the small fire which one of the drovers had built of dry twigs and dung. The fire was small, not for warmth — for light. Here warmth was superfluous.

The drovers ate silently apart, squatting by the camels. They did not look at August, did not look at Kane, did not look at one another. Each was alone with the road, and this silence was not estrangement — it was rhythm, as natural as a step or breath.

“There is no kingdom here,” said Kane, looking into the fire. His voice was even, without preachiness, as if he were not telling a story but showing a map. “Here there are sultans. Each major city has its own. They guard their lands, their roads, their wells. But beyond them — no one. Only the desert, the rocks, and those who seek easy plunder.”

August listened. Twigs crackled in the fire, and that crackle was the only sound in the silence, if one did not count the distant, even roar of the wind carrying sand across the plain.

“We are going to Dead Rock,” Kane continued. “It is not a city. A small settlement at the foot of the rock. There is water there, shelter, people who know these lands. From there we go on — to Shaan-Tar, a large trading city in the east.”

He paused and added, not taking his eyes from the fire:

“If we make it.”

August looked at the dark desert beyond the fire. There, beyond the circle of light, the world ended: neither stars nor horizon nor ground could be seen — only blackness, dense and thick, as if the desert were hiding in it for the night, to come out into the light again tomorrow. In that blackness one could make out only movement: sand lifted by the wind traveled over the ground in thin, swift streams, like water along a riverbed.

“Do you believe in fate, Kane?” asked August, and his voice sounded unusually loud in that silence.

Kane did not answer at once. He looked into the fire, and his face was lit by an uneven, flickering light that made him younger, stripping away the weariness and strain.

“I believe that the road itself chooses who walks it,” he said at last. “If you are here, it means the road has decided you may. And if it decides you may not, you will not make it. Not because you are weak. But because the path is not yours.”

August nodded. He was thinking that the road had already chosen him several times: in the North, in Alba-Mare, in Trade Cliff. Each time he had thought he was walking on his own. But perhaps it was the road leading him, not he walking it.

They ate in silence, watching the fire. He felt the desert breathing around them — quietly, heavily, like an old beast that sleeps but hears every sound. The night here was not like the North: there the darkness had been cold, ringing, full of stars. Here it was warm, dense, almost tangible, and the stars in it were not piercing but soft, as if shining through cloth. He did not know what lay ahead. But he knew this was the right road. Not because he was certain. But because he felt it, as one feels the breathing of a person nearby: one does not see it, but knows it is there.

The night passed without sleep — or rather, without true sleep. August lay on a mat, covered with his cloak, and watched the sky slowly change color: black, then dark blue, then grey, and at last, at the very edge of the horizon, a strip of light appeared — pale, thin, like the first stroke on a canvas. The desert woke slowly: first the wind, then the light, then the warmth. And August rose when the sun had not yet come from behind the horizon, but its nearness could already be felt.

In the morning they walked again. The heat intensified, and his skin grew used to the dry, scorching air but would not surrender: it broke out in small cracks that stung with sweat, and his lips dried and split like a clay crust under the sun. August wrapped his face in cloth as the drovers did, and it grew easier: his breathing became moist, the air passed through the cloth and cooled, as if filtered. He understood why the locals wore such clothing: not from modesty, but for survival.

The camels walked at a measured pace, without slackening. They seemed the only ones untouched by the heat: they walked, chewed their cud, blinked their long lashes, and in their movements was a calm August was beginning to understand. Not dullness, not indifference, but knowledge: the heat will pass, the sand will end, the road will lead. One need only walk.

The drovers did not say a word. Only the wind, only the sand, only the endless land running beyond the horizon. The landscape did not change: the same stones, the same sand, the same sky, whitened by the heat, without a single cloud. The only sign of movement was the shadows of the stones, which crawled slowly over the ground like the hands of an enormous clock.

By evening of the second day they saw the rock.

At first it was merely a dark spot on the horizon, barely distinguishable in the shimmer of heat. But with every hour the spot grew, rose, darkened, until it became clear: this was no mirage, no cloud, but stone. Enormous, dark, thrust into the sky like a tooth the earth could not swallow. The rock stood alone in the midst of the desert, with no other mountains beside it, no hills, no ledges — simply a vertical mass of stone rising from a plain flat as a table. At its foot — several low structures of stone and clay, with flat roofs where rags and hides were drying. Smoke curled above the chimneys in thin, lazy wisps, as if it too did not wish to rise into that hot, dense air.

“Dead Rock,” said Kane, nodding toward it. In his voice there was neither alarm nor relief, only statement. “A place where one can rest.”

They entered the settlement when the sun was already leaning toward sunset, and the shadows had grown long, sharp, as if cut from black paper. The people watched with curiosity but without hostility: traders raised their heads from their stalls, children ran between legs, and an old woman sitting by the wall followed August with a gaze he could not decipher — whether appraising, or pitying, or simply watching because there was nothing else to do. It smelled of smoke, dried meat, and something sweet August could not place — something like honey, but with a bitter tang, as if from a flower that had grown on stone.

Kane stopped by one of the houses and gestured for him to follow. Inside it was dark and cool — the coolness felt like a gift; after two days of heat it touched the skin like water. The walls were thick, built of stone and clay, and they held the cold of night, not letting in the heat of day. The host — an old man with a grey beard and dark eyes in which there was the same calm attentiveness as in the drovers — nodded to them as if he had been expecting them. He did not smile, did not offer a hand; he simply nodded, and in that nod there was more hospitality than in any southern greeting.

“There is lodging,” said Kane, without turning around. “Rest. Tomorrow we will decide what comes next.”

August sat on a mat by the wall, set down his pack and the guitar. The mat was woven of dry grass, stiff and rough, but after two days on stone and sand it seemed soft as a feather bed. He ran a hand over the guitar's case: the wood was hot, and the strings answered the touch with a quiet, muffled sound, as if complaining of the heat. He did not take it out. Not now.

He did not know what tomorrow held. But he knew this was one of those days that are remembered. Not because they were bright or frightening, but because they were firsts: the first desert, the first camels, the first heat from which there was no hiding. All of it was new, and the new was not foreign — merely not yet familiar.

He closed his eyes. Beyond the walls the wind howled, somewhere a donkey brayed, and in the distance, perhaps, those who sought easy plunder were already gathering. But here, in this house, in this coolness, it was quiet. And August fell asleep — soundly, deeply, the way one sleeps only after a long road.

When he opened his eyes, dawn had already broken outside the window. The sky was pale pink, and the first rays of the sun were touching the summit of the rock, tinting it a warm, reddish color. August went outside, kneading his shoulders, and felt the morning coolness touch his skin, slip under his shirt, cool his body. It was the last cool moment, and he knew it: within an hour the heat would return and would not let go until sunset.

The settlement was waking. Several old men were already sitting by the walls, warming themselves in the first rays of the sun — they sat silently, motionless, like stones, their faces turned to the light with the same calm greed with which flowers reach for the sun. Children ran between the low houses, and their voices were the only living sound in the stillness. One of the boys, noticing August, stopped, looked at him with serious dark eyes, then ran off, as if he had seen something unusual but not frightening.

Kane was already up. He stood by the stable, where the camels — already unsaddled, freed of their bales — were chewing hay. The drovers busied themselves nearby: checking the harness, retying the bales, examining the animals' hooves. The camels stood calmly, sleepily chewing their cud, only occasionally snorting their displeasure at the early rising — a displeasure shared, probably, only by August and the camels themselves. Kane noticed August, nodded and waved a hand toward the well.

“Help with the water,” he said. “We need to fill the waterskins. Beyond it is dry — there will be no water all the way to Shaan-Tar.”

August went to the well and took the leather waterskins Kane held out to him. The water was cold, almost icy — it smelled of stone and earth, and that smell was the smell of life in a world where everything else was dry and dead. He filled skin after skin, feeling the weight grow on his shoulders, and tied each by the neck. The water sloshed inside, and that sound was quiet but alive, and August listened to it the way one listens to a song that reminds you why you keep walking.
