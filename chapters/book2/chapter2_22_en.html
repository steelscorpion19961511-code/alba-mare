He left the market square when the sun had risen higher and begun to beat down so hard that even shadows seemed a treasure. The noise of the market fell behind, giving way to the rustle of leaves, the murmuring of water, and scattered voices that now sounded not like cries but like separate notes in the quiet melody of the day. The city changed around him: trading rows gave way to spacious courtyards, stone walls to trees growing along the canals, as though the land itself wanted to breathe more freely here.

He found the park by accident. Turning a corner, not knowing where he was going, August saw a low arch overgrown with green — as if someone had deliberately hidden the entrance so that only those truly seeking quiet would find it. He stepped inside — and stopped.

The silence here was not empty but thick, filled with life. The green stood like a wall — the lush crowns of old trees walled the park off from the city's bustle, and the humid air smelled of raw earth, fresh leaves, and the barely perceptible sweetness of flowering bushes. Along the canal grew trees with thick gnarled trunks and full crowns casting a dense shade in which it was cool even at midday. The water in the canal was dark green, almost motionless, and in it, as in a mirror, clouds drifted slowly across the sky. A wooden footbridge, darkened by time and moisture, crossed the canal and led to a small arbor wreathed in wild grapes.

At first glance there were hardly any people: only an old man with a book on a bench, sitting so motionless he might have been part of the park itself; and a woman with a child at the water's edge — she was showing the little girl how to skip flat stones across the water, and the stones jumped, leaving light circles on the surface. It was another world — no less real than the noisy market, but living in a different rhythm.

August sat on the grass at the water's edge and laid the guitar across his knees. He did not want to play loudly. He wanted the music to become part of this silence, to dissolve in the rustle of leaves, in the splash of water, in the few bird voices.

Quiet, almost in a whisper. His fingers barely touched the strings, drawing out soft, transparent sounds that did not argue with the silence but completed it. The melody came by itself — out of the morning, out of the shade of the trees, out of the way the water touched the stones as if whispering something eternal. It was playing for himself — hardly hearing the sound, feeling it in his fingers, in his chest, in the way he grew calmer inside.

The old man on the bench closed his book and raised his head, listening. The woman with the child stopped and looked at August, and for a moment even the stones stopped skipping across the water. He played, gazing at the water, until he felt a presence beside him.

He raised his eyes. A few steps away stood a woman holding a child — a little girl with dark curly hair and big eyes that watched him unwaveringly, as if she saw not simply a man with a guitar but something beyond the reach of grown-ups. The woman smiled without saying anything, but that smile held more words than any conversation.

August shifted to a melody he had known from childhood — a simple, almost lullaby-like one his mother had once sung. In it there was neither sorrow nor joy — only warmth and peace, the kind one wants to keep. The girl smiled and clapped her hands, as if the melody were a gift. The woman laughed, whispering something in the local tongue — words August did not know but whose meaning he understood at once.

Smiling in return, he went on playing. Time lost count: it stretched like the shadow of a tree and contracted like circles on the water. The music belonged to this girl, to the old man, to himself — and to no one at the same time, because it simply was, like wind or sunlight.

When he finished, August saw the woman nod and walk on, holding the girl in her arms. The girl waved, as if wanting to carry that sound away with her. The old man closed his eyes, then opened his book again, but there was something new in his gaze now — as though he had heard in the melody something he had long forgotten.

August stayed sitting by the water. He did not get up right away. He watched the clouds reflected in the dark surface, watched the sun breaking through the leaves and scattering into thousands of golden sparks. He felt calm spreading within him — deep, real, undisturbed by any noise or hurry.

He did not earn a single coin in that park. But he received what was more important: that music can simply be music. Not payment, not work, not a way to survive — but something one can give for nothing, expecting nothing in return. And that too had its worth — quiet as the whisper of water on stone, but no less real than the chime of coins on the market square.
