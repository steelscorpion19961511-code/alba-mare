It happened on one of the evenings at the Nanna Tavern. August had finished playing and sat at the counter, sipping warm herbal tea — bitter, with the taste of mint and something floral whose name he did not know. His fingers were still humming from the playing, and he rubbed them one against the other, feeling the calloused pads slowly let go of their tension. The hall was quiet: a few people at the far tables talked in low voices, someone dozed leaning against the wall, someone slowly finished his wine, staring before him with the scattered gaze of a person gone deep into his own thoughts. August did not listen to them — he simply rested, feeling how the tiredness of the long day released not only his fingers but his shoulders, his back, the whole body that over half a year in Al-Sharan had grown used to work, to heat, to the rhythm of the city — but had not unlearned to remember the road.

The tavern keeper — the same man with the lively eyes — was wiping the counter with a cloth, unhurried, with a practiced motion, and from time to time glanced at August with that silent attention that had become a habit between them. They spoke little: over half a year, all their conversations together would have fit on a single page. But they understood each other without words, the way people understand who are bound not by a shared history but by silence.

Then he caught a fragment of a phrase that made him freeze.

“…on the southern outskirts again. They say a village was burned. The same ones as last time.”

The voice came from a far table, from two men sitting in the shadows, almost indistinguishable in the half-dark. August did not turn his head. He simply sat, holding his mug, and listened as the voices carried on — calmly, without fear, with the matter-of-factness of people speaking of things they had grown used to.

“Northerners?” asked the second voice, younger, with a note of surprise.

“Who else? Only they sail the sea when hunger presses. They come from the western North, round the ridge by water, and land on the southern shore. At night, usually. They creep up on a village while everyone sleeps, and…” He did not finish, but the silence said more than words.

“But we thought the eastern northerners no longer attacked,” said the young one. “After the passes were blocked off…”

“It is not the eastern ones,” the older one cut in, and in his voice there was the weariness of a man who has had to explain the same thing many times. “It is the western ones. Those beyond the ridge. They know no trade, they know no language. They simply come in boats, take what they can carry, and leave. Or burn it, if there is nothing to take.”

August looked into his mug, and in the dark tea a face was reflected — his face, unfamiliar and familiar at once. He felt a cold rise within him — not the cold of the North, the familiar, known cold, but another: the cold of understanding, the cold of recognition. He had not known the North was divided. He had not known there were “western” ones and “eastern” ones. When he had lived in the North — there, with Bjorn, with Signe, with the shaman — the world had seemed simple: there was the North, and there was the South, and between them the ridge that held them apart. But now, out of this half-darkness, out of this conversation that was not meant for him, he learned: the world was more complex. There was the North, and there was another North. And that other one was worse.

Those who had burned Alba-Mare had come from the north. August knew this: he remembered the fire, remembered the screams, remembered his father's face in the last moments. But who had they been — eastern or western? He had never thought about it. For him they had all been “northerners”: one word, one enemy. Now it turned out that in the North there were people who traded and knew the language of the South — and people who knew neither and came only for plunder. And perhaps it was these last who had stood at the gates of Alba-Mare.

He set down the mug and turned toward the speakers. Two men — an older one, with a weathered face and grey temples, and a younger one, with a short beard and restless hands that kept worrying something: the edge of a sleeve, a belt, the rim of a mug. They noticed August's gaze and fell silent, wary, the way people fall silent when a stranger intrudes on a conversation.

“You were speaking of the western northerners,” August said quietly, trying to keep his voice even, without the tremor he felt inside. “How do you know of them?”

The men glanced at each other. Then the older one looked at August more closely, and in his gaze something flickered — not fear, not curiosity, but a weighing: who this man was, what he wanted, whether he could be trusted. August knew that look: that is how they look in the North when they meet an outsider. Assessing, without hostility, but without warmth.

“I was on the southern outskirts,” the older one said, after a slight pause. “I saw the ashes. The village of Qasr-ed-Dain, if you know it. It stood on the trade road, twenty houses, maybe more. A month ago — smoke, ash, silence. Not one survivor. The same traces as in Alba-Mare when it fell. The same manner: houses set alight, doors broken open, bodies where the night had found them. No sword wounds — only axes and spears. No sorting through: they took grain, cattle, cloth, everything they could carry to the boats. The rest they burned.”

August listened, and inside him, like an old wound, something stirred that he had thought forgotten. The same traces. The same manner. He remembered Alba-Mare: how the houses burned, how his father stood at the gates, how his mother screamed, how the smoke sealed the sky so that neither sun nor stars could be seen. And now, seventeen years later, in a tavern at the other edge of the world, someone was describing the same thing — a different village, a different year, but the same hand, the same handwriting.

“And the eastern ones?” asked August. His voice sounded even, but he himself caught the note in it that gave away more than he would have liked.

“The eastern ones are different,” the man said, and in his voice there was not respect but acknowledgment. “They trade, they know our language. Sometimes they come to the southern villages, buy grain and iron, pay with furs and amber. They live harshly, but honestly. They are not feared. But the western ones…” He paused, searching for the word. “They are feared. They say they do not go only south. They plunder the western lands beyond the sea as well — places none of ours has ever been. No one has seen them up close, because those who saw them do not return.”

The younger man nodded in confirmation:

“Last week a caravan came from Taruma. A trader told how on the coast, three days' journey to the west, they found a village — all burned, the people gone into the mountains. The tracks led to the shore, where boats had been. Many boats. Not fishing boats — long ones, with outriggers. Northern ones.”

August was silent. He listened. And inside him, like an old wound, something stirred that he had thought forgotten. The western lands… He knew nothing of them. Bjorn had never spoken of the western North — perhaps because he did not know it, perhaps because he had not thought it necessary. The shaman had spoken of the ridge that divides the North in two, but briefly, as of something distant and unimportant. “Beyond the ridge are others,” he had said. “They are not ours.” And nothing more. Only this: not ours. August had not asked. Then it had not mattered. Now it did.

Because if the western northerners plundered both the southern lands and the western ones, then they were stronger than they seemed. Then they were not one village and not one boat, but a force that moved by sea, rounded the ridge by water, and landed where it was not expected. And if that same force had stood at the gates of Alba-Mare, if the same hands had set his home on fire, killed his father, broken his life — then the question he had carried within himself for seventeen years took on a new meaning. Not "who did this," but "where are they."

“When were they last seen?” asked August.

The older one shrugged.

“Two weeks ago. Fishermen saw boats at the cape, a day's journey to the south. They were moving west. They may have gone away, or they may return. They always return.”

“Always?”

“In winter. When hunger presses. They come in winter.”

Winter. August remembered the northern winter: long, dark, hungry. Bjorn had said: "In winter the North is hungry. In winter the wolves come down into the valleys. In winter men become wolves." If the winters of the western North were the same, and food was scarcer, then they came not out of malice but out of hunger. That made it no easier. A hungry wolf bites no more gently than a well-fed one.

The younger man added:

“They say they have a leader. Someone has united the tribes beyond the ridge. Before, they fought among themselves; now they move together. If that is true, then the worst is yet to come.”

August looked at him. A leader. Someone who had united. Someone who had led. That changed everything: not raiders, not random raids, but a force with a face and a name. Though he did not know the name. Not yet.

He rose, left a coin on the counter, and nodded to the tavern keeper. The man caught his glance, and in it flickered understanding — perhaps he had heard the conversation too, perhaps he simply saw in August's face something that had changed in those few minutes. He said nothing. He only nodded back.

He went out into the street. Evening Al-Sharan clamored around him: the cries of traders calling to the last customers, the laughter of children running through the lanes, the ringing of bells on camels, the clatter of shutters being closed for the night, the distant splash of water in the canals. The city lived, breathed, moved — but August did not hear it. He walked the streets without noticing where he was going, and thought.

He thought of how the world he had believed he understood had turned out to be more complex. There was not one North, but two. There were not only enemies, but traders. There was not only chaos, but a leader who had gathered the tribes and led them south. And there was not only his own private fire — Alba-Mare, his father, the screams, the smoke — but other fires, other villages, other screams that burned and sounded for the same reason.

And the road was not finished. Not because he had nothing to keep him in Al-Sharan — here he had everything: home, work, music, respect. But because somewhere in the west, beyond the sea, beyond the ridge, there lived a leader who had burned his world and, perhaps, was burning other worlds. And August did not know what he would do when he found him. Did not know whether he wanted vengeance, or truth, or simply an answer to the question: why. But he knew he had to go. Because what is unfinished pulls like a snapped string, and does not let new ones sound.

He walked through the night city, and the stars emerged above the minarets — the same stars as everywhere, the same as in the North, on the island, on the sea. He looked at them and thought: Bjorn had said the stars were the eyes of those who had gone.
