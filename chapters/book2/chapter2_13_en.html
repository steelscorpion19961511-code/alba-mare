The ship was making its way back toward the mainland. The wind was weak but steady, and the vessel moved slowly yet confidently, as if it knew the shore was near and was in no hurry, giving the men a chance to breathe after weeks on the open sea. August stood on the bow, watching the line of the horizon gradually take on shape. Somewhere there, beyond that water, land began — land he had not seen for nearly a month. The sun hung low, and the water seemed golden, molten, and in that gold the shadows of the waves were drowning, slow and heavy.

The morning was quiet. Too quiet. August felt it: not with his mind, but with that inner sense the North had honed to a blade's edge. Last night he had seen sails in the south. Today he saw none. But that did not mean they had gone. It might mean they had come closer and were waiting.

Hasan stood nearby, checking a rope, and his fingers, usually quick and sure, moved more slowly, as though filled with lead. He felt it too. Dariush stood at the rail, looking south, and his hand, as on all the recent days, rested on the amulet. The other sailors went about their business: some washed the deck, some mended rigging, some simply sat and smoked. An ordinary morning at sea. But August knew: ordinary mornings deceive. Before a blizzard in the North, the morning had been ordinary too. The sun shone, the snow lay even, the silence stood like a dome. Then the wind came down from the mountains, and in two hours everything changed.

The captain stood at the helm, gazing ahead. But his gaze was not on the horizon, not on the shore they were approaching. He was looking aside, to the south. His face was calm, but August noticed: the knuckles of his fingers on the wheel had gone white. The captain saw what August saw, and was doing what August was doing: preparing. Quietly, without words, without fuss.

He followed the captain's gaze — and saw.

Sails.

Not yesterday's — distant, indistinct. Today they were closer. Much closer. And there were not three or four of them but more — maybe six, maybe eight. Small, sharp, like the fins of predatory fish, and they moved fast, far faster than yesterday. The wind blew from the south, and it was their wind. August narrowed his eyes, and his heart sank: the ships came in a wedge, like a pack of wolves closing in for the attack. That was how wolves surrounded a deer in the North: they did not rush in all at once, but spread out, cutting off the ways of escape. It was the same here — only on water.

"Captain!" Hasan cried, and for the first time in all these weeks there was fear in his voice.

The captain did not answer. He acted. His voice, usually quiet and even, became sharp, clipped, like the strokes of an axe:

"Everyone on deck! Sails — hard to port! Grab anything that cuts or strikes! Quickly!"

The sailors ran. Some threw themselves at the sails, trying to wring the last of the wind from them. Some grabbed a boat hook, some a kitchen knife, some a broken plank. There was almost no weaponry on a merchant ship: a short dagger at the captain's belt, two fish spears, several knives. Against those coming from the south, it was nothing.

August already stood at the rail with his father's sword in his hand. The blade left its sheath without a sound, with a familiar ease, as if it had been waiting for this moment. August felt its weight — heavy, solid, real — and within him there was calm. Not from confidence, but from a familiar feeling: when the sword is in your hand, the body knows what to do. The mind had not yet decided, but the body had already decided for it. Bjorn's knife was at his belt too, in its place, as always in battle.

"Hasan," said August without turning. "Stay behind me. Don't push forward. If anyone gets close — strike here," he pointed to his own thigh. "With your foot. Your knee. Anything. But don't stand still. Move."

Hasan nodded. His face was pale, but his eyes were alive, and August saw in them something he had not expected: not panic, but resolve. Perhaps he had learned it from August. Perhaps from the sea. Perhaps from that moment when he had drawn the first clean note from the guitar and understood that he could.

The pirate ships drew near. Now August could see them clearly: low, narrow, with black sails, worn but catching every gust of wind. Men stood on their prows — dark, hooded figures with weapons in their hands. Curved blades, spears, axes.

One — on the largest vessel — stood with a bare sword raised overhead, shouting something, but the words were lost: only wind, only a cry, only the rhythm of the waves.

The first ship struck the hull with a dull, heavy crash. The ship shuddered, the planks groaned, and the mast tilted by several degrees. Grappling hooks flew over the rail — iron, barbed, on chains — and bit into the handrails, the masts, the ropes. And along them, as along ladders, men climbed.

August stood at the rail and waited. Not because he was brave — because he knew: the first blow matters most. In the North, Bjorn had taught: "Don't rush to meet them. Let them come. Whoever comes is already yours. You are on your own ground, even if that ground is a deck."

The first pirate tumbled over the rail — swift, agile, a crooked blade in his hand. August swung — one stroke, downward, on the diagonal. The blade entered the shoulder and came out at the waist. The pirate collapsed on the deck, and his blood — dark, thick — ran between the planks like water after rain.

The second — right behind him, with a spear. August sidestepped, caught the shaft, yanked it toward himself, and in the same instant struck with his foot at the chest. The pirate went over the side, and his cry was cut off by a splash.

The third Hasan met — not with a sword but with a length of rope, which lashed across his face. The pirate recoiled, and August finished him with a short blow to the side.

But there were too many of them.

They came from every side — over the rail, over the bow, over the stern. Black clothing, dark faces, shouts in an incomprehensible tongue, and each one carried death. August moved across the deck as he had moved across snow: quickly, precisely, without wasted motion. His father's sword worked — cutting, thrusting, parrying. He did not count the slain. There was no time. There was only rhythm: strike, step, strike, recoil, strike.

He killed two. Three. A fourth. A fifth. He did not count. But he saw his comrades falling, one after another. Blood flooded the deck, the planks grew slick, and his feet found footing with difficulty. Cries mingled with the slap of the waves, the crack of wood, the clang of metal. The smell of blood — coppery, heavy — smothered everything: the salt, the tar, the wind.

He saw one of the sailors — young, whose name he did not know — fall, grasping at the air, an open wound at his throat. He saw another — old, bearded — fight back with a boat hook until they drove a spear into his back. He saw the cook — silent, stout, the man who had made them fish and flatbread — come out of the galley with a knife and throw himself on a pirate, and they fell together, and neither rose again.

He understood: this was not a battle. This was a slaughter.

In the North battle was different: there was an enemy, and you knew where he was. Here the enemy was everywhere — on every side, endless as the sea. August retreated toward the mast, covering with his back those who still stood. There were few of them: maybe four, maybe three. Hasan — beside him, a knife in his hand, pale but on his feet. Dariush — at the rail, with a broken oar, and his face wore an expression August had never seen on it: not frightened, not furious, but ancient, as if he had seen this before, knew how it would end — and stood all the same.

And then August saw the captain.

The captain stood at the helm. In his hand — a dagger, short, no longer than a palm. Two pirates approached him — slowly, confidently, as though they knew their prey was not going anywhere. The captain did not retreat. He stepped forward and struck the first — quickly, precisely, in the belly. The man folded in half. But the second — with a sword, a wide blow — laid open his chest. The captain fell to his knees, covering his chest against the next stroke. August lunged toward him but was too late — the blade came down, and the captain collapsed onto the planks, and did not rise again.

August froze for an instant. Everything froze. Time stopped, as it does in battle when the world contracts to a single point, and in that point there is a body on the deck, and blood, and a silence louder than any scream.

Then he saw Dariush — old Dariush, who touched his amulet and spoke little and heavily — lift the broken oar and step toward the pirates. He struck one — on the head, and the man spun away. He struck a second — but a third drove a spear into his side, and Dariush sank down onto the deck, slowly, as though sitting down to rest. The amulet slipped from his hand and rolled across the planks, clinking against the mast.

Hasan stood at the mast. He breathed hard, the knife trembling in his hand, but he did not run. Did not cry out. He simply stood. August ran up and seized his shoulder:

"Hasan! Into the water! Jump!"

Hasan looked at him. Eyes — huge, dark, full of tears that did not fall. He did not move. Not from fear — from the impossibility of leaving those who were still alive. But there were almost no living left.

"Jump!" August shouted. "Can you swim?"

"Yes," Hasan's voice was quiet but steady.

"Then jump. Swim north. Toward the shore. Don't look back."

Hasan gave him one long, last look. Then he nodded, climbed over the rail, and jumped. A splash — and he was gone. August did not watch. He hoped Hasan would make it.

He fell back toward the rail, parrying blows. There were fewer pirates now — not because they had withdrawn, but because there was no one left to fight. The crew was dead. Those who survived threw themselves over the side, tried to save themselves by swimming. The pirates finished off those who still moved — calmly, methodically, the way livestock is slaughtered. Not with anger — with habit.

He reached the rail, vaulted over it, and dove into the water.

The cold seared his body — not icy, as in the North, but sharp, sudden after the heat of battle. He went under, feeling the waves wash the blood from his hands, his face, his hair. Underwater it was quiet: no cries, no clang, no crashing. Only a hum — deep, low, as if the sea were sighing. He opened his eyes: greenish murk, bubbles, shadows above. His body worked: arms pulling, legs kicking, lungs holding. In the North he had learned to hold his breath under icy water, washing in an ice hole. Here the water was warm, but the skill remained: don't panic, don't breathe, move.

He surfaced and looked back. The pirates no longer paid attention to the water. They were plundering the ship: hauling off bales, breaking open barrels, searching for valuables. The cries had become businesslike, hurried: they were dividing the spoils, arguing, cursing. Someone laughed. The laugh was short, barking, and August felt rise within him something that had not been there in battle: fury.

A quiet, cold, northern fury, which does not shout but waits. But there was no one and nothing to wait for. The crew was dead. The ship was someone else's now. There was only looking.

He took nothing. The guitar remained in the hold. The pack — by his sleeping place. The herbs gathered in Holmstad. The coins. The ring with the red stone, taken from the slain bandit in the desert. All of it remained there, on the burning, sinking ship. But his father's sword and Bjorn's knife were with him, at his belt, because he always went into battle with them, and had jumped into the water just so, without removing them. All he could do was survive.

He lay on the water, holding on to a broken plank — a piece of the deck planking torn loose in the boarding — and watched.

The pirates finished their looting. One of them — the leader, it seemed, tall, in a dark cloak — stepped up onto the robbed ship, looked around, said something. Then he took out a torch — simply, without ceremony — and brought it to the sails. The sail caught fire at once: the cloth was dry, tarred, and the flame ran over it like water, fast, greedy, leaving black smoke behind. Then — the masts, then — the deck. The pirates leapt down onto their ships and pulled away without looking back. The fire did the rest.

He watched the fire devour the ship. First the sails: they burned bright as torches, and the smoke rose in a black column visible for many miles. Then the masts: the wood cracked and groaned, and sparks flew upward like stars — only unfriendly, falling ones. Then the deck: the planks blackened, twisted, caved in, and from the hold poured thick white smoke — the provisions, the oil, the cloth burning.

He heard the wood crack, heard the water hiss where it touched the burning hull. He heard the ship moan — not a creak but a true moan, as if a living thing were dying, and there was so much pain in that dying that August closed his eyes. But the sound remained: even through water and distance he heard the ship letting go of its life.

The fire was reflected in the water, and the sea around the burning vessel turned orange, alive, as if blood were glowing beneath the surface. August lay on the plank, watched, and felt only emptiness.

Not grief — that would come later. Not anger — that was already there, quiet, hidden, like embers beneath ash. Only emptiness, clean and cold as the sky in the North on a moonless night.

Once more he was alone. The ship on which he had spent almost a month, which had become home — the only home of the past years — burned and sank. The people he had known lay on its deck — dead, forgotten, abandoned. Hasan — had he made it? August did not know. Could not know. Could only hope.

And now there was nothing — only his father's sword and Bjorn's knife at his belt, and a broken plank beneath his hands. Everything else — the guitar, the herbs, the coins, the things gathered over weeks of travel — had vanished into the fire or to the bottom. It had been like this once before: when Alba-Mare burned, and he fled, taking nothing but his father's sword and Bjorn's knife. Then, too, he had been left alone. Then, too, he had not known what to do. But then he had been twenty-four. Now he was almost thirty. Then he had run from the fire. Now he watched it. The difference was this: he did not run. Not because he could not — because there was nothing to fear. The fire had taken everything it could take. The rest was within. And what is within does not burn.

The ship went under slowly, as if reluctant to drown. First the bow, then the deck, then the masts, and last of all the flag disappeared — dark, scorched, become part of the smoke. The water closed over it with a quiet, gurgling sound, and circles spread across the surface, widening and fading until they vanished altogether.

Silence.

August lay on the plank and looked at the place where the ship had just been. Now there was only water there — even, dark, indifferent. The sea does not remember. The sea does not mourn. It simply is, as it was and will be, and it has no concern for men and their ships. August knew this. He had known it before. But now he felt — not with his mind but with his whole body — how that knowledge settles on the shoulders, like a weight that cannot be lifted off.

There was only swimming left. Where? — he did not know that either. The shore was somewhere in the north. Maybe far. Maybe near. He could not see it. He could see nothing — only water, sky, and the stars beginning to show in the darkness, one after another, like the fires of distant camps.

He adjusted the plank — it was long and narrow, and it held on the water like a raft, though it was not one. He lay on it chest-down, legs trailing in the water, and began to paddle with his arms. Slowly, evenly, without breaking rhythm. In the North he had learned to conserve strength: not to tear, not to strain, but to move in a rhythm the body could sustain for hours. Arm, arm, breath in, arm, arm, breath out. The waves were small but even, and they rocked him like a cradle, and there was something almost tender in that rocking, as if the sea were trying to soothe the man it had just robbed.

The water was warm, and that saved him: in the North he would not have lasted an hour. Here the body did not lose heat so fast, and he could swim for a long time. But "long" is not "forever." Strength ebbed. The arms that had swung the sword now rowed more and more slowly. The legs that had beaten off blows grew heavy as stone. Thirst — that was the worst of it. Salt water all around, and not a drop of fresh. His lips cracked; his tongue grew dry and rough. In the North there was no thirst — there was snow, ice, springs. Here there was only salt.

He swam an hour. Maybe two. Maybe more. He did not count. There was no point in counting: time had no meaning when you do not know how much of it is left. The sky darkened, the stars brightened, and August steered by them as the shaman had taught: he found the one that always stands in the north and swam toward it. Not straight — the waves carried him aside, and he corrected, corrected constantly, like a helmsman who cannot afford to sleep.

Somewhere nearby a piece of wreckage appeared — another plank, perhaps from the same ship, carried by the current. He seized it, drew it in, laid it beside the first. A small, unreliable raft resulted — two planks bound with a length of rope that floated nearby. Not a raft — a suggestion of a raft. But it held.

He rolled onto his back, and the planks held him, and he looked at the sky. The stars were the same as in the North, and in the south, and everywhere. The same ones Bjorn had seen. The same ones his mother had seen. The same ones that would burn when he was gone. There was something consoling in that: the world goes on, the stars burn, and you are a small point between sky and water — but you are alive.

He thought of Hasan. Had he made it? Hasan was young, strong, able to swim. Maybe he had. Maybe not. August could not help him.

He could help no one. The crew was dead. The ship was on the bottom. The guitar had burned or sunk. The herbs — the same. Everything he had gathered, carried, saved — gone in a single hour.

There remained the sword and the knife. His father's sword — the one that had defended the gates of Alba-Mare. Bjorn's knife — the one he had given on that last evening, when the snow fell on their shoulders and words were unnecessary. Two objects, two men, two worlds. Father and Bjorn. South and North. What was before the fire, and what was after. Everything else can be restored. These two things — never.

And there remained what cannot be taken away. The North in the body: how to breathe, how to walk, how to listen to silence. The voices in memory: Bjorn, Signe, Teir, Ulrik, Dariush. The songs that lived not on the strings but within. The experience that had become part of him, like the tattoos on his arms, like the scars, like the lines on his face.

He was alone. But he was alive. And that was the main thing.

He did not know how long he swam. The hours ran together into one long, endless passage, like those he had made in the North, walking through a blizzard and seeing nothing but a white wall. Here there was no wall — only water on every side, and sky above, and stars, and silence. The body worked: arms pulling, legs kicking, lungs breathing. The mind switched off: it did not think, did not remember, did not plan. It simply was.

Sometimes he dozed — or sank into half-sleep, when the body kept moving and the mind drifted somewhere deep, into a warm stillness. He woke from the cold — at night the wind turned cooler — and rowed again. Strength ebbed, but not quickly: the North had taught his body to live on the minimum when there was nothing left but will.

Before dawn he saw a dark strip on the horizon. At first he thought — a cloud. Then — a wave. Then he understood: land. A shore. Dark, low, barely distinguishable, but real. Land.

He did not rejoice. He did not weep. He simply rowed faster — a little faster — and his body answered, as if it had heard a call his mind had not yet grasped. The land was far — maybe an hour's rowing, maybe more — but it was there. And August swam toward it.

By dawn the shore grew distinct: low hills, a dark band of vegetation, a strip of sand at the water's edge. He did not know what land this was. He did not know who lived here. He did not know whether they would receive him or kill him. But he knew he had to keep swimming. Because to stay in the water meant to die. And he did not intend to die.

When his feet touched the sand, his body went slack. He crawled ashore on all fours, like a beast, and fell on his back. The sand was warm, soft, and he lay looking at the sky, which grew lighter with every minute, listening to the waves run up the shore and fall back. Breath in, breath out. Breath in, breath out. A rhythm that does not deceive.

He lay watching the sunrise. The sun rose above the horizon — red, enormous, as if it too were weary and wanted to rest. The light touched his face, and August closed his eyes. His body hummed with fatigue, his arms trembled, his lips were chapped and cracked. But within — within there was quiet. Quiet as in the North before a snowfall. Not empty — quiet. As if every question had been asked and every answer given, and only one thing remained: to live on.

His father's sword lay beside him on the sand, gleaming in the rays of the rising sun. Bjorn's knife — at his belt, warm from his body. August laid his hand on the hilt of the sword, felt the familiar weight, and closed his eyes.

He was alone. But he was alive. And that was the main thing.
