The first days of the voyage were quiet, as though the sea had decided to give the travelers a respite before showing its true temper. The wind stayed fair and even, as though some unseen hand were gently steering the vessel, and the ship glided easily over the waves, leaving a long foamy wake behind. The sun rose over the water a pure, serene sphere, and the first rays slid across the deck, warming the planks that had grown cold overnight.

August quickly fell into the rhythm of life at sea: watch, work, sleep — everything went in a circle, like the tides. He helped the crew with the sails, mended ropes, scrubbed the deck until the wood shone pale. His hands grew used again to this work, to the smell of tar and salt, to the way a knot lies under the fingers exactly as it should, as though memory lived not in the head but in the fingers themselves. He grew used to the rolling of the deck underfoot almost at once: it turned out the body had not forgotten how to keep its balance in a world where the ground never stands still.

He felt his body remember what had been nearly forgotten after long months on land. The swell, the damp wind, the creak of the rigging — all of it returned like an old song known by heart. He woke before dawn, when the sky was still grey, almost colorless, and came out on deck to meet the sun over the water. In those morning hours, when the crew still slept and the sea was quiet and motionless, he felt almost free. Not free of obligations — free of himself. Of a past that would not let go but had ceased to strangle. In that quiet there was a place where heavy thoughts could be laid down and left, so that they did not stand in the way of breathing.

Sometimes, when the sun sank behind the horizon, staining the sky in deep shades of scarlet and violet, August took the guitar and played. The sailors listened in silence, sitting on deck; some leaned against the mast, some settled onto a coil of rope. Some nodded in time; some closed their eyes as though wanting to hold the melody inside. The music was quiet, flowing, like the water they sailed through. He played about what he had seen: the endless sea, the wind, the road. About the people left behind, and about those who waited ahead. Sometimes he noticed the crew falling silent as they listened, and in that silence there was more understanding than in any words. He noticed how the old sailor sitting by the side closed his eyes and swayed his head in time with the melody, as though remembering something long forgotten.

How the young man at the helm stopped watching the horizon and simply listened, and his face grew calmer, as though the music took away part of the anxiety the sea always carries.

"Where do you know that from?" the captain asked one day when August had finished playing. The song of the sea, drawn-out and a little sad, still hung in the air, mingling with the salt wind. "That song. I have heard it before, when I sailed the southern ports. It seems familiar, but I cannot recall where."

"I wrote it myself," said August. "On the island, when I was alone. Time moves differently there, and the words come on their own if you know how to listen for them."

The captain nodded without a word. But in his eyes there flickered respect — the kind sailors grant each other without unnecessary words, simply because a man knows the price of loneliness and can turn it into strength.

On the fourth day of the voyage the ship passed an archipelago. August stood in the bow and watched the islands drifting slowly past on the right, as though turning before him the pages of an old, almost forgotten book. They were familiar, recognizable to the smallest detail. He knew them at once — that very shore where he had spent a year in exile. Where he had built a house of branches and palm leaves, where fire had been his only companion at night. Where the guitar had fallen silent for long weeks and then sounded again — differently now, deeper, as though it had drunk in the noise of the wind and the cries of the gulls.

He recognized the outlines of the rocks, the dark patches of forest, the line of the shore where he had once gathered crabs and drunk from a stream. He did not know how much time had passed since then. But he felt that the island had remained in him, like a scar that no longer aches yet keeps reminding of itself. He thought of how he had survived where many would have died. And he remembered the day a ship appeared on the horizon. Matteo's ship. He remembered how at first he had not believed his eyes, how his heart had beat so hard it drowned out even the noise of the waves.

August told the crew about that place — briefly, without unnecessary detail, not of hunger and despair, but of how silence can become a teacher. He told of how he had learned again to listen: not to words but to the wind, not to people but to the sea. How music had returned to him not as amusement but as a way of staying afloat. The sailors listened in silence, watching the islands slowly vanish astern, as though they wanted to keep that shore in memory even for a moment.

Matteo came up, stopped beside him, leaned on the rail, and also watched the archipelago melt into the haze.

"Do you recognize the place?" he asked calmly, without pressure.

"Yes," said August, not taking his eyes off it. "I will never forget it."

"Do you think you have changed since then?" asked Matteo, and in his voice there was no curiosity but that quiet care which comes when one has known a person long enough.

August was silent for a long time, watching the island grow smaller until it became a dark sliver on the horizon.

"I have become different," he said at last. "And not only because I survived. But because I understood that I can be alone. And that is not frightening. I understood that even when there is no one near, you are not empty. You have a voice. You have songs. You have memory. That is enough to live."

Matteo did not answer. He simply stood beside him, looking at the sea, and in his silence August felt agreement — not in words but in something deeper, something understood only by those who have seen the horizon erase the border between fear and strength.

In the following days they were becalmed. The sea grew smooth as a mirror, reflecting the sky so exactly that the ship seemed to hang between two boundless expanses. The sails drooped as though tired, and the crew received the rare chance to rest. August sat on deck, ran his fingers over the strings, and composed a song about the archipelago. About how a man can be alone and not lose his mind. About how silence becomes a voice. He wrote the lines into his new book — the one with the white pages smelling of sea and leather. For the first time in a long while he felt the words settle onto the paper easily, without effort, as though someone dictated them to him in the whisper of the wind.

Sometimes he caught himself thinking that his fingers remembered those melodies even better than his memory did. They moved on their own, unsupervised, drawing out sounds he had not planned. He let them go where they wanted, and in that there was freedom — as though he ceased to be the author and became the conduit of something greater.

On the seventh day a storm burst upon them. It began suddenly, as often happens on the open sea: the wind rose within an hour, the sky darkened, and the horizon vanished in grey haze. The ship was thrown from side to side, waves rolled across the deck, flooding the planks and knocking down those who had not managed to hold on. August helped the crew: lashing the rigging, steadying the sailors, gripping the ropes so hard they cut into his palms. He did not panic. The North had taught him not to fear the elements — he knew fear was useful only when it made one more attentive, not when it robbed one of will. He knew what had to be done: hold on, work, not think about what might happen but only about what had to be done now.

He worked alongside the sailors, and they looked at him with respect — not as a guest but as one of their own, as a man who knew what the sea was and what fear was. He moved quickly but without bustle. The commands were clear, the movements precise. He knew when a rope must be hauled taut, when a sail must be taken in, when simply to wait — for in a storm sometimes the most important thing is to do nothing superfluous.

The storm died by morning, leaving behind a wet, gleaming world. The sea grew calm again as though the rage had never been, and the sky cleared, revealing stars that seemed especially bright after the dark. August stood on deck, soaked, exhausted — but alive. His clothes clung to his body, his hair was heavy with salt, but inside him spread a quiet pride — not for being a hero, but for not having given in. He had held out again. Had not given in again.

Matteo came up and clapped him on the shoulder — firmly, like a father.

"You held up well," he said. "Like a northerner."

August smiled, wiping his face with his sleeve, and felt the salt sting his skin.

"I am a northerner," he answered calmly. "Only from the wrong side."

They stood together, watching the sun rise over the sea, painting the water in golden bands. The wind was dying, and a new day was coming, clean and fresh, as though washed by the storm. August felt the road continuing. He did not know what lay ahead. Did not know whether he would meet those who could hear him, or remain alone with the cold and the silence. But he knew he was ready. Ready for the possibility that words might not help, and then deeds would have to speak. Ready for the road to be hard — but for it to be his road.

And he knew one thing more: he was not going for vengeance. Vengeance would have been easier. It would not have demanded so much patience, so much understanding, so many words. He was going for an answer. For the thing that would help him fit the shards of his past into something whole. Or at least understand why those shards had scattered at all.

The wind ruffled his hair, salt spray settled on his skin, and the ship carried him forward — to where the ridge pushed into the sky, where the sea grew wild and the land grew stern. Ahead was the North. Ahead was the road that had waited for him seventeen years. And now he walked it not as a fugitive, but as a man who had finally found where he needed to arrive.
