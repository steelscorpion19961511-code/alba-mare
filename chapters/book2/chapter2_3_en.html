In the morning he broke camp. The hare pelts, strapped to his belt, swayed slightly as he walked; the dried meat lay in his pack; there was water enough. The forest gradually thinned, yielding to open, rolling land covered with harsh, sun-scorched grass. The air grew drier, warmer, and his body adjusted to the new weather. Not without resistance — the fur cloak, rolled up on his pack, dragged at his shoulders, and his boots, made for snow and stone, baked on the heated ground. He knew that soon he would have to change his clothing. Change his habits. Change his rhythm.

He walked all day, almost without stopping. The road — if it could be called that — wound between low hills, looped around dried-up streams and sparse groves. Now and then he noticed traces — not of beasts, but of people. A hoofprint, old fire-rings, a shard of clay pottery. Someone had lived here. Someone had walked these paths. He did not turn toward the traces, but he remembered them. In the North he had learned: to remember is to live. To forget a path is to lose the way home.

By evening he came upon a small village.

It lay in a hollow at the foot of a hill and seemed almost grown into the earth — low, squat houses of stone and wood, with flat roofs where herbs and hides were left to dry. Smoke curled above the chimneys in thin, lazy wisps. Around it — fenced pens for livestock, a few carts, an old well. Everything was simple, sturdy and free of excess. He recognized a familiar logic here: this is how they build where resources are prized above ornament.

He stopped at the edge of the hill, looking down. He did not know who these people were. Did not know their language, their customs, their attitude toward strangers. But he knew he could not go on without trying to speak with them. Signe had once taken him in without words. Bjorn had once answered a nod instead of a greeting. Perhaps here, too, it was not words that worked, but gestures.

He descended slowly, openly, keeping his hands in view. The people noticed him at once — someone came out of a house, someone froze by the pen. They watched with a curiosity that held no fear. Only wariness, like that with which Bjorn had once studied his tracks in the snow, trying to make out what manner of man he was.

He stopped by the well and said the first word that came to mind:

“Hello.”

Silence. Then one of them answered — in a language he did not know. The sounds were soft, flowing, nothing like the guttural northern speech or the ringing southern tongue. He understood not a word. But he smiled — not broadly, but the way he had smiled in the North when he did not know what to say: calmly, openly, without challenge.

“I don't understand,” he said in his own language.

And silence again. Then one of them — an old man with a grey beard and a face scored with wrinkles — stepped forward and said something that sounded almost familiar. In a tongue he had once heard in the markets of Vistaria, a blend of southern dialects.

“Are you a trader?” the old man asked, slowly, searching for his words with difficulty.

“No,” August answered. “I am a wanderer.”

The old man looked at him for a long time. In his gaze there was neither hostility nor pity — only a calm weighing, like that of a man who has seen enough strangers not to be surprised, and is wise enough not to judge at first glance. Then he nodded and waved a hand, gesturing for him to follow. No words at all. Just a step to the side and a turn of the back — a sign of trust that needs no explaining.

He followed.

He was led to a small house at the edge of the village. Inside, it smelled of smoke, dried herbs and old leather. The smell was different, not northern, but there was something familiar in it: a dwelling where fire and food and people live side by side, in one space, and do not hide their warmth behind separate walls.

The old man indicated a place by the hearth, and a minute later a clay bowl of hot stew was set before him, with a piece of bread and a mug of water. A woman — probably his daughter or daughter-in-law — set down the food silently and stepped away, neither lingering with her gaze nor turning away.

He did not wait for an invitation. He took the bowl and began to eat — unhurriedly, but with respect for what he had been given. The hot food spread through his body, restoring strength. The stew was thick, with grain and chunks of meat, seasoned with some herb he did not know. The taste was new, but good. He ate, and with each swallow he felt his body relax, felt the tension of the road release his muscles.

The old man sat across from him and watched. He asked no questions, did not try to speak. He simply waited. That is how they waited in the North, giving a stranger time to come back to himself, not hurrying him toward answers he was not yet ready to give.

When he finished, he set the bowl on the table, took the hare pelts from his belt and laid them before the old man.

“For the food,” he said simply.

The old man looked at the pelts, then at August. He nodded — not in thanks, but in acceptance. That is what people do who understand that a gift is not payment but respect. August remembered how Signe had once nodded in exactly the same way when he brought her fish. The gesture was universal. No language was needed.

“Thank you,” said August.

The old man nodded.

“What is your name?” he asked in the same broken southern dialect.

“August.”

“Au-gust,” the old man repeated, trying the name by ear. “Where do you come from?”

He was silent for a moment. Then he said:

“From the North.”

The old man gave him a long look. Something flickered in his eyes — not surprise, but rather recognition, as if he had already heard of people who come from the North and are not afraid.

“Far.”

“Far,” August agreed.

They fell silent. The silence was not awkward — it was part of a conversation that had not yet begun. In the North, silence did not mean the absence of words; it meant the words had not yet ripened.

Then the old man asked:

“What are you looking for?”

He thought. He did not hurry. He remembered how the elder in the mountains had asked him almost the same question, and how he had answered then: “I am seeking understanding.” The words had been right for that place and that time. Here they would have been foreign.

“I am not seeking,” he said. “I am simply walking.”

The old man nodded again, as if this were the only right answer. Or the only one he was prepared to accept from a stranger.

Night fell on the village quickly, as it does in places where no mountains hold back the dusk. Darkness came whole, without transition, and with it came the cold — not the northern, piercing kind, but another: dry, quiet, slipping under one's clothing when the sun disappears. He went out of the house and found a place by the fire that burned in the center, between the houses. People sat around, murmuring softly in their own language, whose sounds recalled a stream babbling over small stones. They watched, but now without wariness. Rather with that quiet curiosity with which one looks at something new that has not yet decided whether it is dangerous or not.

He unslung the guitar. Took it out of the leather case. Once again, with it in his hands, he felt warmth return to him. Many days had passed since he had played. His fingers remembered, but the strings had grown unaccustomed, slackened by damp and changes of temperature. He began to tune them — slowly, listening to each sound, turning the pegs until every note rang true. It took time. No one hurried him.

The people watched the instrument with curiosity, but no one interfered. Some of the children came closer, examining the wooden body, the gleaming strings, the strange curve of the neck. August did not drive them off. Let them look. He had learned in the North: when people look without fear, that is already half of trust.

When the guitar was ready, he closed his eyes for a moment. He breathed evenly, feeling the night air fill his lungs, feeling the warmth of the fire touch his face. Then he began to play.

At first the voice was low, almost guttural — like wind humming in the crevices of rocks, like the breathing of an old forest. He sang in the northern tongue, rough, with a rasp, and in that voice one could feel the harsh, cold land where he had spent three winters. He sang of snow, of nights when no stars could be seen, of people who do not smile at strangers but give them a place by the fire. His fingers found the strings on their own, and the melody was less music than memory coming out into the open.

But then — without his noticing it himself — the voice began to change. It rose, grew softer, warmer. The words passed into the southern tongue, and lightness, airiness, an almost translucent quality appeared in them. He sang of the sea, of the sun, of the white walls of Alba-Mare, of how salt and wind smell, of the woman he had left, and of the city that had burned. The voice was like a crossing — from one world into another, from cold into warmth, from silence into sound. And somewhere in the middle, between north and south, a third melody was born — not northern and not southern, but simply human. About the road. About what a man feels when the ground beneath his feet keeps changing while the sky above his head stays the same.

The people listened in silence. Some closed their eyes; some stopped chewing. The old man sat by his house and watched August without looking away. The children froze, mouths open. Even the wind, it seemed, fell quiet, as if it too wished to listen.

When he finished, silence fell. Not empty, but full — as it is after rain, when the drops still hang in the air but no longer fall. Then someone said something quietly in their own language. And the old man, the one who had invited him, rose and said in broken southern speech:

“You are not just a wanderer,” he said. “You are one whom people listen to.”

August looked at him, not knowing what to answer. In the North they had called him “August Fjord.” Here, in this small village at the foot of the hill, they had given him another name. Perhaps not in words, but in the way they had listened. Perhaps that is how it should be: every place gives you a new name, and you wear them all, like rings on the trunk of a tree.
