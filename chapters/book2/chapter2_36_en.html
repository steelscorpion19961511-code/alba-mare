The fog held for two days — thick, impenetrable, as though the sea itself had decided to hide the shore from them, testing their patience and their will. There was that special quiet that exists only on the open sea, when even the cry of gulls cannot be heard — as though the birds were afraid to fly into that white emptiness. The ship moved slowly, feeling its way, almost on trust in the waves, and only the muffled, rhythmic sound of water against the hull reminded them they had not yet dissolved in that milky haze. Moisture settled on the skin, on the face, crept under the clothing, and in that damp, heavy wetness the breath of another world could already be felt — a world where cold was not merely weather but the master, to whom one must knock rather than burst in.

On the third morning the fog began to thin. First shadows emerged from the grey veil — dark, enormous, barely distinguishable, as though the backs of ancient guardians, covered with snow and ice, were rising from the water. Then came a pale, cold light breaking through the gaps, like a cautious glance from behind a half-open door. And then, when the fog finally dispersed, the shore opened before them.

It was distant but already discernible — a black-and-white band on the horizon: a land covered with snow and ice, stretching along the whole edge of the world, like the border between what August knew and what he had yet to learn. The shore was wild, stony, inhospitable, and ice floes floated along it, slowly swaying on the dark water. They rose and fell in time with the waves, like a herd of white beasts adrift on the open sea — mute, cold, indifferent. The air had become different: piercing, dry, almost ringing with cold, as though every breath passed through invisible icy needles. Breath escaped the mouth in clouds of vapor, and that vapor melted at once in the frosty air, not managing to become a memory.

August stood in the bow, peering at the line of the horizon, and felt everything within him draw taut, gather into a tight string — as happens when one has long awaited something and finally sees it. He did not know what waited there, beyond those cliffs, beyond that ice. But he knew he was ready. Not because he was unafraid, but because fear had already become part of him, like a scar that no longer aches yet reminds you who you are.

He stepped away from the side and went to the hold. There, on his sleeping place, lay the clothing he had bought in the Northern Port. August took off the light southern shirt, which now carried a whiff of something distant and almost unreal, and put on the woolen one, rough and warm, that smelled of smoke and sheep's wool — a smell that here was not a memory but a necessity. Over it — a dark cloak of dense cloth, with a fur collar that reliably guarded his neck from the wind, as though it embraced him and whispered: "Hold on." He tightened the belt, checked how his father's sword lay at his hip — not for beauty, not for threat, but as part of himself, as the voice of the past that must sound in this new world too. On his feet — tall boots lined with fur, which he had worn in the eastern North: heavy, reliable, knowing snow, ice and long roads. He fastened the last button, ran a palm over his sleeve — and suddenly understood: he was a northerner again. Not by blood, not by birth, but by choice, by the habit of meeting the cold face to face.

The crew fell still. The sailors came out on deck and silently watched the land approach with every beat of the waves, as though afraid to frighten off that moment. Even the captain, who had seen many lands and crossed the borders of worlds more than once, stood at the helm and did not take his eyes off the horizon. He gave no commands, changed no course — he simply led the ship forward, to where the new world began, and in that silence there was more meaning than in any words.

The ice floes grew larger, the water darker, almost black, as though it had drunk in all the cold of that land. The shore approached slowly, as though the land itself were deciding when to let them in, and that unhurried movement made the waiting almost unbearable. August stood by the side, holding the cold rails, and looked at the black cliffs, at the snow that lay on the summits, at the ice that bound the water near the very shore, turning it into an uneven mirror. The air touched his face, and he felt the frost penetrate his lungs, felt his muscles contract from the cold — but in that cold there was something dear, almost forgotten, like a voice heard many years ago and impossible to forget.

He felt how this world recognized him, how the air filled his lungs, how the cold touched his skin and seemed to test: "Are you still one who can be here?" And August understood that this was his place. Not because he had been born here, not because his forefathers had walked these stones, but because he had chosen it.

And that choice was not accidental — it had ripened in him over the years, through every loss, every meeting, every song, through every chord that sounded not for glory but so as not to fall silent.

Matteo came up, stopped beside him. The wind ruffled his grey beard, and his eyes watched the shore calmly, without bustle, as though he knew that what was happening now was not merely an arrival but a crossing. He silently looked at the land, and in that silence there was a quiet, warm understanding — the kind there is between people who have weathered more than one storm together.

"Are you ready?" Matteo asked, without turning to August, as though the answer was to sound not in words but in a state of being.

August turned to him. In the old trader's eyes there were no questions, no doubts — only that same understanding, dearer than any promises.

"I have always been ready," he answered calmly, and in those words there was no bravado — only truth.

Matteo smiled with the corners of his lips and nodded, as though he had heard exactly what he had expected to hear.

August turned again to the shore. The ice floes drifted past, white and cold, and he watched how they touched the ship's hull, how the water washed them, how they moved on into the unknown, leaving behind a thin ripple. Behind was the sea. Behind was Al-Sharan, noisy and bright. Behind were Sea Cliff and the ruins of Alba-Mare, full of memories and pain. Behind was his whole life up to this moment — with all its turns, meetings and losses. Ahead a new road began, and it was no brighter and no easier than the old one — but it was his own.

The ship moved forward, and with every stroke of the wave the shore grew closer, sharper, more real. August breathed that icy air, felt it scorch his throat and at the same time clear his thoughts, and understood: he had not merely arrived. He had returned to where he was fated to be.

When the shore was very close, he took the guitar from his shoulder — the one he had kept with him all this time, like a talisman, like a part of his soul. Not to play it now — but to feel its weight, its warmth, its silent promise: "I am with you." He ran his fingers over the wood, over the strings, and for a moment it seemed to him the guitar trembled slightly, as though it too recognized that shore.

Turning to Matteo, August said quietly:

"Thank you for bringing me here."

The old trader looked at him, squinting against the wind, and simply laid a hand on his shoulder — firmly, like a father, without needless words.

"Do not thank me," he rasped, outshouting the whistle of the wind. "I was merely carrying the one who was meant to get here."

And in those words there was so simple and so great a truth that August only nodded.

The shore was now very close. One could make out individual stones, cracks in the rocks, the snowcaps lying on the sharp ridges. One could hear the ice floes softly knocking against the hull, the waves whispering something in their ancient tongue. And when the ship finally slowed, preparing to drop anchor, August stood in the bow, looking straight ahead, and knew: beyond that shore there waited for him not merely a new land. A new chapter awaited him.
