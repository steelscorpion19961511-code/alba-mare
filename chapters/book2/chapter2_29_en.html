A few more weeks passed.

August told no one of his decision except Matteo. He kept working — helping at the quay, unloading ships, mending rigging, pulling knots tight as though each one had to hold not a rope but his own resolve. Sometimes he played at the Nanna in the evenings, and people listened as always — but now there was something new in his music: not longing, not farewell, but firmness, as though the strings had been drawn a little tighter and the voice had sunk a little deeper. A special unhurriedness had appeared in his movements, the kind a person has who knows he will soon leave: he did not hurry, because he wanted to accomplish everything that could be accomplished before departure.

He said farewell to the city in silence, without a word — even to himself. He simply walked the familiar streets, remembered the smells and sounds, folding them into a secret pouch of memory, where one keeps what is most precious. He remembered the smell of fried bread on the corner — warm, dense, with a note of cumin, as though it embraced him and said: "You were here. You lived here." He remembered the ringing of the bells on the camels — measured, calm, like the pulse of the city. He remembered the voices of children running between the stalls, their laughter, bright and light, like raindrops on a tin roof. He did not know whether he would ever come back. And he wanted to remember everything — to the last shadow, to the last glint of sun on the copper bowls.

One evening he came to Matteo again. They sat in the old trader's house, drank tea, and the silence between them was the kind that does not need filling: it was itself the conversation, long and honest. Matteo did not ask, did not hurry, did not try either to hold him back or to push him forward. He was simply there, and that was enough.

Matteo broke the silence first:

“Do you still want to go?”

August raised his eyes to him. The question hung in the air but did not waver. He had been waiting for this conversation and was ready.

“Yes,” he said simply, without pathos, without fine words. “Every day I spend here, I feel that I am putting off something important. I cannot stay at peace knowing that out there, in the west, there are answers I do not have. And without them, peace is not peace — just a silence in which you can hear something inside you refusing to let you breathe.”

“You know what awaits you there?” asked Matteo, and in his voice there was no attempt to dissuade, only care — heavy and calm, like an anchor holding a ship in a storm. “Cold, hunger, people who do not smile at strangers. And even if you speak their language — you are not one of them. You will be a stranger, however hard you try.”

August was silent for a long time, looking into his mug of tea. Steam rose above the surface, curled in thin threads, and dissolved into the air. He watched it, gathering his thoughts, and saw in that movement something familiar: how smoke rises into the sky, how sound dissolves into silence, how a person disappears on the road.

“I know,” he said at last, and his voice sounded even, without strain. “I understand it better than you think. In the eastern North I was a stranger too. And I too did not know their language at first, did not understand their ways, did not know how to look so as not to offend, or how to be silent so as not to seem weak. But I learned. I survived. And I became one of them. Not because I wanted to be one of them, but because I understood them. Understood why they do not look into the eyes, why they keep silence, why they value strength not in a shout but in a step.”

Matteo watched him attentively, not interrupting, not nodding, simply listening the way those who know the worth of words know how to listen.

“The western northerners do not want to trade,” August went on, and now a new note had appeared in his voice — not hope, but conviction. “I understand that. But if I come to them not as a trader, not as a southerner with a bag of gold and promises, but as a man who speaks their language, who knows their ways, who respects their land… perhaps they will at least listen to me. I do not need them to love me. I need them to stop killing. To stop coming in the night, setting fire to houses and leaving before dawn.”

Matteo nodded slowly, as though this answer were the only one possible, as though he had been waiting for these very words and could accept only these.

“And if they will not listen even to you?” he asked calmly, without drama — simply so that August would hear the question and answer it slowly, not for Matteo but for himself.

August looked at him with a steady gaze. In his eyes there was no fear, only a calm resolve, like that of a man who has already made his decision and is now simply waiting for the time when it can be carried out.

“Then I will find another way,” he said. “But I cannot stay here and pretend this is not my war. It is my war, Matteo. From the day I saw my father at the eastern gates, when the flame was already licking the walls and the screams drowned out even the wind — from that day it became my war. And I must end it in my own way. Not with a sword, if it can be done without one. But end it.”

Matteo was silent for a long time, looking at August. Then he drank his tea and set the mug aside, as though putting a period to an invisible conversation.

“In a month we set out,” he said simply, as of a matter already decided, with only the right wind left to wait for. “The ship is ready. I have gathered goods for exchange — things that may be of use to the northerners: grain, dried fruit, iron tools. Not to buy their friendship, but to show that we did not come empty-handed. There is only the wind to wait for.”

August raised his eyes to him, and in that gaze there was so much gratitude that it almost did not fit in the room.

“You really will come with me?” he asked quietly, as though afraid to frighten off that promise.

“I said I would take you to the shore,” Matteo answered. “And I keep my word. I will not make war on them, and I will not trade if they do not wish it. But I will bring you to the land and wait while you decide what to do next. And I will wait as long as needs be.”

“And if I do not return?” whispered August, and there was no cowardice in the words, only honesty: he wanted to know what would become of his name if the road proved longer than he thought.

Matteo looked at him with a long, warm gaze. In his eyes there was no fear — only the quiet, tired wisdom of a man who has seen much and knows that the world is not always fair, but is always worth telling about.

“Then I will tell your story,” he said calmly. “To all who wish to listen. So that they know that a man with a guitar and his father's sword went north to understand why people kill one another. And so that they remember your name. Not as a hero, not as a warrior, but as a man who could not live with a question that had no answer.”

August found no words. He simply looked at the old trader, feeling something warm spread inside him, almost forgotten — gratitude so strong that it was almost pain.

“I will return,” he said quietly but firmly, the way an oath is given that cannot be broken. “I will return, Matteo.”

The old man smiled, showing his grey beard, and in that smile there was no mockery, only tenderness for one who still believes that everything will be well.

“I know. You always come back. But do not forget: what matters to me is that you come back alive. No victories are needed. You are needed.”

He reached out and squeezed August's shoulder — firmly, like a father, so that the gesture held more than any words: a blessing, protection, and the promise that somewhere there is a place one can return to.

“That is the only thing I ask,” he added.

They did not say another word. Outside, night Al-Sharan clamored — somewhere there was laughter, somewhere singing, somewhere dishes rang, and that noise was like the heartbeat of the city, alive and real. August felt how this city, which had become almost his own, remained behind him, the way a shore remains behind when a ship puts out to sea. Ahead was the wind. Ahead was the North. And ahead was the road that had waited for him seventeen years.
