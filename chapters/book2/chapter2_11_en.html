The ship moved along the coast steadily, unhurried, as if it knew this shore as well as it knew its own deck. The wind was favourable, soft but insistent — it filled the worn sails and made them hum like the strings of an old lute. The sea remained calm but not quiet: it whispered, muttered, sometimes sighed with a heavy wave that rolled lazily against the hull and slid back down, leaving a thin veil of salt on the planks.

August fit into the crew quickly — not because he wanted to belong, but because he knew how to be useful. He helped with the cargo: heavy crates, tightly bound bales, barrels of water and wine — all of it had to be stowed properly so the ship would not lose its balance. He stood his watch, scanning the horizon where sky merged with sea into one endless line, and learned to read the shades of the water: a little darker meant the depth was changing; light ripples meant the wind was rising; a dark strip in the distance might be a storm — still a stranger, but already watching them from afar. He mended rigging alongside the old bosun, a silent man whose hands resembled gnarled roots: the man almost never spoke, but every movement was precise, measured, as if he knew the sea forgives no haste.

Space aboard was tight: narrow passages, low ceilings, hammocks slung in two tiers, the smell of tar, salt, damp cloth and human warmth. Years of wandering had taught August to live in tight quarters — taught him to feel part of any place the road brought him to, without becoming one of its own, yet without remaining a stranger. Here he was simply one more pair of hands, one more man who knew when to keep silent and when to act.

On the fourth day they entered a port. The town was small but lively: piers, warehouses, awnings under which merchants hid from the sun, a market where men shouted as loudly as if they meant to outshout the sea itself. It smelled of fish, tar, spices and nuts — and of a dozen other scents August had never known before. All of it blended with the sea breeze, which smelled different here: not of salt and seaweed but of something dry, warm, almost sweet, as if the wind had flown over endless savannas and carried their breath with it.

When the work aboard was done, the captain, passing by, clapped August on the shoulder and said: "You can rest, look around. We're waiting for a big caravan — twenty camels are to bring spices from the depths of the continent. Until the cargo arrives, there's nothing to be done."

August nodded, thanked him and went ashore. He walked the streets, past shops, past awnings, past shouts and laughter, past porters hauling sacks as though that were their sole purpose in life. Past children running carefree after one another, raising dust that settled at once on their bare feet. He walked and listened to the town: the creak of wheels, the ring of metal, scraps of talk in unfamiliar tongues, laughter, curses, prayers. But he did not stop. He wanted silence — the kind that exists only outside a town, among grass and wind, where noise fades to a whisper and then disappears altogether.

Beyond the town began the savanna — tall grass rustling in the wind, scattered trees with flat crowns, dry earth, hard as stone and yet alive, holding within it the remnants of the night's moisture. August walked toward it, feeling the wind touch his face, the sun warm his shoulders, the air grow cleaner and simpler. It smelled of dust, dry grasses and something tart found only in these parts — as if the land itself were older and wiser than the people who walked it.

He went farther and farther, until the noise of the port died behind him, replaced by the rustle of grass, the cries of birds and the distant roar of some beast — a sound that carried no threat, only a reminder: there is life here, and it is not always friendly.

And still he did not expect to meet predators so soon.

A pack of hyenas — three or four — came out of the tall grass when August had walked half an hour from the town. They moved without a sound, almost gliding along the ground, and watched him with cold interest, licking their lips as though they had already tasted him. August stopped. His hand went to the hilt of his sword of its own accord, fingers closing in the familiar grip, testing how easily the blade would leave its sheath.

The hyenas did not retreat. They fanned out in a half circle, as if they knew a lone man was easy prey. Their eyes were dark, attentive, calculating. August felt no fear — only the cold clarity that came to him before battle: the world grew sharper, sounds louder, movements slower.

He drew his sword. Steel rang, and the sound seemed to tear the silence, making the hyenas freeze for an instant. The first leapt first — swift, soundless, only the grass parting before it. August swung, and it fell, writhing in its death throes. The second lunged after it, aiming for his unguarded side. He met it with a short, precise blow — it dropped to the ground shrieking, and the sound was almost human, and for a moment it was bitter. The rest withdrew, vanishing into the tall grass, leaving behind only rustle and the feeling that they had not gone but merely gone to ground.

August stood breathing hard, looking at what they had left: trampled grass, dark patches of blood on the dry earth. He felt no triumph. Only weariness — the kind that comes not from physical exertion but from having had to take a life. He had not wanted to kill. But there had been no choice.

Having examined the carcasses, he decided not to let good things go to waste. The meat was a little tough but fit to eat, and the hides might be of use. Hoisting one carcass onto his shoulder and dragging the other behind him along the ground, August returned to the port. The way back took longer than he had reckoned: the sun beat down, sweat stung his eyes, his body hummed with strain. But he walked, step by step, refusing to let himself stop.

Aboard, the crew met him with surprise but no unnecessary questions. Someone clapped him on the shoulder; someone silently helped him unload the carcass. August butchered both, skinned them and gave the meat to the cook. That evening the crew had fresh hyena for supper — a little tough but filling, spiced with the smell of the savanna, which mingled with the smell of the sea and made the taste strange, almost wild. And August hung two tawny hides from the rigging to dry. They would come in handy — for warmth on cold nights, for bedding so as not to freeze on deck, and as a reminder that even in a foreign land he could survive.

In the evening August sat by the rail, watching the dark sea and listening to it breathe: a slow, heavy inhalation, a short exhalation, another inhalation. Waves beat softly against the hull as if trying to tell him something. For the first time in a long while he felt himself not merely a survivor, but a man who knew what to do next. Not because he had a plan, but because he felt the ground beneath him again — even if that ground was a swaying deck and not solid earth. He had learned to be flexible like those waves: to take the blows and rise again.

Above the sea the first stars kindled, bright and cold, like shards of ice scattered across the black velvet of the sky. August watched them and thought how strange the world was made: somewhere, right now, people sat by their hearths drinking hot tea and telling one another stories, while he was here, on a ship's deck, listening to the whisper of the waves and feeling weariness give way, little by little, to calm. Tomorrow there would be work again, the sea again, new shores again. And he would be ready.
