Half a year had passed.

August was no longer the man who had stepped ashore in Al-Sharan with only a guitar and his father's sword — as if his whole former world had fit into those two things. Now he had lodging, work, a place at the Nanna tavern, and money he almost never spent: it accumulated in a small leather purse, as if waiting for its hour. He helped Matteo, played in the evenings, and in his free time wandered the city, drinking in its sounds, smells, light — and each time found something new in it: how the shadow of a minaret falls on the pavement exactly at noon, how at the market people argue over price as if it were not haggling but a song, how at dusk the city grows softer and seems to open itself to those who know how to look.

Al-Sharan had become almost native. But inside, somewhere deep, beneath the ribs, an unease began to stir — quiet but persistent, like a wind that first barely stirs the leaves and then tears them from the branches. August felt that he had said everything to this city that he wanted to say. The songs began to repeat themselves. New ones were not being born — or if they were, they were pale, like shadows of real melodies. It seemed to him that he was singing the same thing, and the city, though it listened, already knew those notes by heart.

One evening he sat by the window, watching the sunset, when the sky turned like molten gold and the shadows stretched and merged into one another. And suddenly he understood: time to pack. Not because the city had ceased to be kind, but because that kindness had become habitual — and what is habitual no longer feeds the soul.

The next day August went to the market — to the place where everything can be found if one knows where to look and how to listen to the whisper of the counters.

First — boots. Sturdy, of camel leather, with a thick sole that feared neither sand nor stone nor the sharp shards that hide in the shadows of lanes. He tried them on, walked on the cobblestones, listening to how they creaked and how they received his step. He felt his feet grow used to the new, firm support, as if they themselves decided: yes, one can go far with these.

Then — khaki trousers, dense but breathing, that kept one from sweating even under a blazing sun. He already had a shirt — light and simple — but a cloak... The cloak mattered. August wandered a long time between the stalls until he found what he sought: a light, warm poncho-cloak of camel wool that could be thrown over the shoulders in cool evenings and nights. It smelled of wood and smoke — the way things smell that have lived long beside fire and road. August threw it over his shoulders, felt how the cloth lay along his back, and understood: this was what was needed.

He bought a keffiyeh too — a long, thin scarf with which to cover head and neck from sun and dust. He tied it about his head, feeling how the cloth shielded from wind, how it became a second skin, concealing and protecting.

The traveling sack turned up at an old leather-worker's — sturdy, the color of desert sand, with a wide mouth and long straps for carrying on the shoulders. Inside it was roomy enough to hold everything necessary, and August packed each thing carefully, checking that all lay tight and made no sound in walking: the silence of a step is also part of the road.

He bought a small pot with a lid, a fire-striker, and several leather waterskins — heavy but reliable, such as would not burst from heat nor leak from dew. All this he packed into the sack, tried it on his shoulders, took a few steps through the market, listening to himself: did anything hinder, pull to one side, rattle needlessly.

Then — the leather-worker, for the sheath. The old one, which he had worn from the very beginning, had long since come apart: the leather had cracked, the seams split, the metal darkened. He ordered a new one of dark, dense leather, with a metal fitting at the tip — so the sword would not snag or scratch. When he took it in his hands, it was stiff and unyielding, as if it did not yet know whom it would serve. But August knew: in a few days it would take the shape of the sword, become part of his movement, part of his step. He chose a new belt too, with a buckle in the shape of a sun — bright but not shouting, such as warms the eye without dazzling it. He buckled it on and felt how surely and comfortably the sword lay at his hip — as if these months of rest had never been, and the road had always been somewhere near, waiting for its hour.

But the most important thing — the case for the guitar. August searched long for a craftsman who would understand that a guitar is not simply wood and strings but a voice, which must not be dropped, must not be scratched. At last he found one. The man chose dark, almost black leather, with a soft wool lining inside that would not scratch the wood and would not let the instrument tremble on the march. The edges of the case were stitched with thick thread, securely and firmly, and on the outer side the master pressed a thin ornament — a wave passing into wind. August ran his finger over the pattern and felt the memory of sea and road return to him, as if the wave whispered: "You remember. You are ready."

The last stop — a bookshop. Small, almost unnoticeable, in the depths of one of the lanes where those looking only for wares rarely strayed. Inside it smelled of old paper and leather, and that smell was like the smell of time — astringent, thick, as if thousands of stories had congealed in it. August went on sorting through books until he found what he sought: a blank book in a dark-brown leather binding. The paper inside was soft as silk, snow-white as the walls of Alba-Mare, and when he ran a finger over the page it answered the touch — quietly, almost inaudibly, but perceptibly. Beside the book lay a glass flask of black ink — August knew they were made from cuttlefish, and they smelled of the sea and of depth. With the flask came a stick with an iron tip instead of a quill — not for beautiful letters, but for those written on the move, between tasks, between steps.

He paid, packed everything into the traveling sack, and went out into the street. Evening Al-Sharan glowed gold in the rays of sunset, and the stones that had drunk in the sun during the day gave it back, as if unwilling to part with its warmth. August stood in his new cloak, with the new sword at his belt, with the guitar in its new case, and felt anticipation waking within him — quiet, deep, as before dawn, when night still holds on to the horizon but already knows it is time to go.

He was ready to walk again. Not because he had nothing more to say to this city — he had said much to it. But because now he had something to hear in other places: in the noise of unfamiliar markets, in the silence of new parks, in the voices of people who do not yet know his songs. And he knew: somewhere ahead a melody waited that he had not yet heard, and it would be the most important one.
