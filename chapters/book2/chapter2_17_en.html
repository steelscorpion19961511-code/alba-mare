He did not at once understand what he was seeing.

At first — simply a point on the horizon. He had seen many such points in all the time he had spent here. Each time his heart stood still, but then the point vanished, dissolved in the haze. He had learned not to trust his eyes.

But this one did not vanish.

He stood on the shore and watched it grow. Slowly, but inexorably. The outlines grew sharper: sails, hull, high sides. The ship was heading straight for the island.

He did not believe it. He was afraid to believe. But he could not take his eyes off it.

The ship came nearer, struck its sails, dropped anchor. A boat put off from it — small, with four men aboard. He stood motionless, watching them. Everything within him trembled, but he did not move.

The boat touched the sand. The men leapt ashore and looked around. They were dressed simply, in seaman's fashion: shirts, trousers, boots. One had a short cleaver at his belt, another a rope. They looked weary, but alive.

One of them, a short man with a weathered face, noticed August. Noticed his wasted body, the overgrown face, the bare feet, the sword in his hand. They looked at each other for several moments.

"Do you live here?" the sailor asked. His voice was wary, but not hostile.

"Here," August answered. His voice was hoarse — he had not spoken with people for almost a year.

The sailor exchanged glances with the others. They said something quietly to one another in their own tongue.

"Are you alone?" he asked again.

"Alone."

"What is your name?"

He was silent. He looked at them, at the boat, at the ship behind it. He did not know whether to trust them. He did not know what they would do if he told the truth. He did not even know whether he wanted to leave this island. But he knew he could not stay.

"My name is Fjord," he said.

The name sounded firmer than he expected. It was not his first name. It was the one he had chosen. The one the wind had given him. And now he pronounced it before strangers, as if testing whether it still sounded the way it had then, on the northern shore.

The sailor nodded, as if the name meant nothing to him. He looked him over once more and noticed the sword August still gripped in his hand.

"Leave the sword," he said. "You won't need it aboard."

August looked at him for a long time. Then lowered the sword and laid it on the sand. He stepped back, but did not take his eyes off the blade.

"My father's sword," he said quietly. "Take it with you. I cannot leave it here."

The sailor looked at the sword, then at August. Something flickered in his gaze — perhaps understanding, perhaps respect. He agreed, picked up the sword and handed it to one of his men.

"It will be aboard," he said. "Come. There is nothing for you here."

August stepped forward. His feet trod the sand heavily, uncertainly. He was not used to walking on level ground without branches and stones underfoot. He came to the boat, stepped over the side, and sat on the thwart, feeling his body tremble with strain.

Beside him lay the guitar in its leather case. The case was old, worn, and after a year on the island the leather had almost crumbled — cracks covered it, the edges had frayed, the straps hung by a thread. But inside there was still the guitar. Mute, with an empty, stringless neck — but alive.

He pressed it to his chest as he had never pressed anyone. He did not look at the island, which slowly disappeared astern. He looked at the guitar, at its cracked wood, at the strings that were no more. And he held it as though it were the only thing that remained.

The boat pulled slowly away from the shore. He sat on the thwart, pressing the guitar to his chest, and watched the water. It was transparent, blue-green, and the clouds were reflected in it. He watched and felt time slow, felt every stroke of the oars echo in his body.

The rowers worked evenly, without fuss. They did not look at him, did not say a word. They simply rowed, measured and calm, as they had done a thousand times. The boat moved softly, swaying on the waves, and his body gradually accustomed itself to the motion — the one it had sought so long.

The island grew smaller. First it was green, then grey, then simply a line on the horizon. He watched it and felt no regret. He did not want to remember it. He wanted to forget.

The guitar lay on his knees, and he felt its weight. The old leather case was falling apart in his hands, but August did not loosen his grip. He held it as though it were the only thing linking him to the man he had been.

The boat drew up to the ship. The side was high, dark, with peeling paint and the traces of time. A rope ladder was lowered from above. One of the rowers gestured that he should climb.

August rose, feeling his legs tremble with weakness. He had not eaten properly for several days. He tucked the guitar under his arm, took hold of the rope, and began to climb. Each step cost him effort, but he did not stop.

When he clambered over the side and set foot on deck, he felt solid wood beneath his feet. The ship smelled of tar, of sea, of old timber. Somewhere gulls cried, sails flapped, rigging rang. The world was noisy, alive, real.

He stood on deck, breathing hard, looking around. Sailors passed by without noticing him. Someone tossed him a piece of bread, someone a mug of water. August took them without a word and ate, feeling the food restore him to life.

Then he noticed a man at the stern. He stood at the helm, looking ahead, holding the course. He was old, grey, with a weathered face. But his bearing was the same as it had been many years before.

August recognized him at once. The ship with the carved ornaments. The very one that had stood in the port of Alba-Mare. And the man at the helm — Matteo.

He did not know how to speak to him. What to say. Perhaps Matteo had already forgotten that boy? So many years had passed. August stood and watched him, unable to move.

Matteo did not see him. He looked at the sea, peering at the horizon. August watched him and felt time contract, past and present merging into one long, warm moment.

He said not a word. He simply stood at the rail, holding the guitar in his arms, and waited. He knew: there would be time yet.

Matteo turned to give an order and noticed August. For a moment he froze, taking in the wasted face, the overgrown beard, the bare feet. Then his gaze fell on the old leather case with the guitar — almost crumbling in the stranger's hands.

"You're from the island?" he asked hoarsely, without much curiosity — more out of politeness.

"Yes," August answered.

"Been there long?"

"I don't know... Maybe a year. Maybe more."

Matteo said nothing, unsurprised. He turned his gaze to the receding shore.

"We stop here for water," he said. "The only place on the route where supplies can be renewed. Without this island we would never reach the next port. We've been here before. But to find someone..." He shook his head. "For the first time."

He looked at August again, and something flickered in his eyes — curiosity, perhaps, or the old habit of not letting strange encounters slip away.

"What is your name?"

August looked at him. Old, grey, weathered. The man who had once given the guitar. The man who had promised to return and had not returned. But he felt no resentment. Only a quiet, long gratitude.

"My name is Fjord," he said.

Matteo nodded, and the name meant nothing to him. He waved a hand toward the hold.

"Rest. When you've come back to yourself — we'll talk."

August obeyed the captain's word and, pressing the guitar to his chest, went in the direction the old man had indicated. He did not know whether Matteo would ever recognize him. He did not know whether he would tell him who he was. But he knew: this was his new step.
