The ship had been sailing northwest for several days. The wind was even, the sky changeable. Sometimes the sun broke through the clouds, sometimes a drizzle fell. August was growing accustomed again to life aboard: to the roll, to the crowding, to the smell of tar and fish. The sailors treated him calmly, without needless curiosity. They could see he had survived on the island, and that was enough to keep them from asking questions.

He worked, helped about the ship, mended rigging, carried water. Sometimes he simply stood at the rail and watched the sea, listening to it breathe. The guitar lay in a corner of the hold, in its old case, which had almost crumbled. He did not take it out. Not yet.

Matteo sometimes came by. They spoke little — of the weather, of the wind, of how many days to the next port. August did not speak of himself. Matteo did not ask. But between them there was something that needed no words.

On the fifth day of the voyage, rain began. Not heavy, but steady, persistent, falling all day. Toward evening it turned into a downpour. The crew gathered under the awning at the stern — the only place to shelter from the water. Some slept, some played dice, some simply sat watching the rain.

August sat in a corner, his back against the wooden partition. The guitar lay beside him. He looked at it a long time, then took it in his hands. The case had all but fallen apart; the leather cracked and flaked. He opened it and took out the instrument.

The wood was dry but cracked. There were no strings — all had snapped on the island. But he took the guitar in his hands and ran his fingers along the neck, feeling the wood answer his touch. Then he began to play. Without strings. Simply moving his fingers along the fretboard, reproducing the movements he knew by heart. It was music no one could hear, but which he felt within.

One of the sailors noticed and smirked. August paid him no attention. He kept playing, until at last he began to speak.

He began to sing. His voice was hoarse after the long silence, but it gradually warmed and gathered strength.

He sang in the southern tongue — of the desert, of the rocks, of the reefs, of solitude on an uninhabited island. The people listened in silence. Someone opened his eyes. Someone put the dice away.

He sang of how life had led him through the north, through ice and cold. How he had found himself in the east, among sands and caravans. And how all of it had brought him back — aboard the old ship that had once stood at the white walls of Alba-Mare.

Then his voice grew quieter, and he sang of what had happened many years ago. In the square, in the white city by the sea. How an old merchant had given a guitar to a boy of thirteen. How he had said: "Learn to play so that at least one person weeps or smiles when they hear you." How he had promised to return — and had not returned.

Under the awning it grew quiet. Everyone looked at him. He did not look at Matteo. He looked at his own hands, at the neck that remembered every movement.

When he finished, someone let out a soft breath. Someone lowered his head. Matteo sat motionless, looking at the floor. He said not a word.

Several moments passed. Then he raised his head and looked with a long, searching gaze.

"Where did you hear that song?" he asked quietly.

August smiled with the corners of his mouth. He looked at the old man, at his grey hair, at his hands that had once held that very guitar.

"I wrote it," he said. "Back when you gave me the guitar. I was thirteen. I waited for you for three years."

Matteo looked at him without breathing. In his eyes were reflected the rain, the light, and a long, long silence.

More than seventeen years had passed. But he recognized.
