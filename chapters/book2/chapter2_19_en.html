The ship moored at a long stone pier. August stood at the rail and looked at the city that opened before him. Al-Sharan was enormous — houses of pale stone, tall minarets, noisy markets, hundreds of ships at the quays. As large as Vistaria, but different — more open, more sunny, more spice-scented. The air here smelled unlike anything else: of salt and spices, of the smoke of braziers and fresh herbs, and the smell seemed to slip beneath the skin at once, restoring the feeling of being alive.

Stepping ashore, August felt the solid ground sway beneath his feet. He was not used to it after the long voyage: every step seemed strange, too confident, too heavy. His feet had, it seemed, forgotten what it meant to tread on an unmoving surface. Matteo came up and laid a hand on his shoulder — firmly, fatherly, without unnecessary words.

"Come," he said. "You need to come back to yourself."

He led August to a small house near the port itself — modest but cosy, with low ceilings and thick walls that kept their coolness even on the hottest day. Inside it smelled of wood, herbs and old leather. Matteo pointed to a room with a bed and clean linen.

"Rest. Supper in an hour."

August undressed, lay down and closed his eyes. How long he slept he did not know. Maybe an hour, maybe the whole night. He woke when dawn was already showing outside the window, and the first rays of the sun lay on the floor in thin golden bands. On a chair lay clean clothes; beside it stood a bowl of hot stew, steaming, fragrant with cumin and garlic. He ate slowly, feeling the food restore his strength, feeling the warmth spread through his body and drive out that icy weariness which had worked itself into his bones over the months on the island.

A few days later Matteo took him to a hammam. The smell of damp stone, of oil, of herbs — familiar, soothing, as if from some distant childhood. August sat in the steam room, feeling the heat enter his body, feeling it release the tension he had carried in himself for months, as if the very air were washing the island's salt and sand out of him. He closed his eyes, allowing himself simply to be: not to think, not to remember, not to fear.

Then came the shaving. The barber worked in silence, drawing the blade carefully over the skin, taking from August not only the beard but part of the weight he had carried. August looked in the mirror and for the first time in a long while saw his face without a beard. It seemed older than before, deeper, as though every line held its own story. But it was he — not a ghost from the island, not a shadow of the past, but a living man.

Several weeks passed. August worked for Matteo: helped with the cargo, carried sacks, mended rigging. The sailors treated him with respect — they knew what he had lived through, and that was enough. Matteo did not burden him with unnecessary work, but did not spare him either: he gave exactly enough for August to feel needed, useful. August liked working with his hands — feeling the weight of the rope, the heaviness of the sack, the resistance of wood under the knife. It brought him back to life better than any words.

One evening they sat in Matteo's house by the hearth, and August began to tell. He spoke for a long time — of how he had received the guitar, of how his father had taken him into the guard, of how he had found the guitar again seven years later. Of the fire, of his father, of his travels, of meeting his mother. Of life in the North, of the East, of the island. The words flowed slowly, as if he were afraid to spill them, and yet he wanted them to sound aloud at last, to be heard by someone. Matteo listened in silence, without interrupting. He sat in his chair, looking into the fire, and only now and then nodded, as if confirming: I am here, I hear you. When August finished, the old man was silent for a long time. Then he said:

"You have passed through what few have passed through. And you are still here. You did not give in."

August answered nothing. Words were not needed. There was more understanding in that silence than in any consolation.

Several more months passed. August worked well, and Matteo saw it. One morning he came up and said:

"I want to repay you for your work. Come with me."

He led August into a small alley hidden from the noisy streets. It was quieter here; it smelled of shavings and resin, and even the cries of the merchants came through muffled, as if the city did not dare intrude upon this place. In the alley stood a workshop — low, with a wooden door and a small window from which came the smell of shavings and resin. Matteo opened the door and went inside.

The workshop was cramped. Tools hung everywhere; pieces of wood, strings, pegs lay about. At a table sat an old man whose hands were covered with calluses and scars. He raised his eyes, looked first at Matteo, then at August. His gaze was calm, attentive, as if he could see not only a face but what hides behind it.

"Master," said Matteo. "This man has earned a new guitar. Make him a true one."

The master looked at August for a long moment, as if trying to guess what sound must live in the wood that would become the instrument. Then he nodded.

"Tell me what you need," he said.

August closed his eyes for an instant. He thought of the North, where the wind draws from the air every superfluous sound, leaving only a hum and the cold. Of the desert, where silence rings like a taut string. Of the ocean, which knows how to sing in many voices — now softly, now furiously. Of how sound can be cold and warm at once, like ice and sun.

"Deep," he said. "So that the voice comes from the very bottom, from the very heart of the wood. But so that the upper strings sound light, like water in the sun. So that the guitar can speak, and whisper, and sing. So that it remembers everything I have seen, and can tell it without words."

The master listened without interrupting. He ran a finger along the wooden blank, as if testing its breath, listening for what the wood was ready to give.

"I know such wood," he said. "It grows in the mountains east of here. Dry, dense, with a thin layer beneath the bark. It fears no damp. It sounds long."

He rose and went into the depths of the workshop. There, on the wall, hung several pieces of timber — dark, grained like old maps on which one could read the history of winds and rains. The master took down one piece and laid it on the table before August.

"This is your guitar," he said. "If you agree."

August ran his hand over the wood. It was smooth, warm, almost alive. His fingers found their way along the grain as if along a melody he had once heard and was now remembering. There was something native in the touch, as if the wood had been waiting for him all this time.

"I agree," said August.

The master nodded, sat down at the bench. He took out a knife, chisels, glue. He worked in silence, and August watched his hands. They moved slowly but confidently, as if they had done this a thousand times, and every movement was precise, measured, free of fuss. August saw a form being born, saw the wood yield, saw a curved soundboard appear, a neck, frets. In every curve there was its own logic, its own beauty, and it seemed the master was not so much carving an instrument as freeing it from captivity in the wood.

Matteo stood by the door and did not interfere. Now and then his glance passed from the master to August, as if he wanted to remember this moment — how something new is born from silence and labour.

"It will take time," said the master, not raising his eyes. "A month, maybe more. The wood must grow accustomed to the form, must learn to hold a sound. You cannot hurry wood — it answers only those who know how to wait."

"I will wait," said August.

Leaving the workshop, he felt something rise within him that he had not felt in a long time. Patience. And hope. It was not the hope that shouts and demands, but a quiet, calm one, like a dawn that comes whether you await it or not. He did not think about what would come next. He simply looked at his hands and knew: soon they would touch the strings again, and the world would speak to him again in a language he understood.

Sometimes he sat on the shore, gazing at the sea, feeling the wind touch his face. Behind him roared Al-Sharan — enormous, warm, foreign. But now it no longer seemed foreign. Here there was a home. There was work. There was someone waiting. And there was a guitar that would soon sound, and in its sound all the roads he had travelled would merge together.
