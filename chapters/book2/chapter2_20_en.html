A month had passed.

August worked without complaint, without asking when his new instrument would be ready. He helped Matteo with the cargo, carried sacks, retied bundles, mended rigging. Sometimes he went along to the market, helped with the trade, learned the prices, grew used to the language, to the people, to the smells.

The city lived its own life — loud, motley, flowing. From morning on, the markets filled with the cries of traders, the ring of coins, laughter and haggling. It smelled of fried meat, fresh bread, spices, and something else he could not name but remembered all the same: the smell of sun-warmed stone, of dust mixed with cinnamon, of smoke from the braziers that drifted over the stalls as though it were part of the conversation. By day the streets were full: camels, donkeys, carts, children darting between the grown-ups as if trying to outrun the bustle itself. In the evenings — the light of oil lamps, music, singing, voices blending with the murmur of the sea.

He fit into that rhythm. He did not look for himself in it, did not try to understand it — he simply was a part of it. He woke before dawn, while the city was only beginning to stir, as though shaking off sleep. He breakfasted on flatbread with yogurt and spiced greens, went to Matteo, worked, came back, had supper, and went to bed. Each day resembled the last, but in that repetition there was its own truth. He did not think of the past. He simply lived.

And waited.

One morning Matteo came up on the pier, put a hand on his shoulder, and said:

"Come. Your instrument is ready."

Something trembled in his chest, but August gave no sign. He only nodded and followed.

The workshop met him with the smell of oil, shavings, and dry wood — warm, almost homey, as if the room itself knew that something important was going to happen today. The master sat at the table. Before him lay a guitar — new, untouched by anyone else's hands.

August froze.

It was made of dark honey-colored wood, with grain that shifted softly from light to shadow, as if gleams of sunset had frozen in it. The body was a little narrower than the old one's, but deeper, with a gentle curve that lay against the hand as if made for his fingers. The neck was warm and smooth, as though it had been polished for long days so that the wood would learn to accept a touch. On the top there was a carved ornament: a thin line like a wave passing into wind. August recognized it. He had asked for it himself. The wave — a memory of the tattoo on his right arm. The wind — of what lay ahead.

Silently the master slid the guitar to the edge of the table. August reached out and touched it. The wood was alive — warm, dry, almost breathing. His fingers ran along the neck, over the frets, over the strings — thin, silver, strung with the precision only a hand that knows instruments can feel. In that tension was silence, gathered into sound.

"Try it," said the master.

August sat on a chair by the wall and laid the guitar across his knees. For a moment he closed his eyes. It smelled of wood, of resin, of something ancient and living, as though it held within it the smell of forests and mountains where this tree had grown. He could hear the city sounding behind the wall, the gulls crying, the water touching the stones, and all of it merged into one even hum, against which the first chord had to sound.

And he touched the strings.

The sound was deep and velvety; it did not fade but went inward somewhere, like the breathing of an old forest. Each string answered in its own voice, and together they sounded as one. August did not begin a song at once — he simply ran his fingers over the strings, listening to the guitar speak to him. It was different. Not better, not worse — simply his. He felt the wood answer to his fingers, felt the sound being born out of silence and returning into it, as though leaving a trace.

He played a few chords — simple, almost timid, like the first words after long silence. And stopped. The silence was complete but not empty: the sound still trembled in it, as if the air went on vibrating.

The master watched without taking his eyes away. Matteo stood by the door with his arms crossed. He was not smiling, but in his eyes was something August had not seen before — pride, quiet and restrained, like that of a man who knows the price of patience.

"Are you pleased?" asked the master.

August looked at the guitar. At the warm honey wood. At the wave and the wind carved into the soundboard. At the silver strings that caught the light of the oil lamp, as if they kept a little of that light in themselves.

"It is mine," he said.

The master nodded, as if that was all he had been waiting for.

They went out into the street. The sun was already sinking toward evening, and the city was flooded with golden light that made even the dust beautiful. The noise — the very one August had grown used to — sounded like music: voices, creaking wheels, cries of gulls, the splash of water. He walked the streets holding the guitar close, and felt how it settled into his hands, as if it had always been his, as if all these years it had simply been waiting for him to be ready to hear it again.

At the corner of the market, under an awning, August stopped. He sat down on a stone step, laid the guitar across his knees, and began to play. Not a song — just sound. Quiet, tentative, learning. People passed by; some slowed their step, some turned their heads, some simply stopped hurrying for a moment. August did not look at them. He simply played, letting his fingers remember what the body remembered even when the mind tried to forget.

And in that sound, in that city, in that evening, he felt himself beginning to come back. To himself. To life. To that part of himself he had left in the North, scattered in the deserts, sought on the islands. And it answered from the depths of the honey-colored wood — not with a cry, but with an even, calm voice that at last could be heard again.
