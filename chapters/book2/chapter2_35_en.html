The ship sailed along the southern shore for several more weeks. The weather changed rapidly, as though the land itself could not decide which face to turn to the sea: now the sun scorched the deck so that the planks grew hot to the touch, now slanting rain poured down, washing away the salt and leaving behind a sharp smell of wet wood, now fog rolled in — thick, milky, it wrapped everything around, making the world unsteady and almost unreal, as though the ship were sailing not over water but through someone's old, half-erased memories. August had grown used to these changes of nature's moods. He knew the north lay ahead, and that every day brought him closer to what he had set out for.

One morning the captain came up to August as he stood by the side, peering into the grey line of the horizon, and, without raising his voice, so as not to frighten off the fragile silence of dawn, announced:

"In a day we will reach the Northern Port. It is the last large settlement on the southern coast before the open sea and the lands of the western North. Farther on, the royal laws end and the winds begin, which only the sea and the stones know."

August nodded, without taking his eyes off the horizon. He did not expect beauty or comfort from this place. He expected truth from it.

He stood in the bow, watching the shore gradually approach. First it was simply a dark band; then it began to acquire details: the sharp angles of roofs, the heavy silhouettes of warehouses, the stretched shadow of a lighthouse that did not burn by day but even without fire seemed a guard who never took his eyes off the sea. The town was small, built of grey stone and dark wood, darkened by salt and time. It was not beautiful like Al-Sharan, where the streets rang with music and silk, and not noisy like Sea Cliff, where every voice contended with the wind. In it there were neither rich houses, nor tall minarets, nor gilding. It was a stern, squat port that survived rather than lived. The houses huddled against one another, as though that made them warmer, and the roofs were slanted at such an angle that the wind did not tear them off but slid over, leaving the town in peace.

But in it there was a particular quiet of its own — not empty but gathered, like a bowstring before the shot. And suddenly August thought that it was here that everything began in earnest. Here the South ended not gradually but abruptly, the way a rope breaks if it is cut through with an axe.

The ship docked at a wooden pier whose boards were so old they seemed part of the sea bottom — darkened, swollen, covered with the green film of algae. August came ashore first, pausing for a moment to feel how the boards sprang under his boots, and breathed in the air of the Northern Port. It was different. There was no smell of spices and flowers here, as in the south. Here it smelled of smoke, wet wool, cold sea and tar soaked into the wood over decades. In the air hung that special dampness that comes only before a long winter: it settled on the skin, crept under the collar, made one wrap the cloak tighter. And in that air there was a cold that had not yet come but already stood at the threshold, listening for footsteps.

He walked the narrow streets, studying the houses. They were built as though their makers wanted to say to the wind: "You may blow, but you will not break us." The windows were small, so as not to let the heat out; the shutters thick, so as not to let the storm through. On the walls here and there hung bundles of dried herbs, bunches of roots, talismans of bone and iron — not for beauty, but to keep the cold at a distance.

August stopped at a small market located not far from the pier, under a canopy of tarred canvas. They did not sell jewels or fine cloth here. Here they sold goods necessary for those setting out on a long journey or simply wanting to survive the winter: heavy cloaks with fur collars, woolen blankets, knives with broad blades, axes with sturdy hafts, furs, rough mittens, caps of thick wool. The traders did not shout, did not lure customers — they stood calmly, knowing that the one who needed would come up himself. Their faces were weathered, their hands calloused, their gazes direct, without needless bustle.

Matteo came up and stood beside him, looking not at the goods but at August.

"Here we will make our last stop," he said calmly. "Beyond — the open sea and the western North. I advise you to stock up on everything that may be of use. And buy warm clothing. Where you are going, warmth is not awaited. It comes rarely and briefly, and it always leaves too soon."

August nodded. He knew what cold was. He remembered how frost squeezed the lungs, how the wind pierced to the bone, how snow blinded the eyes. He remembered the eastern North, where the cold was sharp as a sword-strike. But the western one was different — it did not attack; it crept in quietly, imperceptibly, becoming part of you until you began to feel it in every breath. And August wanted to be ready.

He walked along the rows, choosing clothing. A heavy cloak with a fur collar that the wind could not pierce. Woolen shirts, thick trousers, tall boots lined with fur. Everything was rough but sturdy, sewn not for beauty but to hold warmth and not tear in the wind. He bought another pair of mittens and a warm cap. He took nothing superfluous — only what could save a life.

When the purchases were made, August stopped at the edge of the market and looked around again. He saw a woman in a thick cloak carrying a basket of fish, an old man mending a net, paying no attention to the drizzle that had begun to fall, making the paving stones slippery. He saw thin columns of smoke rising from chimneys, mingling with the fog and becoming part of it. And he understood: this town did not await miracles. It awaited work. And in that simplicity there was its own strength.

In the evening, when the sun was already setting, staining the sky in dull shades of grey and violet, August went to the bathhouse. It was not the bathhouse he remembered in Al-Sharan, where the steam was light and the walls were decorated with mosaic. Not the one in Vistaria, where herbs and oils were added to the water. This was a cramped, dark, smoke-blackened bathhouse where it smelled of smoke and wet wood, where the stones were grey with soot, and the light penetrated through narrow cracks, falling in slanting bands on the floor. But the water was hot, and August sat in the steam-room, feeling the warmth penetrate his muscles, feeling dirt and tiredness leave the body, dissolving in the steam.

Other people sat around him — silent, with weathered faces, with calloused hands. No one stared. Here everyone was a stranger, and everyone was their own — because everyone knew what the road was and what the cold was. No one asked needless questions. No one demanded stories. Here they valued silence.

August closed his eyes and allowed himself simply to be — without thoughts, without memories, without plans. Only warmth, only quiet, only water and steam that washed away the last of the south.

When he came out, his skin was clean, his breathing fresh, and the world seemed a little clearer. He stood in a new cloak, with a new sword at his belt, with the guitar on his back, and felt a quiet, calm assurance spread within him. He was ready. For the cold. For the unknown. For the North.

At night, when the crew was already asleep, August sat on deck and watched the town slowly falling quiet. The wind was cold, and he felt his breath turn to vapor, hanging in the air for a moment before dissolving. He knew that tomorrow they would leave. And that this was the last time he saw the south.

In the morning, when the sun was only beginning to rise, staining the grey stones a pale pink, they left the port. August stood at the stern and watched the Northern Port vanish over the horizon. First he saw the roofs, then the lighthouse, then only a dark line of shore — and then that too dissolved in the haze. Ahead was the sea. Ahead was the North. He did not know what waited there. But he knew he was ready. And in that knowledge there was no bravado, only the calm firmness of a man who had understood: the road is not only movement forward but the ability to say farewell to what remains behind.
