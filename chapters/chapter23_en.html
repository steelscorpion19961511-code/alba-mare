He did not leave the capital alone.

He was invited to join a small troupe of wandering musicians — they were heading north, where, they said, fairs opened in autumn and people gathered who were ready to listen, and to pay. August did not hesitate long. After the warm weeks in the capital it seemed to him he was ready for a new road, and companions were a chance not to walk alone. But there was doubt inside him too: he was used to being by himself. Could he become part of something again? Could he keep from running away when things grew hard? Looking at their carts, at their smiles, at the lightness with which they were setting out, he felt something rise in his chest that he could not name. But he slung the guitar over his shoulder and stepped toward them.

They were four: a fiddler named Thomas, a flutist called Lena, an old percussionist with a grey beard whom everyone called simply Uncle, and a young singer, Mira. August quickly fell into their rhythm — they traveled in two carts, stopped in small towns and played in the squares, and in the evenings sat by the fire and talked of roads, of music, of people.

Mira was young, but her voice was deeper than one would have expected for her age. She sang as though she knew what it meant to lose. Her songs were of the sea, of separation, of those who leave and do not return. August sometimes caught her gaze turned toward him, and looked away. He was not ready to speak of his life. But sometimes, when she sang, he closed his eyes and listened.

The first two weeks of the road were mild. Autumn in those lands was not cold — it was damp and windy, but tolerable. Yet the farther north they went, the more noticeable the change became. The wind shifted, growing sharper. The nights grew longer, the days shorter.

One morning August woke and saw that the grass around the fire was covered with frost. He touched it with his fingers — it was brittle, cold, almost glassy. Bringing his hand to his face, he felt the cold touch his skin and not let go. He understood: the north had begun. Crouching and looking at the whitened blades of grass, he felt a strange calm rise within him. He knew where he was going. And that knowledge warmed him more than any fire.

By the end of the third week they came out to the coast. The sea that August had known as azure and calm was here grey, nearly black. Waves broke against the rocks with a hollow rumble, and the wind was so piercing that even through his heavy cloak August felt the cold reaching for his bones.

He said nothing of it to his companions. It seemed to him he would grow used to it, that it was only a passing discomfort — but the cold kept mounting. He began to notice that his fingers went numb even in gloves, and at night he could not get warm, no matter how he wrapped himself in his cloak.

In the fourth week he felt the first signs that something was wrong with him. First a light weakness, then a chill that would not pass even by the fire. He tried not to show it, but one evening, when they were all sitting by the fire, he took out the guitar and was going to play — and realized he could not. His fingers would not obey. They trembled, and the sound he drew was weak and false. He tried again — but the strings answered him dully, as though the guitar had frozen too. He put it back into the case and sat a long time looking at the fire, feeling the cold rise from within. For the first time in a long while, he could not play.

The next morning, as they made ready for the road, he could not rise to his feet.

Mira came over, sat down beside him, and touched his brow.

"You're burning," she said quietly. "Why didn't you say anything?"

August tried to smile, but it came out weakly.

"I thought it would pass."

"It is not passing," she answered. "We will stay here until you are well."

He wanted to protest, but his strength had left him. He only felt someone cover him with another cloak, heard the wheels of the cart creak, and heard Uncle's voice saying:

"We'll carry him to the nearest village. There's a healer there."

August closed his eyes. The cold receded — but only because it was replaced by a dull, clinging weakness. He sank into sleep, and he dreamed of the sea — but not the black, not the grey one: the old one, the azure one, where he had grown up. He did not know whether he would see it again.
