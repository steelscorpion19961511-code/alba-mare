The city was called Seacliff. It was a great port town, an important economic point of the southern realm. It stood on a rocky shore of the Eastern Sea, where the water looked grey and the wind cut to the bone. There was none of the quiet August had grown used to in the last days: the streets were noisy, the houses stood packed tight, and hardly any sunlight passed between them. But there were crowds of people here, and every one of them was hurrying somewhere.

He was looking for news of the refugees. Every conversation in the market, every stray phrase in a tavern — he caught them like scraps of sail in a fog. Some said the refugees had come by boat from the south. Some had heard they had scattered to different ports. No one could say for certain, but August clung to every detail.

By the end of the first day he was hungry. His stomach growled: for two days on the road he had not eaten hot, proper food. He went into a tavern by the quay called At the Silver Mackerel. Sailors and fishermen gathered there. It smelled of fish, smoke and wet cloth. August sat at a free table and ordered a plate of hot fish soup with vegetables, stewed mackerel in tomato with garlic, a piece of fresh rye bread and a mug of warm beer.

He ate slowly, listening to the voices, absorbing scraps of conversation. When he was nearly done, someone at the neighboring table said, not loudly:

"They say a city was burned in the south. A white city. Alba-Mare, I think."

August froze. Without turning, without moving his head, he only leaned slightly to the side.

"They say everyone there was cut down," added another voice. "Or carried off. It's an empty place now."

August could not bear it. He turned to them and asked:

"Do you know anything about the refugees? From there?"

The fishermen exchanged glances. One of them shrugged:

"Rumors, lad. Some say they were seen in the south, some say they sailed farther on. Nobody knows for sure. And don't ask — no one will tell you the truth."

August said nothing. He left his coins on the table and went out of the tavern.

He wandered the city until evening. In the markets, by the quays, in the lanes — he listened, asked, remembered. Sometimes someone dropped a careless phrase, sometimes only an empty glance. No one knew for certain. But he kept searching.

By night the streets emptied. The wind off the sea turned cold and biting, cutting through his cloak. August understood that he had almost no money left — not enough for a lodging. He found shelter under the awning of an old warehouse by the quay, where the air smelled of fish and damp wood. He wrapped himself in his cloak, pressed the guitar to his chest, and watched the dark water for a long time before falling asleep.

He woke from the cold. The sky over the sea was grey, and the wind carried a fine drizzle. August sat under the awning, looking at the water, and felt emptiness rising inside him. He had learned nothing. No one knew. No one had seen.

He sat and thought about what to do next. The city was foreign, and he had no money even for bread. He could try to find work as a tavern musician, as in Greymoot — but in Greymoot he had had a room and a keeper who gave him a chance. Here he was nobody. And there was nothing to wait for. Time was slipping away. His mother was out there somewhere, across this sea, along these roads, and every minute spent idle carried him farther from her.

The wind blew from the sea, and in its gusts there was neither answer nor hint.

August stood up, slung the guitar over his shoulder, tightened the straps of his pack, and made for the quays. He did not know what he would do. He did not know where to go. But he knew he could not stay here.

He went around several quays, offering himself as a laborer aboard ships. He was refused: captains had no use for chance companions. Some waved him off without looking. Some threw out a curt "not wanted." August did not give up. He moved from ship to ship, from quay to quay, and in every refusal felt hope melting like snow in the morning sun.

But at the third quay he saw an old vessel being loaded with sacks. The captain sat on a barrel by the gangway, watching the work. August came up to him, stopped and said:

"I need work."

The captain raised his eyes to him. He gave him a long, heavy look — and asked:

"Can you work with your hands?"

"Yes," August answered.

"Then start loading. Prove yourself, and I'll take you on to the eastern shore."

August did not ask where the ship was bound. He did not ask what the pay would be. He simply nodded and set to work.

For two days he hauled sacks, scrubbed the deck, rolled barrels of water and provisions. For two days he hardly spoke — he simply worked, the way he once had in the guard, when any action became a way of not thinking.

On the third evening the captain came up to him and said briefly:

"We sail tomorrow. If you want — stay."

August agreed.

The ship was called the Northern Dawn. She was old, worn, but sound. The captain said their route would run northeast along the coast, with several stops in ports where supplies could be replenished.

On the night before departure August stood on the quay and looked at the water. He did not know what lay ahead. He knew only that this was his road.

He remembered his father clutching a broken spear at the gates of Alba-Mare. He remembered the silence of the cove and the smoke over the city. He remembered the old bard Arno and the girl Lis. All of them were behind him now, but they lived in him — in every note, in every word, in every movement.

August turned and walked aboard the ship.

The wind was fresh and salty, as it always is by the sea. At dawn, when the first rays of the sun flushed the sky pink, the Northern Dawn weighed anchor and began her journey to the northeast.

Aboard, August found a place on the forecastle — the forward deck, where one could sit with legs dangling over the side and watch the shore slowly disappear behind.

He did not know what lay ahead. He did not know whether he would find his mother. But he knew that he was no longer simply a wanderer. He was a man following a call, and the wind in the sails promised him a new meeting.
