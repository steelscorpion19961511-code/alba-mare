They put in at a small port on the eighth day of the voyage.

The town was called Windholm, smaller than the one where August had boarded, but there was something captivating about it — wooden quays, old lamps, the cries of gulls and the smell of wet fish. The captain said they would lie here a day, to take on water and provisions.

August stepped ashore with a strange feeling of unsteadiness. After days at sea, the land seemed motionless, almost foreign. He took a few steps along the wooden planking and understood that his legs adapted to stillness more slowly than to the roll: his knees, as it were, remembered the rhythm of the waves and still gave a slight spring at every step. He paused for a moment simply to stand, to feel that the boards under his soles were not pitching and swaying but lay firm and sure.

He walked through the narrow lanes, looking at the houses. It was quiet here — not as in the big cities. People did not hurry, did not shout. It seemed that even the air was different here — more humid, more salt-laden, as though the town itself had absorbed the breathing of the sea. August passed drying fishing nets hung out to dry, an old woman sitting at her door with her knitting, a boy rolling an empty barrel along the cobblestones. The boy froze for a second, looked at August with curiosity, then shoved the barrel again — and it rolled on, clattering, down the street.

He stopped at a small market. It smelled of seaweed and dried fish. Trade was sluggish here — several women sorting their wares, an old man dozing on a bench with his face covered by the hem of his cloak. August bought bread, some cheese and a few dried apples. He handed over a coin and felt his hand find the right weight in his purse with practiced ease, as if this were a gesture from another life, long left behind. The seller, a grey-haired man with a weathered face, nodded silently, poured the purchases into a linen pouch and held it out to him.

"Thank you," August said quietly, and it seemed to him that even this simple word sounded different here — not as politeness, but as acknowledgment: he was a guest, and he had been received.

On his way back he noticed an old man sitting close to the water. He wore a faded cloak, and beside him lay a long staff — a walking stick, or perhaps a fishing rod. August came up and sat down beside him without a word.

The man did not turn; he went on watching the sea, and the silence between them was natural, not strained. It demanded no words, for both of them knew how to listen to the water.

"Have you been here long?" the old man asked, without turning his head.

"Only just," August answered. "I came by ship."

"Where are you bound?"

"North," said August. "I'm looking for someone."

The old man nodded, as if he had known August would say so. He was silent a moment, then said, without looking at him:

"This sea does not tolerate haste. It decides for itself where to carry the traveler. You sail, but it is not you who steers the ship. So do not search — the road will find you of itself."

August wanted to object, to say that he could not wait, that every minute mattered, that his mother was out there beyond the horizon and he must hurry. But the words stuck in his throat, for in the old man's simple speech there was so much calm truth that arguing seemed pointless.

The old man rose, leaning on his staff, and walked slowly along the pier, limping slightly. August watched him go for a long time. The old man's words stayed in his memory like a splinter — not a sharp one, but a dull one, that does not wound but makes itself known at every movement.

When he returned to the ship, the sun was already sinking, and the sky above the water had turned gold and rose, as if someone had poured honey into it. He found the sailors at supper — they sat on the deck, eating stew and talking quietly among themselves. For the first time in the whole voyage, he sat with them.

"What did you find ashore?" asked one of them — that same old mariner who had once held out to him the dried cod.

"Nothing," said August. "I just walked."

The sailor smiled and pushed a bowl toward him:

"Then eat. Tomorrow we sail on."

August took the spoon and felt that for the first time in a long while no one was expecting him here, and yet he was not superfluous. He had become part of this evening, this crew, this ship.

After supper he climbed to the forecastle. The wind was cool but not cruel — the kind that comes before quiet nights. August took out the guitar, laid it on his knees and ran his fingers across the strings. At first the sound was slightly rattling, as if the strings too had not yet grown used to dry land, but then he turned the pegs, listened to each tone, and the guitar rang true again.

He played a quiet melody he had just composed. It was born of everything he had seen and heard that day: of the creak of boards underfoot, of the cries of gulls, of the old man's words by the water. There was no rage in it, no despair — only a quiet hope, like a dawn not yet visible but already felt in the air.

The melody was about the old man, about the wooden quays, about how hard it is to walk when you do not know where you will stop. He played it several times until it became even and complete, and understood that this song would stay with him, like all the rest.

While he played, the captain came quietly up to him. He did not sit down, did not speak — he simply stood nearby, listening, and in that silence there was more approval than in any words. When August finished, the captain inclined his head slightly and said:

"A good song. You can hear the sea in it — but you can hear you in it too. That is rare."

August did not know what to answer, so he simply nodded. The captain stood a while longer, then turned and left him alone with his thoughts and his guitar.

Later, when the sky had turned deep blue and the first stars lay reflected in the water, August sat by the side and watched the ship rock on the waves. It suddenly occurred to him that the road is not only movement forward, but also the ability to stop, to listen, to notice. And perhaps the old man had been right: one does not always need to search for the road — sometimes it finds you of itself, if you are not afraid to stand by the water and wait.
