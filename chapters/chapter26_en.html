The next morning August woke earlier than usual. Outside it was still gray, and the sky seemed heavy, as if bearing the whole winter weariness of the earth. But the house already smelled of melted butter and fresh bread — a smell so cozy and simple that for a moment August froze, listening to it as to a quiet, kind melody. He dressed, threw on his cloak, and went outside.

The air was cold, but no longer the piercing cold that had met him in the first days — it did not dig into his skin with icy needles, but merely announced itself with an even, calm chill. August was growing used to it, and now this cold seemed to him not an enemy but a stern traveling companion, testing whether he was ready to go on.

The market in Old Bridge stood on the main square, right by the old stone bridge thrown across the river. The bridge was low and broad, its dark stones covered in a thin crust of ice, dusted with snow in places. The water beneath it flowed muffled and unhurried, as if guarding itself against the frost. Stalls lined the bank — wooden, hastily built but sturdy, as if made by people accustomed to wind and bad weather. The traders had already laid out their goods: coarse woolen cloth, smoked fish, honey in clay pots, knitted things smelling of smoke and wool. Over some stalls hung makeshift awnings; here and there small braziers glowed, sending up light steam.

August walked along the stalls, studying the goods. He knew what he needed — not only for warmth, but for the road. The old clothes in which he had arrived were no longer fit for the north. They were too light, too southern, as if sewn for sun and wind rather than snow and icy rain. Now he needed things that knew how to hold warmth and did not fear damp.

He stopped at a stall where an elderly man with a thick gray beard was trading. The man sat on a low bench, puffing at a pipe, watching passersby with the lazy attention of someone who had seen everything this market had to offer: joy, despair, plain need. Smoke rose from his pipe, mingling with the frosty air, smelling of dried mint and something tart.

'Come over, lad,' he said, catching August's glance. 'I can see your clothes aren't made for these parts. Take a look at this.'

He took from the rack a thick sweater of coarse sheep's wool. It was dark gray, almost the color of wet stone, dense, with a long collar that could be wrapped tighter. To the touch it was almost prickly, but there was real warmth in it — the kind found only in things made for a long winter, when the night stretches on endlessly. August held it against his shoulder, judged the size, ran his fingers over the dense weave, as if hoping to hear in that touch a promise that it would not fail him.

'Warm,' said the trader. 'The wind can't get through it. And it doesn't soak through at once. They wear this in blizzards and under rain alike.'

August nodded and took it. Then he noticed, on the neighboring stall, a heavy cloak of leather and wool with a fur-trimmed hood. It was heavy, almost crude to look at, but when August threw it over his shoulders he felt at once that the wind no longer touched his body, as if it were breaking against an invisible wall. The cloak sat comfortably, did not hamper his movements, and could be worn over the lighter cloak he already owned.

'I'll take this one too,' he said.

The trader nodded approvingly and named the price. August counted out the coins without haggling. It seemed to him the right price to pay for being able to go on without glancing back at the cold.

He also bought woolen socks and mittens — plain, without ornament, but sturdy, with a tight weave and thick seams. The mittens were a little too big, but August knew that was even a good thing: thin gloves could be worn under them if his fingers began to freeze badly. And as he was about to leave, he noticed in the corner of the stall a rucksack — a solid one, of heavy sacking, with leather straps and a large inner compartment. It was slightly larger than the one he had, and looked strong and reliable, as if it could withstand snow and rain and long marches over trackless ground. August took it, checked the seams and straps, pulled at the buckles — everything held firm. He added the rucksack to his purchase, feeling a quiet confidence rising in his chest.

When he returned to Elsa's house, he laid everything out on the table: the sweater, the cloak, the socks, the mittens, the new rucksack. The things lay side by side, plain and substantial, and there was something soothing in their silent weight. He looked at them and felt a calm readiness growing inside him. He was ready for winter.

In the evening he sat by the stove, smoothing the new sweater. The wool still smelled of the workshop, of smoke, and of something subtly familiar. And suddenly he thought of how much had changed since he had left Alba-Mare. He was no longer the boy who had run from a burning city with his father's sword at his belt, not knowing where his next step would fall. Now he was a man who knew how to pack for a long journey, how to choose warm clothing, how to walk toward a place he had never been and not fear that the cold would catch him before he reached his goal.

And it seemed to him that this, precisely, was what growing up meant — not in loud words or great deeds, but in the ability to prepare for the road and not fail oneself.
