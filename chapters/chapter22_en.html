The next morning August woke not to a voice, not to wind, but to sunlight flooding the room. He lay a while looking at the ceiling, feeling how the clean linen smelled of the sea and herbs, and realized he could no longer remember when he had last felt so rested.

He raised his hands before his face and looked at them. His fingers no longer trembled. They were calm, warm, ready to touch the strings again. He clenched them into a fist, unclenched them — and smiled.

He washed, put on clean clothes, and went downstairs. The tavern smelled of hot butter and bread. He ordered a fresh roll, a piece of cheese, and a mug of herbal tea, sat by the window, and watched the city wake.

The city met him with noise. But through that noise — through the cries of vendors, the clink of coins, the clatter of hooves — he caught a scent. Sweet, slightly bitter, floral. August raised his eyes and saw that along the walls of the houses, over balconies and cornices, long clusters of lilac-colored flowers trailed. They fell from on high like waterfalls, touching windows and doors. He did not know the name of this flower. But when he asked a passerby, the man answered with a smile:

"That's wistaria. The city is named after it. They say the first settlers saw it on the hillsides and decided that a land where such a marvel blooms could not be a bad one."

August stopped for a moment, looking at the flowers. He thought of Alba-Mare — white, azure, smelling of salt and sun. Here it smelled differently. Here it smelled of a warmth that is not afraid of time.

He went out and headed in the direction where, it seemed to him, there was more greenery. Soon he came upon a large park. It was fenced with wrought iron, and beyond the fence began a true green oasis: broad avenues, worn paths, old trees whose crowns closed overhead into a green canopy. Inside the park lay a shallow lake with clear water and a small wooden pier. Water lilies swayed on the surface, their reflections visible in the mirror-smooth water. A little bridge led to an old gazebo where lovers used to sit, or people simply read books.

August chose a bench under an oak, from which the lake and the little bridge were visible, with people crossing it. He took off the guitar and, without thinking long, began to play — softly, almost for himself. The sun warmed his shoulders, the wind stirred the leaves overhead, and August felt the last anxiety left from the long road slip away. He watched the water, watched clouds reflected in its dark surface, and it seemed to him the music was born of the silence of this place. Couples passed by, children stopped, someone dropped a small coin into the case.

He played until midday — easily, freely, without strain. It was not a performance. It was a part of himself.

Toward noon he finished and left the park to have lunch. He found a small tavern with a sign shaped like a fish, where they served hot soup with greens and a piece of baked fish. The food was simple but filling, and August ate unhurriedly, listening to the voices around, taking in the sounds and smells of the city. After lunch he went out onto the main street.

Vistaria was a city of flowers and sun. Here even the stones seemed warmed not so much by light as by time — by the fact that the city had existed so long it had itself become part of the earth. People did not hurry here — not because they were afraid, but because they felt the city was not going anywhere. And August, for the first time in many months, allowed himself to feel it too.

He wandered the streets, studying faces, memorizing the colors of houses, the patterns on shutters. And everywhere he looked, the same lilac vine trailed — on walls, on gates, over doors. It had become part of the city, like its breathing.

That day he took in more than in the past few weeks combined: every turn, every step, every sound adding itself into a new song.

He stayed in that city longer than he had planned. For almost three weeks he played in the square, in the park, on the embankment. The city began to recognize him. People smiled at him, some nodded when they met, children tugged at his sleeve, and once a fruit seller dropped into his case not a coin but a ripe fig. He felt that here he was welcomed, and it made him feel warm and at peace.

One morning a boy of about eight ran up to him. He stood by the fountain and looked at August with such burning eyes that the latter could not fail to notice.

"Are you the bard from the square?" he asked.

"Yes," August answered.

"Teach me to play!" the boy burst out.

August looked at his hands — dirty, covered in scratches and scrapes, with bitten-off nails. He took them in his own palms, examined them closely, and then smiled:

"First learn to wash your hands. Then come back."

The boy laughed, nodded, and ran off. But two days later he stood at the fountain with clean, washed hands, waiting for August to finish playing. August showed him how to hold the guitar, how to touch the strings, how to hear when a note rang true. The boy listened with held breath and repeated every movement with such seriousness that August could not help smiling. The lesson took no more than an hour, but as the boy ran off he shouted over his shoulder:

"I'm going to practice!"

August watched him go and felt something warm spread through his chest.

He was even invited to play in the house of a wealthy merchant — a quiet evening with a narrow circle of guests — and he was paid more for it than in several days on the square. It was not triumph, not fame, but simply recognition that he was doing what he knew and loved.

After the performance, as the guests were beginning to leave, a woman came up to him. She was older than he, about thirty-five, with dark hair gathered in a careless knot, and eyes that seemed to see more than they said. She stopped in front of him and smiled — without coquetry, without play, simply with a warm curiosity.

"You play as though you were bidding someone farewell," she said. "Perhaps you ought to stay here a while, and bid someone else farewell instead?"

August looked at her. In her gaze there was nothing but a quiet, understanding invitation. She was not waiting for an answer — she was simply offering him a choice.

"I don't know whether I'll stay," he said quietly.

"I do," she answered. "You won't stay. But that doesn't mean you can't linger here a while."

She touched his hand — lightly, almost weightlessly — and smiled the way people smile who know that words are not always necessary. August did not follow her. But when she had gone, he felt the trace of her fingers on his skin — warm and light, like the memory of something that might have been. He sat a long time in the empty hall, watching the fire in the hearth, and thought how strange a thing life is: it offers you warmth where you are not expecting it.

By the end of the third week he had earned enough not only to live but to set money aside. But the surplus of silver began to weigh on him. He decided it was time to put in order what had traveled with him all this while.

He went to the workshop of a musical instrument maker — an old man whose hands were covered with calluses and scars. The man examined the guitar at length, ran his fingers along the neck, listened to the sound of the strings. Then he nodded and said:

"A good instrument, though I've seen better. But I can give her back her voice."

The craftsman loosened the pegs and removed the old strings — one by one, carefully, as though stripping old skin from the instrument. They were dark, worn, with barely visible traces of rust at the top nut. Then he removed the frets — gently, with a special tool, lifting each metal strip so as not to damage the wood. August watched the craftsman's fingers move with confident softness, as though he had done this a thousand times.

The neck emerged clean, and in the light the worn patches became visible — the places where August's fingers had searched for the right notes over the years.

The craftsman took a piece of soft cloth, dropped oil onto it, and began to polish the neck. With circular motions, slowly, working the oil into the wood until it began to glow with a warm, honeyed tint. The worn spots vanished, and the neck became smooth as water.

Then the craftsman went over to a small brazier. He lit a candle and held a piece of dark resin to the flame. It began to melt, giving off a thick, woody smell — astringent and slightly sweet. The craftsman mixed the melted resin with fine shavings, worked the mixture into a uniform paste, and with the tip of a thin knife carefully filled the crack in the soundboard.

He did it with such care, as though stitching a living wound. Then he smoothed the surface and left it to dry.

While the resin set, he brought out a dark oil — thick, smelling of nuts and old wood — and coated the whole guitar with it, from neck to bottom edge. At once the wood darkened, came alive, revealed its grain. The craftsman set the guitar aside, letting the oil soak in.

"Now the strings," he said. "The old ones have grown weary. They gave what they had. I offer you a new set. From the eastern lands. Of gut from a rare animal — they're gathered far beyond the desert. Strings like these sound longer and softer. They're expensive. But they're worth it."

August considered for a moment, then nodded:

"Put them on."

The craftsman stretched the new strings, fastened them at the pegs, and tuned them. And when he ran a finger along the first string, August heard a sound that could not be forgotten. It was pure, deep, warm — as though the guitar had begun to speak with a voice it had never had before.

When the last string was stretched, August took the guitar in his hands. It was light, but a new strength could be felt in it. The wood had grown deeper, the neck was smooth and warm, and the strings rang clean even without playing.

August held his breath, touched the strings, and the room filled with sound. He did not think about what to play. His fingers found the right movement on their own, and there flowed the melody he had known since childhood — of the white walls of Alba-Mare, of the azure sea, of how the sun gilded the roofs and how the wind played with the sails. There was no ache in that music — there was a quiet, luminous memory.

The craftsman stood by the table, listening, his eyes fixed on the instrument as if watching it come to life. When August finished, he nodded slowly and said:

"Now she has a voice. And you know how to use it."

August set the guitar aside and, taking out his purse, counted out the pay for the work and the new strings. The craftsman glanced at the coins but did not put them away at once — he looked again at the guitar, then at August:

"You do well to care for your instrument. But remember: a guitar is like a sword. It needs not only good strings but a sound scabbard."

He pointed at the case August had brought — an old sack of coarse cloth:

"This case has served its time. It protected the instrument, but its hour has passed. I know a leatherworker not far from here. He makes cases to order, of thick leather, with a soft lining and a strap. If you like, I can tell him you'll be coming."

August thought, then nodded:

"Good. I'll go to him."

The craftsman wrote the address on a scrap of paper and handed it to August. He took it, thanked him, and went out into the sunlit street.

August found the leatherworker without difficulty.

The workshop stood at the end of a narrow lane, and outside it smelled of tanned leather, wax, and something sweetish. Inside it was cramped — pieces of leather hung everywhere, belts, blanks for bags, scabbards, and cases. At a table sat a man with a thick beard, in a leather apron covered with the marks of his trade.

August showed him the address and said he had come on the craftsman's recommendation.

The leatherworker took his measurements, noted down the figures, and named his price. August counted out half the sum, and the leatherworker put the coins away in a desk drawer.

"Come back in three days," he said. "I think by then it will all be ready."

August lifted the guitar from the table and slung it over his shoulder. The old case was still holding on, though it was nearly spent. He thought of asking whether the man could fit the case so the guitar sat snugly, but changed his mind — he decided to trust the craftsman.

"I'll come," he said, and went out into the street.

The guitar was with him, and it felt like a familiar, native weight on his shoulder.

For the next three days August played almost without stopping.

He went to the square where he had first gathered a crowd, and played until midday. He moved on to the park, sat on the same bench by the lake, and played to the sound of leaves and children's voices. Sometimes he went into the merchants' quarter, where echoes tossed the sound between the walls, and stopped by a fountain, playing for passersby, traders, and curious onlookers.

The days were filled with music. He played old songs and found new ones, writing down the words on scraps of paper he folded into his pocket. August felt the city becoming his stage and the people his audience. They began to recognize him in the streets. Children pointed at him and shouted: "The bard!" — and he smiled back at them.

On the third day he came to the leatherworker again. The craftsman met him at the threshold and motioned him inside. On the workbench lay a new case — of thick dark leather, with a soft lining, sewn with large stitches, with a long strap and a metal buckle. The guitar slid into it snugly, like into a scabbard. He fastened the strap, slung it over his shoulder, and the renewed instrument took its place. He paid the remainder, gave thanks, and went out into the street — now with a guitar that seemed to have gained one more voice.

The city saw him off with quiet sunlight. One last time he came to the square — no longer to earn, but to say farewell. He played the song he had composed over these days — of the city, of its people, of how hard it is to leave when they have begun to remember you.

When he finished, someone from the crowd called out: "Come back!" — and he smiled without turning around. The guitar on his shoulder seemed lighter than ever — as if it had finally found its true voice. And August felt that with that voice he was not simply wandering from city to city — he was leaving a particle of himself in each of them.

He left the city through the same gates he had entered several weeks before. But now he was leaving not as a stranger, but as someone who would be remembered here. At the top of the hill he stopped and looked back. Vistaria lay below — white, warm, alive, its tiled roofs gilded by the rays of the setting sun. And along the walls, over balconies and cornices, the same lilac vine still trailed — the flower that had given the city its name. August smiled and raised his hand in a gesture of farewell, though no one could see him. Then he turned and walked on, keeping that city in his memory as the place where, for the first time, he felt himself something more than a wanderer.
