The ship sailed on westward, keeping to the southern shore. The days stretched evenly, without incident — as though the sea had decided to give them a short respite before something greater. The wind was fair, the sea calm, and even the sun set every evening in almost the same place, as though by schedule. The crew had grown used to August and his guitar, and between them a silent understanding had formed that needed no words: when he took up the instrument, the conversations died away of themselves, and when he simply stood by the side, no one pestered him with idle questions. Sometimes in the evenings they sat on deck, and August played — quietly, so that the melody did not contend with the noise of the waves but wove into it. And in those moments the sea seemed a little quieter, the night a little warmer, as though the very sky were listening.

On the third day after Sea Cliff the captain came up to August, leaned his elbow on the rail, and, without looking at him, said:

"Tomorrow we will pass Alba-Mare."

August made no answer. He simply stood by the side and looked at the horizon, feeling something heavy and hot rise inside him, something he had never given a name. It was not merely memory — it was a burden he had carried for years, trying not to notice its weight. He had awaited this day — and feared it. Feared that, seeing the ruins, he would understand: everything he had built within himself after that fire had crumbled to dust. Feared that he would not be able to hold in what he had kept locked away for so many years.

In the morning the sky was grey, as though it had been washed in salt water and not properly dried. The sea seemed heavy, filled with lead, and even the wind was somehow hushed, as though it knew what was about to happen and did not want to interfere. August stood in the bow, peering at the coastline that slowly emerged through the morning haze. First — simply a dark band on the horizon, as though someone had drawn with charcoal on wet paper. Then — the outlines of ruins. White stones scattered over the slope like the bones of a long-dead beast. Burnt walls, black with time and soot. The empty openings of windows into which the wind peered and blew out the last remnants of life.

The town was dead. But it was here.

The ship moved slowly, almost solemnly, as though the captain himself felt that one could not rush past this place under full sail. August stood motionless, looking at what remained of Alba-Mare, and recognized every detail the way one recognizes the features of a dear face, even grown old and changed. He recognized the outlines of the port — that very one where he had once worked as a boy, hauling sacks of fish and listening to the fishermen curse the storm. He recognized the line of the embankment where the old pier had once stood, creaky and unreliable but so dear. He recognized the place where his home had once stood — now a heap of stones lay there, overgrown with coarse grass.

And suddenly — not in the face but from within — the memory covered him. Dense, warm, like the morning sun. It came not as a picture but as a feeling: the smell of tar and wet boards, the weight of a sack on his shoulder, the tickle of wind in his hair.

He was a boy again. Eight years old. Sitting on a sun-warmed stone by the old pier. The sea azure, almost motionless, as though frozen in expectation of something important. Fishermen mending their nets, and that monotonous, rhythmic tapping of needles on rope sounded like music. It smelled of tar, wet boards, salt and fish — a smell that then seemed the rightest in the world. August looks at the horizon — to where the sky merges with the water — and suddenly hears. Not voices. Not splashing. Something else. The voice of the town. He did not then know what to call it. Did not know it was music. He knew only that something sounded inside him that did not exist outside.

His mother found him by the very water, when it had already begun to grow dark. She stood on the pier and looked with a gaze he could not understand — a mixture of anxiety and quiet, almost sad pride.

"You hear what most people stop hearing when they grow up," she said. "Do not lose it, August."

He did not lose it.

And then there was that day in the square. A foreign ship with white sails, as though woven from light. A man with a curved instrument, an unfamiliar melody that sank beneath the skin and stayed there forever. And he, thirteen years old, stands, unable to move, forgetting to breathe. And a voice from behind: "He plays beautifully, doesn't he?"

A merchant with a weathered face and warm eyes. Matteo.

"This is a guitar. Its homeland is across the sea. There they call it the voice of the wind."

A thousand copper coins. Two days of work. Everything he could, everything he had. And in the end — a guitar in his hands. Warm, alive, of honey-colored wood, with a lacquer that clung slightly to the fingers. The first chord, timid and inaccurate, sounded like a promise.

"I will return in a year," Matteo had said. "You will know how to play by then, won't you?"

August smiled, looking at the empty shore. He had learned. He had learned to play so that people fell silent so as not to miss a single note. But Matteo had not returned then. He returned later — when August was already a man who had lost everything, on a bare island in the middle of the ocean.

Now he looked at the ruins again. Saw how the wind played with the debris, how the sea touched the shore, how the light fell on the white stones and made them almost alive. He remembered how he had learned to play. How he found quiet places — the shore, a field, the roof of an old warehouse. How his fingers sought the strings and his heart sought a voice. How he wrote his first songs on scraps of paper, inventing his own titles: "The Wave," "The Dawn," "Loneliness." How his mother silently set a plate before him and looked with the same quiet sadness with which she had looked at the pier.

Then there had been his father. Service in the guard. Seven years of silence, when music hid in his chest, like something too fragile to show to the world. And the return — when he opened the old cabinet again, blew the dust off the guitar and touched the strings, the way one touches an old friend. And the next day the town burned.

Alba-Mare, white, warm, smelling of salt and sun — burned. August ran, not feeling his legs, and when the road opened, he saw black smoke above the white walls. His father lay by the eastern gates. The spear broken, his face calm, as though he had simply fallen asleep in the middle of battle. August took him in his arms and carried him to the shore. To where the sea was clean and the sand white. He laid out branches, took off his cloak, lit a fire. He watched the flame devour his father's body, watched the sparks rise into the sky and dissolve in the darkness. He did not know the words. He did not know the prayers. He simply stood and watched the fire until he understood that his tears were mingling with the salt of the wind.

Now he stood on the ship and looked at that same shore. The sea — the same. The wind — the same. The sand — white. Only he himself was unrecognizable now.

The ship slowly passed the bay. August watched it, feeling time contract, feeling past and present merge into one long, warm moment. He recognized the place where he had once sat with his mother, where she had said: "Do not lose it." He recognized the square where he had first heard the guitar. He recognized the street where his house had stood. Everything was here — and nothing was. Only stones, wind and silence.

"He is here," August said quietly, almost in a whisper, as though afraid to frighten off the memory.

Matteo laid a hand on his shoulder — firmly, like a friend, without pity.

"He has gone into the sea," he said calmly. "The way you people of Alba-Mare always saw off your own."

August nodded without turning around. He did not need to look at Matteo to know that he understood.

When the ruins were left behind, August turned and went to the stern. Matteo followed him with his eyes but did not go after him. He knew that sometimes a man needs to be alone to gather himself out of the shards of memory.

In the evening, when the crew had gone to their places, August sat at the stern and looked at the sea. Behind him lay Al-Sharan, Windholm, Sea Cliff, the ruins of Alba-Mare. Ahead was the North. He felt how the past no longer pulled him backward but became a prop on which one could lean to go on.

He took the guitar, ran his fingers over the strings — and began to play a quiet, almost inaudible melody. Everything could be heard in it: childhood, the roar of the surf, his mother's voice, the ringing of bells, the first chord, the promise, ash, the road, meetings, partings, returns, silence, light. He did not sing. He simply played — for what lay behind, and for what waited ahead.

When he finished, he laid the guitar on his knees and looked at the sea for a long time. The wind touched his face, brought the smell of salt and distant lands, and August felt a quiet, almost forgotten calm spread within him. Now he knew the town had not died. It remained in him. In every note. In every memory. In the way he looked at the sea and saw not only water but what once had been. And that knowledge made him stronger.
