He had been walking southeast for a week now.

The road led him through hills and plains, drawing him gradually toward the coast. There were no large cities here — only scattered villages strewn across the slopes of the hills. Some were home to herders, others to farmers growing barley and rye. August stayed in them briefly: he played on village squares, and in return for food or shelter helped with chores — mending fences, carrying water, chopping wood. Sometimes he was given a few coins, but more often bread, cheese or dried meat, so he could walk on.

He lingered hardly at all in these places. It seemed to him he was seeking not merely a road but the right rhythm of the journey: one in which every step did not take him farther from his goal but brought him closer to it. But the closer the sea came, the stronger grew in him a strange feeling: that he was running not toward something, but away from it. Away from memories that clung to him like burrs to a cloak.

One night, sleeping by the fire at the edge of the forest, he dreamed of his mother. Not as he had seen her last — exhausted and pale — but young, laughing, her hair streaming in the wind. She stood by the water and called to him, but he could not reach her: a line of surf kept rising between them, and each time he stepped into the water, the shore shifted, drifted farther away. August woke in a cold sweat, stared for a long time at the dying embers, and then could not fall asleep again for a long while. From that morning he walked faster, as if hoping to leave the dream behind.

Toward evening on the third day the hills grew lower, and the air turned damp and brackish. There was a smell of seaweed and tar, and even the wind blew differently here: it did not drive dust along the road, but carried with it a sound that could not be mistaken for anything else. It was the sea.

The village he came to stood on the edge of a cliff. The houses were built of time-darkened stone, roofed with shingles, and at the foot of the rock stretched a narrow pier. A single boat rocked there, tied to a rusty ring by a thick tarred rope. The boat was old, its paint peeling, but sturdy: August noticed this at once, for he had grown used to seeing in things not only beauty but reliability.

An old man in an oilskin cloak stood on the pier. He did not look at August; he was untangling a net with unhurried, practiced movements. August came closer and stopped a few steps away, not wanting to disturb him.

"Is it far to the great port?" he asked when the old man paused for breath.

The old man raised his eyes to him, as if only now noticing the traveler. His gaze was calm and heavy, the gaze of a man accustomed to measuring distance not in miles but in storms.

"Three days along the shore to Seacliff," he said quietly, without fuss. "But if you like, I can take you by boat. Tomorrow at dawn. Though I won't promise calm: the sea is out of sorts these days."

August wanted to agree at once — time was burning inside him, and every minute felt wasted. But something in the old man's words made him hesitate. The sea is out of sorts these days. It had sounded not like a warning, but like an invitation to listen.

"Thank you," he answered quietly. "I'll stay a while for now. If I may — where can I spend the night?"

The old man nodded toward the village:

"Ask for Lia. Her house is by the well, with a green shutter. She'll take you in if you say I sent you."

And so August found himself in a small room with a low ceiling and a window overlooking the sea. The hostess, a silent woman with tired but kind eyes, brought him a bowl of hot fish-and-root stew, a piece of black bread and a mug of warm milk. She asked no questions, and August was grateful for that. He ate while the sky darkened outside the window and the first stars lay reflected in the water.

At night he woke because he heard the voice of the sea. Not noise, not a rumble — the voice itself: low, drawn-out, as if someone were singing very far away and the wind carried only scattered notes. August rose, threw on his cloak and went outside. The village slept, and only a single lamp flickered on the pier. The old man still sat there — now not untangling the net, but gazing into the darkness, toward the place where the horizon merged with the water.

"Can't sleep?" he asked, without turning.

"I hear the sea," August answered simply.

The old man inclined his head slightly, as if listening not to the words but to what stood behind them.

"It is always speaking. Most people just never learn to listen. Are you a musician?"

"Yes," August nodded. "I play the guitar."

"Then you should understand. The sea is music too. Only it cannot be played the same way twice."

They were silent for a while. Then the old man stretched out a hand and pointed at the lonely boat:

"Her name is Quiet Wave. I built her myself. She is old, but honest. If you wish tomorrow, we'll go out in her. But if you don't — no harm done. Sometimes a man simply needs to stand by the water to understand where he is going next."

August nodded, promising nothing. He returned to the house, lay down on the hard bench, but could not fall asleep for a long time. The old man's words turned in his head. Sometimes a man simply needs to stand by the water. For the first time in a long while, he did not want to hurry anywhere.

In the morning he came to the pier before everyone. The sun was just rising, turning the rocks pink, and the water was smooth, almost glassy. The old man was already there, checking knots, adjusting the rigging. He gave August a short nod, as if everything had been settled without words.

"The sea is calm today," he said. "We can go out."

August looked at the boat, then at the horizon. And suddenly understood that he did not want to. Not now.

"I'll stay for now," he said quietly. "If I may, I'll help you with the net. Or with anything else."

The old man nodded again, this time with a faint smile at the corners of his lips:

"Good. Your hands are strong, I can see that. And the sea isn't going anywhere. It waits for those who are ready to hear it."

And August stayed. He helped the old man mend the net, carried tar and tarred ropes, listened to his brief tales of storms and tides, of how to read the sky and how to know when the sea was gathering its anger. And in the evenings he sat at the end of the pier, took out the guitar and played. At first quietly, so as not to disturb, then with growing confidence, because the old man nodded as he listened, and something like approval appeared in his eyes.

Once, when the sun was already setting and the water had turned to gold, the old man said:

"You are looking for someone. I see it in your eyes. But remember: it is not always necessary to sail in order to find. Sometimes it is enough to stand on the shore and listen as the sea whispers your road to you."

August did not answer, but the words settled on his heart like a stone that becomes part of a wall. He went on helping, went on playing, went on listening to the sea. And with each day there was less haste in him and more quiet.

And the lonely boat still rocked at the pier, as if waiting for its hour. And August knew: when that hour came, he would be ready.
