August stayed in that village two weeks.

There were no taverns to play in, no squares where people gathered. Only the sea, a few fishing boats, and a dozen houses. And that was exactly what he needed: after the long road, after the cities and villages and people, he wanted simply to listen to the waves.

For a modest payment he settled in a small, cramped room with a view of the sea — in the old house of a woman the trader who had given him a lift had told him about. She proved silent and unsociable, but she kept the room spotless, and every morning she left a piece of bread and a mug of milk on the doorstep.

The days passed slowly. He walked the deserted shore, sat on the cliff, watched the horizon. Sometimes he took the guitar and played — not for anyone, but for himself. The music mingled with the sound of the surf, and he let it flow, not trying to compose, not searching for new songs. He simply moved his fingers over the strings until the rhythm merged with the breathing of the waves.

Once, wandering among the rocks, he found a small cove — hidden from the village, quiet, with clear water and smooth stones. The waves reached it almost soundlessly, and not a soul was there. He sat on a sun-warmed rock and sat for a long time, listening to the sea breathe. For the first time in a long while he thought of neither the road, nor people, nor what lay ahead. He simply was.

And then he noticed that his shirt — a grey linen one, long since faded — was covered in dust and stains. His trousers were worn white at the knees. He felt less a man than a shadow carried by the wind.

He undressed, waded into the water up to his waist, plunged in headfirst and surfaced. The water was cool, but the sun had warmed it through the day. He washed off the road dust — all that weariness, all that sweat, all that heaviness that had caked onto him over weeks of travel. Then he washed his shirt and trousers, laid them out on the hot stones warmed by the sun, and lay down beside them, feeling the wind — warm, dry — touch his skin.

When his clothes were dry, he put them on — clean, smelling of sun and wind — and walked slowly back to the house, feeling lightness fill his body.

The days stretched one after another, quiet and almost identical. He grew used to the place, and it grew used to him. The locals asked no questions, did not try to pry out his story. They simply nodded in passing, sometimes leaving fish or bread by his door. August accepted it in silence, and for the first time in years felt neither obligation nor any wish to explain himself.

So a week passed, and then a few more days. He had begun to forget that he had once been in a hurry. But one morning, as he stood by the water, the wind carried him scraps of conversation from the pier:

"They say, in the port city..."

"Yes, I heard. People from there. From the south. In boats..."

"Their city burned," said a third, without turning.

August did not move. He looked at the water, but he listened. They spoke of a great port town two days east, of ships, of foreign lands. And of refugees who had appeared there recently. No one knew exactly where they came from. But they said — from the south.

August did not press them with questions. He walked away from the pier and stood long by the water, gazing at the horizon. A city he had never heard of. Refugees from the south. All of it could be coincidence — or it could be a thread leading to her.

The thought of his mother, of her face, of how she had looked at the sea that last time, flared in him with new force. He had hardly allowed himself to think of her — it hurt too much to admit she might have perished, that he would never learn the truth. But now, in that scrap of conversation, a hope kindled. A small one, barely visible, but it was there. What if she was there? What if she was waiting for him, as he had waited for her all these months? He did not need answers. He needed to know that he had not stopped searching.

He went to his room, gathered his things, and took to the road before dawn.

He left at dawn — but before that, on his last evening, he sat once more on the rocks by the water. The sun was sinking into the sea, gilding and blushing the water. He did not play. He simply sat and watched until dark.

In the morning, before leaving, he stopped by his hostess. She was sitting on the doorstep mending a net, but seeing him with his things she asked nothing. She only held out a bundle of food.

"The sea does not forgive those who don't return," she said quietly. "But you are not one of them."

For a moment she touched his shoulder — lightly, like a blessing — and withdrew her hand. August felt that touch through his cloak: warm, brief, but the kind that stays. He took the bundle and went out the gate.

Two days later he was in the port.
