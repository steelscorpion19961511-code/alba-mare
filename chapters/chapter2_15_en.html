August did not count the days. There was no point. To count means to wait, and to wait means to hope, and hope, as he had already decided, was a luxury one cannot afford when one has nothing. He simply survived. He rose at dawn, went to the stream, drank, washed, checked the fire. If the coals still smouldered — he blew on them, added dry grass and twigs. If they had died — he lit the fire anew. It was a ritual, and rituals matter: they hold you up when everything else collapses. In the North, Bjorn said: "A ritual is the skeleton of the day. Without a skeleton the day falls apart." August did not think about skeletons. He simply did, and the day held.

Then he went to the shore. He wandered the sand, looking under stones, checking the traps, gathering crabs and seaweed. He memorized at what hour the tide left the deepest pools, which rocks yielded the most prey, where the sand was firmer and easier to walk. The island was small, but it lived its own life, and August learned to read it as he had read the snow in the North: by tracks, by smells, by the behaviour of the birds and crabs. The birds cried differently at different hours: in the morning sharply, briefly, as if waking one another; by noon more quietly, more lazily; in the evening loudly again, but differently — not calling, but taking leave, as if saying good night to the world.

He explored the island — carefully, a yard at a time. He found an elevation on the eastern side, a low hill of reddish stone overgrown with stiff grass. From it one could see the sea to the very horizon — in all directions, without obstruction. He climbed it several times a day, peering into the emptiness. Nothing. Only water, water and water, and the line where sky met sea, and sometimes — clouds that drifted about their own business, paying no attention to the man on the rock.

He stood and watched. In the North, when one was lost in a blizzard, one too climbed a height and looked: was there smoke, fire, movement anywhere. There was no blizzard here, but the principle was the same: you stand on a height and search. A sail, smoke, a boat — anything. Nothing. August came down, went to the stream, drank, ate, checked the traps, mended the shelter, and climbed the hill again. Nothing again. And so every day.

He memorized where the edible fruit grew — three kinds of trees he had found in the first days. The first — with small green figs, sourish, astringent, but filling.

The second — with yellow, oval fruit, sweet, cloying, with mealy flesh. The third — with large, hard, green spheres that had to be cut open with the sword to reach the white, milky core. All three — edible, proven, safe. He memorized where they grew, how many fruits each tree bore, how quickly new ones ripened. The island fed him, and August was grateful, though the gratitude was wordless, without speech, without gesture: he simply took what was given and took no more.

He found edible roots — long, thin, with brown skin — which he dug out with Bjorn's knife. He washed them in the stream, roasted them on the coals. The taste was starchy, bland, but filling. In the North they ate roots too — willow, birch — and Bjorn taught: "A root is the earth's reserve. It hides its best in it, the way a mother hides sweets from her children." August thought of this as he dug, and there was truth in Bjorn's words: the roots were faintly sweet, though bland, and in that sweetness there was something maternal, as if the earth truly cared for those who knew how to seek it.

He learned to catch fish. Not only the ones thrown up on the shore — living ones. He made a spear from a long branch, whittled it with the knife, sharpened the tip, hardened it in the fire. He stood in the shallow water by the rocks, motionless as a heron, and waited. The fish were small, silvery, quick, but sometimes they froze, and then August struck — quickly, precisely, as Bjorn had taught: "Don't aim at the fish. Aim at where it will be. The water bends, and you see not where the fish is, but where it seems to be." August did not at first understand "bends" — it was a northern word that did not translate. It meant: water bends the light, and the fish is not where you see it. You must strike a little lower and a little ahead. He tried — missed. Tried again — missed. On the fifth try he hit. The fish thrashed on the spear, silvery, alive, and August felt what he always felt when he won his food: not joy, not triumph, but quiet satisfaction. Another day lived through. Another hunger stilled.

The first fire on the island was already there — made by friction in the early days. But August perfected it. He built a hearth: stones gathered on the shore, flat, heavy, laid in a circle. Inside — coals that smouldered all night if covered properly with ash.

Outside — stones that held the heat and shielded from the wind. In the North they made hearths like this at winter camps: stones for the base, moss for insulation, ash for protection. Here, instead of moss — dry grass; instead of ash — sand. Different materials, the same principle. The fire became constant — not as a luxury but as a necessity: for food, for warmth, for light in the night, so as not to go mad.

Because madness was a real threat. Not physical — physically August managed. Water, food, fire, shelter — all there. But the silence... Silence was an enemy no sword could strike. Day after day, each one resembling the last, and in that sameness the mind began to slide, as on ice. August felt it: sometimes he caught himself standing on the shore looking at the water, not knowing how long he had stood. A minute? An hour? Two? Time flowed differently here — not by the clock, which he did not have, but by the sun, and the sun moved along the same path, and every sunset resembled the last, and every dawn, and every noon.

He fought it as best he could. He spoke aloud. To no one — he simply spoke. He told himself about the North, about Bjorn, about Signe, about Teir, about the shaman, about how they lived, what they ate, how they hunted, how they laughed. He told about his mother — the one who bore him, and the one who found him. He told about the ship, about Hasan, about Dariush, about the captain. His voice sounded strange in that silence — loud, unnatural, as if someone else's. But August went on. In the North the shaman said: "Words are the ropes that tie you to the world. Stop speaking — and you come untied." August did not want to come untied.

Sometimes he sang. Without the guitar — with his voice alone, quietly, so as not to frighten the birds. The songs were northern, the ones he remembered, and they sounded strange here, in the tropics, among palms and warm wind. But they sounded. And while they sounded, August was tied to the world, and the world did not forget him.

One morning, coming out of the shelter, he noticed wreckage on the shore.

At first he thought it was a trick of the eye. He stood, squinting into the sun that struck his eyes, and looked. No, not a trick. On the sand, scattered along the shore for a dozen paces, lay scorched planks, pieces of wood, tangled ropes, something else — dark, wet, thrown up by the surf. He came closer, and his heart fell, then leapt, then fell again — all in one instant.

He recognized them. The remains of the ship. The pirates had set it alight, and the sea had cast its remains onto this shore. Perhaps by current, perhaps by wind, perhaps simply by a wave that did not know what it carried. The planks were scorched, black, with smooth edges gnawed by the fire. Some still warm, as if the fire had been recent, though August knew many days had passed. Perhaps the wood held the heat of the sun. Perhaps memory.

He went around the wreckage, examining what might have survived. Carefully, turning the planks with his foot, looking under every piece. He found a piece of sail — dirty, scorched at the edges, but strong, dense, able to hold out rain and wind. A long rope, almost untouched by fire — wet, but whole, and August knew: when it dried, it would be as good as new. A broken oar — the handle scorched, but the blade intact, and the wood was hard, good. It would serve for digging, for building, for defence.

And among all of it — the guitar case.

August stopped. He froze the way one freezes in the North upon seeing a wolf: not from fear, but from the impossibility of believing. The case lay on the sand, wet, worn, with dark stains — perhaps from fire, perhaps from water, perhaps from both. The leather straps that fastened it were in place. The case was whole. Not opened, not torn, not burned. Whole.

He could not believe his eyes. It simply could not be. The ship had burned — he had seen it burn, seen the fire devour the sails, the masts, the deck. The guitar had been in the hold, below, and the fire should have reached it first. But the sea... The sea had taken it from the fire. Perhaps, when the ship went down, the hold had flooded and the guitar had floated up. Perhaps it had been thrown out by the explosion when the fire reached the barrels of oil. Perhaps simply a miracle. In the North the shaman said: "A miracle is when the world decides you have earned one more chance." August did not know whether he had earned it. But the miracle was here, on the sand, wet and worn.

He dropped to his knees. His hands trembled — not from weariness, not from cold, but from something else that had no name. Tenderly he undid the leather straps — slowly, carefully, the way one opens a door behind which anything might be. The case opened.

The guitar lay inside. Darkened by moisture, with streaks of salt on the body — but whole. The strings were in place, though slack and loosened, and one — the thick lower one — had snapped. The wood had warped with the damp, and the body had bowed slightly, but had not cracked. The ribs were whole. The neck too. The headstock — in place, the tuning pegs — in place. The guitar had survived. As he had. As the sword. As the knife.

He sat on the sand, holding it in his arms, and was silent for a long time. The sea roared. The birds cried. The wind tugged at his hair. August sat and was silent, and in that silence was everything he could not say: gratitude, wonder, longing, relief, and something deeper still that had no words. Then he said quietly — not to himself, not to the sea, not to the sky, but to her:

"You survived too."

His voice sounded hoarse, unfamiliar — he had not spoken in a long time. But the words were right. The guitar had survived, and he had survived, and they were together again, and in this was something beyond logic and chance. In the North, Bjorn said: "There are things that seek each other. Like a wolf and the pack. Like a river and the sea. If you part them — they will find their way back." August did not know whether he believed this. But the guitar was here.

The finds went to the shelter. The guitar — to dry in the shade, under the awning, not in the sun: the sun could dry the wood too fast, and it would crack. He laid it on a layer of dry grass and covered it with the piece of sail — loosely, so there was air. So that it dried slowly, properly, the way meat dries in the North: not by the fire, but in the shade, in dry air, gradually. He removed the strings — those that remained — and laid them on warm stones by the fire. Not on hot — on warm. So they dried but did not overstretch. He replaced the broken string with a length of fishing line found among the wreckage — perhaps from that same ship, perhaps from another. The line was thin and strong; the sound would not be like a gut string's, but better than nothing.

He dried the rope and strengthened the shelter's roof — lashed the branches, cinched the joints, tightened the sagging places. The roof grew denser, more reliable. He drove the broken oar into the ground by the entrance — it would serve for digging, as a lever, and as a last means of defence should someone unexpected come. He spread the piece of sail in the sun — let it dry. It was the first thing he owned that was more than a scrap of cloth. From it one could make clothing, an awning, a sail for a raft — if ever he had to flee. For now — let it dry.

He waited several days while the guitar dried. He checked every day: touched the wood, felt for dampness, looked for cracks. On the third day the wood felt dry to the touch — not wet, not damp, but dry, warm, alive. The strings dried and tightened, but were far from their best: rusty, with a white crust of salt, sounding muffled, like voices with a cold. He cleaned them with sand — carefully, so as not to damage them. Stretched them back in place. Tuned — as best he could: the pegs had warped with the damp and would not hold evenly; he had to adjust constantly. But that was a trifle. The main thing — the guitar was whole.

In the evening, when the sun had gone below the horizon and the sky darkened, he took the guitar in his hands. He sat at the entrance of the shelter, by the fire, and ran his fingers over the strings.

The sound was muffled, distant, not what it had been. Not clean, not ringing, but dull, as if from behind a wall. The wood had not yet come back to itself, the strings had not settled, and the pegs slipped. But it was music. It was his voice. That voice, which had been silent for weeks, which lived only in his head, in the silence, in the sound of the waves — now it sounded. Faintly, uncertainly, like the voice of a man who had long been silent and forgotten how to speak. But it sounded.

He played. Not a song — he simply plucked the strings, listening to how they answered his touch. Each note was not born at once: first a trembling, then a sound, quiet, uncertain, as if testing the air. Then — a little louder, a little surer. His fingers remembered where to press, where to release, where to draw a nail. The body remembered what the mind had forgotten: positions, chords, transitions. The music came not from the head — from the hands, from the memory of the fingers, from those places where it had lived all this time, while there was no guitar.

He sat at the entrance of the shelter, watched the sea, and played. The sounds were imperfect, but true. Muffled, cold-rasped, with hoarseness and tremor — but true.

Like himself. In the North the shaman said: "True music is not the kind that is flawless. The kind that is honest." The guitar sounded honestly. And that was enough.

For the first time in a long while he felt not quite alone. The guitar was not a person, not a friend, not a companion. It was something greater: a part of himself that had been lost and found. Like a hand that was severed and sewn back on: at first it does not obey, then it begins to stir, then it grasps, holds, feels. So with the guitar: at first — muffled, uncertain sounds; then — cleaner, more precise; and one day — the same music as before. Perhaps not quite the same. Perhaps different. But his.

He played every evening. At first — just arpeggios, chords, exercises, so the fingers would remember and the strings would loosen. Then — songs. The ones he remembered: northern ones, of snow and wind, of silence and fire. He played quietly so as not to frighten the birds, and the birds grew accustomed: after a few days they settled on the branches nearby and listened, heads cocked, as if they understood. Perhaps they did. In the North the shaman said that beasts and birds hear music in their own way — not as people, but as the world. August did not know how the world hears. But the world listened.

The sound improved with every day. The wood came back to itself: the moisture left, the shape straightened, and the body began to resonate as before — not as loudly, not as purely, but alive. The strings tightened, and the new one — of line — sounded no worse than the old ones, even with a timbre of its own, sharper, more ringing. August fitted the pegs with Bjorn's knife: pared, shaved, added wood chips so they would hold tighter. The guitar was becoming a guitar. Not what it had been — but a guitar.

Within a week the sound was nearly what it had been. Not quite — what was, could not be brought back — but enough to play properly. And August began to play not for himself — for the island. He sat on the hill, the one from which the sea was visible, and played, and the music flew over the water, over the palms, over the rocks. The birds circled near. The crabs froze by the water. The wind hushed, as if listening. And August felt: the world hears him. A small, lost island in an endless ocean — it hears. And it answers: with the sound of waves, the cry of birds, the rustle of leaves. A dialogue without words. Music without listeners. And still — a dialogue.

After a time he began to build a house. Not a shelter — a house. The shelter was for sleeping, for protection from rain and wind. A house — for living. A difference August understood not at once, but only when the guitar was found. While there was no guitar — there was a shelter. When the guitar returned — a house was needed. A place where one can not only sleep but live. A place that not only protects, but holds.

In the North, a house was called the place where the hearth burns. Not the walls, not the roof — the hearth. If there is a fire that does not go out — there is a house. If not — merely a camp. August understood it in his own way: a house is the place where the guitar stands. If the guitar hangs on the wall and not lies in its case — it is a house. If it is in the case — a stopover. The difference was in intent: the case is for the road, the wall is for life.

He chose a place — between two rocks, in the natural hollow where the shelter already stood. But the shelter was small, cramped, temporary. The house must be bigger. It must stand. He began with the base: levelled the ground, removed stones, roots, grass. Tamped the sand, wetted it, let it dry. The result was an even, firm platform — neither earth nor sand but something between, dense, able to bear weight.

The walls — of branches. Not simply driven into the ground as in the shelter, but woven one into another, like a basket. In the North they wove the walls of winter houses this way: branches for the frame, moss for the filling, hides for insulation. Here, instead of moss — dry grass; instead of hides — leaves. He selected branches by length and thickness: long ones for the frame, short ones for the filling. Supple ones for weaving, hard ones for the corners. Each row over the previous one, and every joint overlapping, so there were no gaps.

The walls came out strong. He tested them: pushed with his shoulder — they held. Kicked with his foot — they held. In the North, Bjorn said: "A wall holds not because it is thick, but because it is interwoven. Branches one by one — they break. Woven into each other — they stand." August wove, and they stood.

He made the roof of palm leaves laid in layers — like shingles. Each leaf over the previous one, stem upward, so the water ran down the veins and did not linger. There were five layers: the lowest coarse, of large stiff leaves; then thinner, softer ones; and the topmost of the youngest, supple leaves that bent and did not break. The roof came out dense, thick, able to hold the rain. He tested it: poured water from the stream over it — it did not leak. In the North they tested snow roofs this way: they poured water over them, and if it did not leak — it held.

He reinforced the corners with stones — flat, heavy, laid one upon another like a foundation. The stones held the walls, the walls held the roof, the roof held out the rain. Everything held everything. And so it should be: in a house everything is connected, as in a body. In the North the shaman said: "A house is a body. The walls are bones, the roof is skin, the fire is the heart. If one thing breaks — everything aches." August built so that nothing would ache.

Inside — he levelled the floor and strewn it with dry grass and leaves. The result was a soft, warm bedding that held the warmth of the body and did not let in the cold of the ground. Though the cold here was not northern — not the kind that freezes the bones — the ground cooled at night, and sleeping on bare sand was unpleasant. The bedding solved that.

He built a hearth of stones — the same flat, heavy ones as for the corners. Laid in a circle, three rows deep, with sand inside so the coals would not touch the ground. The hearth stood at the centre, like the heart in a body: from it came warmth, light, life. There was no chimney, but he left an opening in the roof, covered by a large leaf that could be pushed aside. The smoke went upward, not sideways. In the North they did this in dugouts: an opening in the roof — and the smoke leaves by itself, because warm air rises.

He hung the guitar on the wall. On a peg whittled from a hard branch, driven between two rows of the weaving. The guitar hung straight, level, and August stepped back and looked at it, and there was something right in it. Not a shelter — a house. Not a case — a wall. Not a stopover — a life.

Then he made shelves. Of flat stones and branches: the stones as supports, the branches as crossbars. Three shelves: on the top — the fruit gathered in the morning. On the middle — Bjorn's knife, the whetstone, lengths of rope. On the bottom — the piece of sail, dried fish, a store of roots. Order. In the North, Bjorn said: "Order is not cleanliness. Order is when every object knows its place. If an object does not know its place — you are not at home." August laid everything in its place, and every object knew where to lie.

He made a door. Not a door — a curtain: a piece of sail hung on a rope at the entrance. It shut out wind and rain, but not sound: the sound of the sea, the cries of birds, the rustle of leaves — everything passed through the cloth, and August heard the world even when he was inside. That was important: in a house one must not shut oneself off from the world. In the North the doors were heavy, of hide, and behind them it was quiet — but that quiet was of another kind: warm, safe, inside the pack. Here — solitude, and shutting oneself off from the world was dangerous: one might forget the world exists.

Outside, by the entrance, — a hearth for cooking. Separate from the inner one: here the fire was not for warmth but for food. Stones, coals, a flat stone for roasting. Over the hearth — an awning of branches and leaves, so rain would not drown the fire. Nearby — the crab traps, the fish spear, a pouch of leaves for gathering fruit. Everything within reach. Everything in its place.

The house stood. Small, cramped, of branches and leaves — but a house. Not a shelter, not an awning, not a pit in the ground. A house. A place where one can stand at full height. A place where the guitar hangs on the wall. A place where fire burns.

August sat by the hearth, gazing at the sea, and felt the place cease to be merely an island. It was becoming his home. Not the home he had left — Alba-Mare had burned, the North was behind him, his mother far away. Another. A new one. A place he had built himself, from what he found, from what the island gave. A place where his father's sword lay nearby, Bjorn's knife on the shelf, the guitar on the wall. A place where the fire did not go out, and there was food, and water flowed from the rock, and the stars burned above the roof.

In the North, Bjorn said: "A home is not walls. A home is the place where you decided to be." August had decided. Not because he wanted to — because he had to. But the decision was his, and the place was his, and the house was his. Small, at the edge of the world, invisible to anyone. But his.

He played long that evening. He sat at the entrance, watched the stars, and played — quietly, calmly, unhurried. The songs were different: of the North, of the sea, of people who were no more. Of Hasan, who laughed. Of Dariush, who touched his amulet. Of the captain, who slept little. Of Bjorn, who said: "You are the road." Of his mother, who baked a pie with figs and almonds.

The music flew over the water, over the island, over the dark, silent ocean. No one heard it. Only the birds, only the wind, only the waves. But August played, and in this was everything that was needed: sound, silence, fire, and a house that stood at his back like a wall, like protection, like a promise.

He did not know how long he would stay here. He did not know whether anyone would find him, whether a ship would come, whether anyone would see the smoke. He did not know whether beyond the horizon there was a world that remembered him. But he knew one thing: while the house stands, while the fire burns, while the guitar hangs on the wall — he is alive. Not merely surviving — living. The difference was this: surviving is when you fight. Living is when you build.

And August built.
