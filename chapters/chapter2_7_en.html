They set out in the dark, before the sky had begun to lighten. At that hour the desert was alien and cold — as if the world had not yet woken and had not decided whether it was worth letting people in at all. The camels walked evenly, steadily, as if they knew the road better than the drovers. Their heavy footsteps sounded in the silence almost solemnly, and the crunch of sand under August's feet was the only voice breaking that soundless rhythm.

He felt the air gradually warming, the shadows growing shorter, the desert growing wider, as if with every step the land drew back, opening ever new expanses. The sun had not yet risen over the horizon, but it already promised heat that would squeeze the strength out of anyone bold enough to walk.

The first day passed without incident. Only sand, stone and sparse bushes clinging to life in that harsh land. At the rest stop Kane silently handed out flatbreads and water, portioning them with the practiced stinginess of a man who knows the worth of every crumb. August ate his share unhurriedly, listening to his own body: it remembered the northern cold, but the desert heat was a different enemy — quiet, patient, gnawing from within. Then they moved on.

The second day was the same. The desert seemed endless, as if reality itself lost its meaning here, leaving only the road, the step, and the breath. August had grown used to the rhythm: step, step, breath, step. The camels did not falter; they walked confidently, as if their memory kept paths forgotten by men. The drovers said not a word — in the desert superfluous sounds seemed almost blasphemy, as if they might disturb silence itself.

On the third day everything changed.

They were passing between two low dunes when the sand before them suddenly exploded with movement. Ten figures leapt from behind a ridge, blending with the color of the desert: light garments of sandy hue covered their bodies completely, and only the eyes were visible — dark, attentive, predatory. In their hands were curved blades, spears; one carried a round wooden shield and a curved blade resembling a kukri.

August did not even have time to draw his sword — his body was already moving, remembering battle before his mind could grasp the danger. The reflexes forged in the north worked instantly: a step, a turn, a thrust.

The drovers cried out, rushed to the camels, snatched up their spears — thin, a meter and a half long, with iron tips. They stood around the animals, shielding the cargo, as if the camels themselves mattered more than their own lives. Kane drew a short dagger but immediately fell back behind August, closer to the camels. There was no cowardice in his movement — only a sober understanding: he was a trader, not a warrior.

August drew his father's sword. The blade was heavy, sturdy, real — as if it held the memory of those who had held it before him. He stepped forward, shielding the others with himself.

The first brigand came at him with a shout, swinging his blade. August slipped aside and struck — one blow, precisely to the neck. The body fell to the sand, and the blood was instantly swallowed by the dry earth, as if the desert greedily accepted the offering.

The second charged with a spear. August caught the shaft, wrenched it toward himself, and in the same instant the blade entered his opponent's chest. The third, with the curved blade, managed a swing, but August was faster: the sword split his shoulder, and the brigand fell, clutching the wound, his cry drowning in the hot air.

The remaining brigands hesitated for a moment. They had not expected such resistance: they were used to their prey either fleeing or freezing in fear. But August was neither.

Then their leader — the one with the shield and the kukri — stepped forward. He shouted something in a sharp, clipped voice, and four of them rushed at the camels, while three, together with him, charged at August.

The drovers met the attackers with spears. One of them cried out and fell, clutching his shoulder: the enemy's spearhead had gone in deep. Kane defended himself with the dagger, but it was clear he did not know how to fight — he simply held on, trying not to let himself be killed, and in that desperate effort there was something almost touching.

The leader attacked first: shield forward, kukri from the side. August parried the blow, sprang back, and immediately cut at the legs of the nearest brigand. The man crashed down, gasping for air. The second charged with a spear — August slipped aside, caught the shaft and struck him with his own weapon, putting into the blow all the weight of the weariness and strain of recent days. The shaft smashed into the attacker's face, and he toppled onto the sand.

The third came at him with the curved blade, but August had already turned, and his sword entered the brigand's side with a frightening precision.

Only the leader remained.

He stood motionless, looking at August. Shield, kukri, sand, blood — all merged into that brief, ringing silence. August stepped forward. The leader took a step back. Then he threw a quick glance at his men — they lay on the sand, and not one of them moved. Something like surprise flickered in his eyes, and then something like recognition. He wheeled sharply and ran, vanishing behind the dune as suddenly as he had appeared. August did not pursue him. He stood, breathing hard, and watched the figure dissolve in the shimmering air.

The other brigands, those who had tried to attack the camels, already lay dead. The drovers stood over them with bloodied spears, their faces pale but resolute. It was over.

August wiped off the blood with a strip of cloth cut from the clothing of a dead bandit, sheathed his sword and went to the wounded drover. The man held his shoulder; blood seeped from the wound, thick and dark. The wound was deep but not mortal — if one made it in time.

“Sit,” said August. “Let me look.”

He unslung the waterskin from his belt, warmed through the day by the desert. The water was warm, almost hot, but clean. He poured a little over the wound, washing it. Then he took from his pack a leather pouch with northern herbs — a mixture he had gathered back in Holmstad, when Ulrik had taught him to tell plants apart by smell and shape. He soaked the herbs in water, laid them on a piece of cloth torn from the drover's clothing, and bound it carefully over the wound.

The drover looked at him with respect in which there was relief and gratitude. Kane came closer; his face was tense, and in his eyes a question could be read that for a long time he did not dare ask.

“Where did you get that?” he asked, nodding at the herbs.

“From the North,” said August. “They know how to heal there too. Not only to make war.”

He rose and surveyed the field of battle. The bodies of the brigands lay strewn across the sand like discarded rags, as if the life that had pulsed in those bodies only recently had simply dissolved in the heat. The third one — the one August had struck in the shoulder — was still breathing: heavily, with a rattle, each breath costing him effort. August went to him. The brigand's gaze was clouded, but fear still glimmered in it — animal, primal, unable to accept the inevitable. August did not wait. One short, precise blow — and it was over. He felt neither anger nor pity. Only necessity — as cold and simple as the shadow of a rock at noon.

Then he began to gather the weapons. He took blades and spears from the dead brigands and piled them up. On one of them he found a small leather pouch with coins — several silver and copper discs. He hid it in his pack. From another he took a ring from a finger — dark, but with an unmistakable gleam of gold, with a red stone that in the midday light looked like a drop of frozen blood. He turned it in his fingers, then thrust it into his pocket. He was no marauder. He did not rob the dead for gain. But he was a hunter: he had killed those who had wanted to kill him, and took what he could carry. So the northerners taught: do not leave your quarry to those who cannot defend it.

“Take what will be useful,” said Kane, watching him. “The rest we leave to the sand.”

August selected one more sturdy blade and several throwing knives. Among the brigands' things he found a piece of dense cloth of sandy hue — evidently part of someone's clothing or cloak. He laid it on the sand, put the blades and knives inside, bound it with a strap taken from the dead man, and fastened the bundle to his pack. The weight grew greater, but he knew: this was not robbery. These were trophies. Payment for having survived.

He nodded, rose, gave the field of battle a final look. The bodies were already beginning to cool; the sand was drinking up the blood, as if it wished to hide the traces of that brief, cruel encounter. August felt neither triumph nor regret. Only weariness — heavy, thick, like the shadow that settles on the ground after sunset. He left the rest lying on the sand — together with the bodies that in a few hours would become part of the desert, turned to dust and salt.

They moved on. Until night they walked in silence. The desert was dark, and only the stars lit the way — cold and indifferent, like witnesses who never intervene.

Toward nightfall Kane pointed to a small hollow between the rocks — a natural shelter, protected from the wind. The camels lay down at the entrance, blocking the passage with their bodies, as if understanding that today they must be the wall. The drovers silently unsaddled the animals and checked the cargo. Kane gathered dry tumbleweed branches the wind had carried from afar, and several cakes of dried dung — which the drovers carried with them specially for such occasions. A small fire flared up with an uneven, hot flame, and all sat around it — quietly, wearily, without superfluous words.

At the rest stop, when the fire had nearly burned down, August sat by the flames, gazing into emptiness. The flame trembled, casting shadows on the rocks, and the shadows seemed alive, as if whispering something in a forgotten language. Kane sat down beside him.

“You are no ordinary bard,” he said.

“I was a guard,” August answered. “Long ago. In a city by the sea.”

“And then?”

“And then I was a northerner. I lived among them for three years.”

Kane was silent, looking into the fire. The flame was reflected in his eyes, making them brighter than they had been by day.

“You have a good blade,” he said at last.

“It is my father's sword,” August answered. “He died defending the city. I carried his body to the shore and burned it on a pyre. That is how we said farewell to those we loved.”

Kane said nothing. He only nodded and looked long into the fire, as if trying to make out in it something more than mere warmth and light. Then he rose and went into the darkness, leaving August alone with his memories and with a silence that was, just then, the most honest of interlocutors.
