The city was called Trade Cliff — or, as the locals called it, the Fortress on the Hill. It stood at the crossroads of roads running north to south and west to east. Here met caravans from the steppes, hunters from the forests, fishermen from the eastern coast, and the rare merchants who came from beyond the mountains. The city was not large by the measure of Vistaria, but it was alive — noisy, dusty, smelling of leather, spices and roasted meat. It did not dazzle like Alba-Mare, did not overwhelm like Vistaria — it simply breathed, and in that breathing there was so much of everything that one wanted to stop and listen the way one listens to a river: not for the words, but for the flow.

He entered through the southern gates when the sun already stood high. The gates were of stone, heavy, bound with iron long rusted by time and wind. The guards at the entrance — two of them, sun-tanned, in light leather breastplates — gave him a look, exchanged glances and nodded. They did not stop him. Did not question him. Here they were used to strangers: the city stood at a crossroads, and every day dozens of people no one knew passed through the gates. August nodded in return and stepped inside.

Along the main street pressed shops, workshops and open stalls. Traders shouted prices in a dozen languages, women haggled, children ran between the legs of passers-by, ducking under carts and reappearing with stolen fruit. Everything was as in any other city, and yet — different. Here they spoke a dozen dialects, and none of them was the main one. A merchant from the east haggled with a hunter from the north, and both made themselves understood with gestures, interspersing them with words from foreign languages that had already become common. A street cook was frying something over coals, and the smoke from his brazier mingled with the smell of dust, leather and spices, creating that particular, dense aroma of an eastern city one can mistake for nothing else.

He walked through the market, looking over the goods. Furs, dried fish, clay pots, forged knives, bundles of dried herbs, pieces of colored stone, copper bracelets, leather sandals, sacks of grain, cages with live birds. His eyes scattered, but August did not hurry. He walked slowly, growing used to the noise, the smells, the rhythm, which was nothing like the northern one.

There, silence was the norm and noise the event. Here it was all the other way around, and it demanded a restructuring the body had not yet learned.

He stopped at a stall with fruit. Red, yellow, green — he did not know their names, but the smell was sweet, almost intoxicating. He reached toward one, but his hand stopped. He had no coins. He had nothing to offer except hare pelts, and those had already been left in Teir's village. For the first time in a long while he felt truly foreign: not where one does not know the language, but where one cannot pay for a piece of fruit.

He moved away from the stall and walked on — no longer looking, but searching. The city was small — one could cross from one gate to the other in half an hour — but densely populated. The houses stood close, their walls almost touching, and between them remained narrow alleys through which a man with a pack could barely squeeze. Children played in the dust, women sat by thresholds, sorting grain or mending clothes, old men dozed in the shade of awnings. Life here flowed not in the streets but in the doorways: everything happened on the border between house and world, between shadow and light.

When the sun began to lean toward the west, he understood he needed to find lodging. Not from weariness — his body was used to long marches — but because a night in an unfamiliar city without a roof overhead was a luxury he could not afford. Not here. Not now.

The tavern stood at the crossing of two streets, by an old well. Two stories, of grey stone, with a flat roof where bundles of herbs were drying. Above the door hung a faded sign depicting a staff and a traveling bag. Beneath the sign — a wooden board on which, in two languages, was carved: “The Wayfarer.” August smiled faintly. The name was simple and precise, like the blow of an axe.

The door was open, and from within came the hum of voices, the clink of clay mugs and the smell of roasted meat, of which his stomach reminded him. He entered.

Inside it was spacious: a long hall with a low ceiling, several tables, a hearth at the far wall, at which a woman in an apron was busy. The smoke from the hearth mingled with the smell of food and wine, and together they created that atmosphere which exists only in places where travelers are fed and given drink: warmth, bustle, and a temporary semblance of home.

There were not many people: a few men at various tables, two playing dice in a corner, an old man dozing by the wall. August sat down at an empty table by the window, set his pack on the floor, the guitar on the bench beside him. He waited.

The owner appeared a minute later. A heavy man with a red face, thick arms, and eyes that sized up a visitor faster than the visitor could speak. His gaze slid over the guitar, the worn clothing, the sword at the belt, the tattoos on the arms, and in it flickered the expression August had already learned to recognize: calculation. Not hostile, not contemptuous, but reckoning: what can be taken from this man, and what he can give.

“You have money?” asked the owner without preliminaries.

“No,” said August. “I can play. For lodging and food.”

The owner looked at him long. His lower lip thrust out, as if he were chewing something invisible. Then he waved a hand, the way one waves off a fly: a gesture at once dismissive and offering a chance.

“There are many like you here,” he said. “All of them play. All of them want to eat. But if you want — show me. If I like it, a room will be found. If not — the door is there.”

August nodded. He took the guitar out of its case, turned it in his hands, growing reaccustomed to its weight after the road. The strings, from damp and changes of temperature, had gone out of tune: two were slightly flat, one sharp. He began to tune them. Slowly, listening to each sound, turning the pegs. He did not hurry. In the North he had learned: if you do something before people, do it calmly, even if you are in a hurry inside. Hurry is visible and frightens. Calm is visible and inspires trust.

The people at the tables paid no attention: one bard in a tavern is no event. But when the first notes sounded, someone raised a head. The sound was soft but clean, and in it was something that made people listen: not mastery, not complexity, but truthfulness. That is how music sounds that does not try to please but simply speaks.

He played a melody he had composed the day before, on the road. It was about two tongues sounding in one voice: about the northern wind and the southern sun, about how a man walks between worlds and belongs to none of them. It began low, slow, like a step through snow, then rose, warm as breath, and fell silent on a note that was neither major nor minor, but simply alive.

Then a second. A third. The third was about the road — not about any particular road, but about all roads at once: about how the legs remember the rhythm while the heart forgets why it walks. He sang quietly, hardly opening his mouth, and his voice was low, with a northern rasp that did not grate but drew one in, like the warmth of a fire that had nearly burned down yet still holds.

By the end of the third song, silence had settled over the hall. Not at once: first those sitting nearby fell quiet, then those at the far tables, then even the dice players. Someone stopped chewing, someone turned around; at the very entrance a woman with a jug froze, never reaching her table. It was not the silence that comes from fear or awkwardness, but the kind that comes when people hear something real and are afraid to startle it, as one fears to startle a rare bird that has alighted on the windowsill.

When he finished, the tavern grew quiet. Then someone from a far corner tossed a coin into the empty case lying on the bench. The ring of copper on leather was soft, but it woke the hall: someone stirred, someone nodded, someone poured himself more wine. Life returned, but gentler, as if the music had slightly dissolved the roughness that accumulates in people living at the crossroads of roads.

He raised his eyes and saw the owner setting before him a plate with bread and meat, a bowl of stew, and a mug of dark, sourish drink.

“Play more,” he said. “And stay.”

August looked at him, at the plate, at the mug. He took the bread, broke off a piece, dipped it in the stew. The meat was hot and rich, with a tang of some spices he did not know, but which were good. He ate slowly — not from hunger, but out of respect for the food. Then he took the guitar and played again.

He stayed in the city several days. The owner of the tavern, whose name was Thorn, allotted him a small room on the second floor — narrow, with a low ceiling, with a small window opening onto an inner courtyard where laundry dried and chickens ran. The bed was hard but clean, and after weeks on the ground it seemed a luxury. The pillow smelled of lavender, and that smell was so unlike everything he had felt in recent months that the first night he simply lay there, breathing it in, unable to sleep.

For the room he played in the evenings, and by day he helped with the household: carrying water from the well, chopping wood, mending an old cart that Thorn had long meant to repair but had never found the time. August did not object. He liked working with his hands. It recalled those days when he had been a guard in Alba-Mare, and those when he had helped Ulrik in Holmstad. But here there was its own, particular atmosphere: dry, dusty, steeped in the smells of eastern spices and roasted meat. The firewood here was different: not northern birch, but something softer, lighter, with a resinous smell, which burned quickly and hot. The water from the well was warm, not icy, and did not need to be melted from snow. The small things that would have been luxuries in the North were the norm here, and August learned not to be surprised by them but to accept them as part of a new world.

Thorn proved a simple and straightforward man. Neither wicked nor kind, but businesslike: he saw in people what they could give and was not shy about using it. But he was fair: if a man worked, he fed him. If he played well, he gave him a room. If not — he pointed to the door. August liked that honesty. No smiles, no superfluous words, no falseness. Like Bjorn, only without the snow.

In the evenings he played in the tavern. Every evening — at nightfall, when the hall filled and the air thickened with smoke, the smells of food and voices. The people listened with interest — not with that greedy attention with which one listens to a rare guest, but with trust. Here, at the crossroads, they were used to strangers, and a stranger's music did not alarm but soothed: it meant the world had not yet gone mad if a wanderer can come in, sit down and play.

He played of the sea, of the North, of the road. Of how a man can be a stranger everywhere and at home nowhere. Of how hard it is to walk when you do not know where you will stop. Of nights by the fire, when the stars are closer than people. Of the white walls of Alba-Mare that are no more. Of the northern lights, which he had seen once and which had changed him forever. He spoke no words between songs — only played, and that was enough. The people here did not await explanations; they awaited a sound that would say for them what they could not say themselves.

One evening, when the tavern was full, an argument broke out at one of the tables. First loud, then — with shouts. Two men, one tall, the other stocky, could not divide something between them. Perhaps a debt, perhaps a place, perhaps the woman who sat between them with a frightened face. Fists clenched, a chair fell, someone recoiled, someone reached for a knife.

August set the guitar aside. He rose unhurriedly and walked over to them. He moved calmly, without threat, but without hesitation either. In the North he had learned: when a fight is brewing, the most dangerous thing is to wait. The strongest thing is to stand between them before it is too late. Bjorn used to say: “The best fight is the one that never began. The second best is the one that ended with a single word.”

He took the stocky one by the scruff — firmly, but without malice — and the tall one by the shoulder, and, without raising his voice, said:

“Outside.”

He did not wait for an answer. He did not persuade, did not explain. He simply led them toward the door, and they went — less out of fear than out of surprise. The hand on the scruff was heavy but not rough, and the voice even, like that of a man who knows he could, if it came to it, but does not wish it to. Outside, in the cool air, he released both. He looked at each. The stocky one rubbed his neck; the tall one snorted. He said quietly:

“Settle it not here. Not now. Morning is wiser than evening.”

They stood. Glared at one another. Then the stocky one spat, the tall one turned away, and they parted. Not as friends, but not as enemies either — simply two men who had been given the chance not to become either.

When the door closed behind him, he returned to his place, took the guitar and went on playing as if nothing had happened. His fingers found the strings, and the melody flowed on, as though it had never been interrupted. The tavern grew quiet. Then someone chuckled, someone shook his head, and the noise returned. But something had changed: the people at the tables now looked at him not as a bard, but as someone who knew how not only to play, but to hold.

The owner came over late in the evening, when the guests had dispersed and the last candle was burning down. He sat across from him, set between them two mugs of the dark drink, drank from his own.

“Well done,” he said. “I thought: a bard has come — what can you get from him? He sings songs, and if anything happens he'll hide in a corner. But you've got character.”

August looked at him, took the mug, drank. The drink was bitterish, warm, with a tang of some herbs.

“I did not want the music interrupted,” he said.

“Good,” said Thorn. “Stay. I need such men. Not only to play — to keep an eye on order too. Not as a guard, no. Just… to have someone there.”

August nodded. Not because he wanted to stay, but because he understood: sometimes one must linger in order to move on. Teir had said: “Cities draw you in. They give food, shelter, talk. But they do not give the road.” Thorn gave food and shelter. The road August would find himself.

The next evening, when the tavern was nearly full and the air in it had thickened with smoke and smells to the point where it was hard to breathe, Thorn approached a table where sat a short man in a traveling cloak. They spoke quietly of something, and Thorn nodded toward August. The man turned, looked. Unhurriedly, attentively, like a buyer examining wares at a market. Then he turned back to Thorn, said something, and Thorn went behind the counter, while the man rose and walked toward August's table.

The man was short, wiry, with a face on which the tan lay so densely the skin looked like tanned leather. His eyes were dark, quick, tenacious — the kind that see more than they show. The cloak was a traveling one, worn, but of good cut, of dense wool that did not soak through. At his belt — a short dagger in a sheath of blackened silver: not a weapon for battle, but a mark of status. On his feet — sturdy leather boots studded with nails, the kind worn by those who walk much on stone and sand.

He sat down across from him without asking permission. He set his elbows on the table, laced his fingers. He looked.

“You are a bard,” he said — not a question but a statement, the way one sets a period at the end of a line.

“I play,” August corrected.

“Thorn said otherwise. Thorn said you know how to work with your hands, and that you quieted two brawlers without a sword and without shouting. That is not a bard. That is a man who knows what he is doing.”

August was silent. Not out of politeness, but out of habit: in the North he had learned not to answer until he understood what the other wanted. Bjorn had taught: “Keep silent until you hear the price. The price is not money. The price is what a man truly wants from you.”

“I am a trader,” said the man. “My name is Kane. I lead caravans across the desert to the eastern coast and back. This year the caravan will be large: spices, cloth, copper. The road is long — three weeks there, three back. The way is not safe: sandstorms, brigands, wild beasts. I need a strong man. Not for fighting — but just in case. You will receive pay, food and a place by the fire.”

“Why me?” asked August.

Kane raised an eyebrow slightly, as if he had not expected the question.

“Because Thorn trusts you, and Thorn trusts no one. Because you are no thief and no hired blade — that can be seen. Because you are going east, and my caravan is going east. And because you do not ask needless questions.”

“I just asked one,” said August.

Kane looked at him. Then smiled — quickly, briefly, like a flash of knife.

“One question is not needless. Ten are. You asked one. That is good.”

He was silent. He did not hurry. In this was fate: he was not seeking the road — the road itself came to him, as it always had. In Alba-Mare, Arno had found him. In the North, Bjorn. Here, Kane. The world offered him the next door, and all that remained was to open it.

“When do we set out?” he asked.

“Tomorrow at dawn,” said Kane. “The southern gates. Be there when the sun touches the horizon. If you are late, we leave without you.”

“I will be there.”

Kane rose, adjusted his cloak and walked toward the door. At the threshold he stopped and turned around.

“One thing,” he said. “In the desert, what works in the city does not work. There are no walls there, no taverns, no owner who will give you a room. There is only sand, wind and sky. Are you ready?”

August looked at him.

“I spent three winters in the North,” he said. “There are no walls there either, when you are in the mountains.”

Kane nodded. Not smiling, but with something like approval.

“Then perhaps you will manage.”

The door closed behind him. August sat in the emptying tavern, listening as the last guests gathered themselves, as Thorn cleared the tables, as the last fire cracked in the hearth. Tomorrow — the desert. A new world, new rules, a new trial. He did not know what awaited there. But he knew he was ready to walk.

He took the guitar and ran his fingers over the strings. The sound was soft, almost inaudible. A melody was born on its own, without words, and in it was something of the east wind he had felt on the road with Halef. A wind that carried the scents of an unknown world and promised a road whose end could not yet be seen.
