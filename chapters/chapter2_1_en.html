He left at dawn. The snow still lay thick, but the air already carried the breath of spring — faint, barely perceptible, like a promise that had not yet found the courage to be spoken aloud.

Nearly five years had passed since the day he fled the burning city. Four and a half, to be exact. Time did not heal, but it changed. It did not mend wounds — it covered them with new skin, made them part of him, the way a tree grows bark over a once-fresh scratch.

For almost a year he had wandered the southern lands and seas — from Alba-Mare to Vistaria, from Greymouth to Sea Cliff, from Windholm to the ports of the eastern coast. And then — three years in the eastern North, among the Heimrings and the Helvings. He had learned their language, their songs, their customs. Had become one of them — as much as a stranger can become one of them in a foreign land.

The mother he had found and left behind. Arno, who had given him wisdom. Lis, who had shown him that even in a strange city one can find warmth. Mira, whose kiss had caught him off guard.

All of it stayed behind. Not erased, not forgotten — simply layered, like snow on the rocks: every layer had been real, but the topmost always covered the ones beneath. And so it was meant to be.

He had changed. Not only within — outwardly too. When he fled Alba-Mare, he was twenty-four. A young man with a smooth face, clean hands and a soft gaze in which a hope still glimmered, like a candle flame in an open window. Now he was nearly thirty. His face was covered by a thick dark beard in which grey was already showing through — not from age, but from the northern winds, from sleepless nights by the fire, from the salt that settles on the skin after long marches. His eyes had grown deeper, sharper — there was no bewilderment left in them. Only the calm, attentive strength of a man who had learned to wait and to see. The one who had once feared silence had now become part of it.

His hands, once smooth, were now covered with scars and tattoos. Thin blue lines ran along his forearms — patterns given to him by the Helvings: symbols of wind, sea and survival. On his right arm, just above the wrist, was the silhouette of a wave — a tattoo the shaman had made just before his departure. “So that you remember water is not the enemy,” he had said. “It will not drown you if you know how to breathe in time with it.” On his left shoulder — two crossed spears, the mark of belonging to the eastern hunters. He had not asked for it. He received it when he first killed a northern beast alone, and Bjorn gave a silent nod, seeing the tracks in the snow — that was worth more than any congratulation.

He had grown stronger. Broader in the shoulders, heavier in his gait. His movements were unhurried but precise, without superfluous gestures, without fuss. The silence that had once been a refuge, then a habit, had now become his natural state. He neither sought it nor avoided it. It simply was — like breathing, like wind, like a step.

August stood on the shore, gazing at the horizon. The wind touched his face, and calm spread within him — not from confidence, but from freedom. The freedom that comes not when you have nothing left to lose, but when you know that everything you ever had has already become part of you, and cannot be taken away.

He turned around. One last look at the land where he had spent three winters. There remained people who had become almost kin. Bjorn, who had taught him to walk on snow and to keep silent when words were superfluous. Signe, who had set before him his first mug of hot tea. The children who had run through the village with sticks, playing at hunters, and then stood by their houses watching him go. Part of himself had remained there — the part that had learned to breathe the cold without flinching.

But he knew: this was not forever. The North would remain in him. In the language, in the songs, in the patterns on his skin. In the way he breathed the frosty air without squinting. In the way he listened to silence and heard in it not emptiness, but a voice.

He turned to the east.

The guitar settled onto his shoulder. A step forward — not south, not north, but east. Toward lands he had never seen.
