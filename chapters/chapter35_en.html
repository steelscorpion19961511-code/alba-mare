August stayed in Holmstad for several days. He helped Ulrik sort goods, carried heavy sacks of furs, mended an old cart — hammering loose staples, fitting boards, binding with rope whatever could still be saved. He was not paid in coin — he received food and a place by the stove, where he could warm his chilled fingers and fall asleep listening to the wind howling somewhere high above the roofs. But that was enough. For the first time in a long while he did not feel superfluous. He was of use, and in that feeling there was its own quiet pride. He felt himself part of this place, though he was a stranger here — like a pebble that has not yet rubbed smooth against the others but no longer rolls down the slope, having found its place to lie.

On the third evening, when the wind was howling outside and the snow was burying the streets, erasing footprints and the boundaries between yards, Ulrik said to him:

“Everyone gathers at the tavern tonight. Traders, locals, those who have come off the road. They have a custom: if someone can play — they play. We have heard about you. Perhaps you will play too?”

August thought it over. He had not played since that evening by the fairground fire, when he had said farewell to the troupe. Then the air had been warm, smelling of roasted nuts and the smoke of resinous wood, and people had laughed and clapped in time. It seemed to him that his music had remained there, in the warmth and southern light; that here, among the snows and heavy silence, it would sound foreign and pitiful. But then he recalled Einar’s words: “In this land they do not look for truth in words — they look for it in deeds.” And he understood that to play here would also be a deed. Not for praise, but to show: he was here, he was open, he was ready to give a part of himself to this harsh land. And perhaps, in return, to receive a little warmth.

“Good,” he said.

The tavern was cramped and noisy, but the noise was not chaotic — it was familiar, homely: the creak of benches, the clink of mugs, the low hum of voices that rose and fell like the breathing of a great beast. Benches stood along the walls, and at the tables sat people of every age and appearance: traders with tired, watchful eyes; locals with rough, weathered faces; travellers whose cloaks still held the smell of the road. Smoke from the hearth rose to the ceiling and there came to rest, as though unwilling to leave this shelter.

Ulrik pointed him to a chair by the hearth itself. The flame crackled, throwing ruddy light on the walls, the faces, the beams darkened by time.

“Sit and play,” Ulrik said quietly, almost in a whisper, so as not to break the fine line between bustle and expectation that hung in the air. “They will not disturb you.”

August sat, took the guitar from his shoulder and laid it on his knees. The wood was warm from his body; the strings rang faintly with the change in temperature. He closed his eyes for a moment, listening not to himself but to the room: to the crackle of the fire, to the hum of voices at his back, to how the conversations died away the moment he touched the instrument. Then he ran his fingers over the strings, and the sound came out quiet but clean, like the first heartbeat after a long run. And silence fell.

No one had asked him to play. They simply listened, when he began. He played of the sea, of white walls, of a road that does not end but only changes colour. The melody was simple, without ornate passages or complex chords, but depth could be heard in it — in it was the silence of the snows in which he now lived, and the memory of the roar of southern cities, which he did not want to lose. At first no one moved; then someone sighed softly, someone stopped chewing, and the silence became complete — the kind in which one can hear the snow falling beyond the walls.

When he finished, he raised his eyes. The silence still held, but it was no longer tense — it was grateful. One of the traders, an older man with a face reddened by frost and thick grey eyebrows, raised his mug and said:

“You play well. Come sit with us — our treat.”

He shifted along the bench, making room, and with a gesture invited August to sit. They brought him hot soup — thick and hearty, with pieces of meat and spiced roots — bread, dark and dense, baked of coarse-ground flour, and a mug of warm herbal drink that smelled of thyme, mint and something else, northern and tart. Someone set a piece of dried meat on the table before him, someone else dried apples, wrinkled but sweet. He ate in silence, feeling himself welcomed into this circle not with words but with the giving of a place beside them, the sharing of food, the showing: you are one of us, at least for this evening.

“There will be a blizzard tomorrow,” someone at the table said, looking out the window where the snow was already flying almost sideways, driven by the wind. “You will stay here, August.”

“I know,” he answered, not raising his eyes from the mug, but feeling the warmth of the drink spread within, driving out the last of the cold. “I am in no hurry.”

And music sounded again — but now it was not only his guitar. Someone began to sing — quietly, with restraint, a song of distant trails and of those who did not return but remained in memory. Someone began beating time with a wooden spoon on the table; someone picked up the melody in a low, dense voice like the hum of wind in the rocks. The tavern filled with sounds that merged into a single breathing, into one voice of that place.

August sat and listened, then took up the guitar again and played along, not striving to stand out but striving to enter into that choir, to become part of it. He understood that even in the coldest land one can find warmth — not the warmth that fire gives, but the kind that is born between people when they cease to be strangers, at least for one evening. And in that warmth there was hope.
