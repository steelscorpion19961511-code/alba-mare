After supper he left the tavern and went toward the hammam the owner had pointed out. It stood at the end of the street — a low stone building with a domed roof and a copper door, from which warmth and moisture streamed. The smell of oil, heated stone and damp wood could be felt even on the approach.

He paid several silver coins — the very ones taken from the slain brigand — and went inside. The guitar and the pack remained in his room. He carried only the sword at his belt and the leather pouch with coins, which he removed and placed in a niche together with his clothing.

The antechamber was quiet. Stone benches, wooden lockers for clothes, oil lamps in niches. A few men sat on the benches, murmuring to one another. No women were to be seen — they were, apparently, in the adjoining hall. He removed his clothing and folded it neatly into the niche. The floor underfoot was warm — evidently pipes with hot water ran beneath it.

He passed into the warm hall. It was lighter here — daylight entered through small openings in the dome, scattering in the warm, humid air. Stone couches covered with mats stood along the walls. He sat down on one of them, feeling the warmth begin to sink into his muscles, feeling the body gradually release the tension it had held for weeks.

After a while he moved into the steam room. The steam was thick, almost impenetrable. The stone benches had heated so that one could lie on them without feeling cold. The humid air wrapped around the skin, opening the pores. He sat, closed his eyes, letting the heat do its work.

When the heat became almost unbearable, he went out into the hall with water. Stone basins — one with hot water, the other with cold — stood along the wall. He dipped a wooden ladle into the cold and poured it over himself, feeling his body shudder at the contrast. Then the hot, and again the cold. His blood ran faster through his veins, and he felt dirt and weariness leaving the body, washed away with the last drops of water.

Then he returned to the steam room and lay down on a bench. The hot stone burned his back, and he closed his eyes.

For a few moments he simply listened to the silence.

And then a girl entered the steam room. Short, supple, with golden skin and long curly black hair gathered into a high knot from which unruly strands escaped. She did not look around, did not seek anyone's gaze — she simply entered and sat on a stone bench a few steps from him. Her movements were smooth, unhurried. She crossed her legs, laid her hands on her knees and closed her eyes.

He could not take his eyes off her. She was beautiful. Quietly, without pretense — simply a living body glowing in the half-dark. Her skin was smooth, with a faint golden tint, as if touched by the sun. Large dark eyes looked somewhere through the steam, calmly, almost pensively, and her full lips were slightly parted, as if she were drawing in the hot air.

Then, without opening her eyes, she slowly lowered herself onto her back, stretched along the hot stone bench and crossed one leg over the other. Her chest rose slowly — evenly, deeply, like the breathing of the sea on a windless day. Drops of moisture slid down her neck, lingered on her collarbones, rolled slowly down.

He looked at her body — graceful, slender, with a fine waist and long, beautiful legs which she held loosely crossed, one resting on the other. At the thick dark brows, the full lips, the slender neck. At the small breast that rose and fell with her breathing. At the flat stomach, warm and smooth. At the long, shapely legs she held with an almost careless ease.

She lay on the hot stone bench, and her body seemed woven of steam and light — alive, flowing, like smoke that had for a moment taken the shape of a person. The oil lamps cast a warm, diffused light on her skin, and she seemed to glow from within.

Men sat near her, other women. No one looked at her with the attention with which August looked. For them it was customary — body, water, steam. They had lived in this world since childhood and found nothing astonishing in it.

Then a masseur came to him — a dry, swarthy man with hands covered in scars and calluses. With a gesture he invited him to lie on the stone table. He obeyed, feeling the warm, strong hands begin to work with the body — kneading the shoulders, back and neck that remembered the weight of the sword and the long marches. He felt the pain recede, the muscles release their tension, the body become light, almost weightless.

When the treatments were over, he was led into a small resting room. There it was quiet and dim. Soft cloths lay on the stone couches — white, fresh, smelling of grass and sun. He sat down, wrapped himself in one of them, feeling clean, dry warmth embrace his body.

On a low stand beside him stood a clay cup with a dark, almost amber drink. He took it, brought it to his lips and drank. The taste was strange — spiced, sweetish, with a tang of something warming that he could not place. He had never tasted such tea. He drank slowly, in small sips, feeling the warmth spread through his body from within. On a neighboring dish lay a fresh flatbread — still warm, with a light aroma of firewood. He broke off a piece and dipped it into a small cup of oil. It was delicate, rich in taste, recalling flax and nuts. The taste was unusual — it tingled the tongue unfamiliarly, yet one wanted to eat more and more of it.

He sat in the half-dark, wrapped in dry linen, and felt peace spreading within him, filling those corners of the body he had not felt in a long time. He did not hurry. He simply was. For the first time in long weeks he was going nowhere, seeking nothing, expecting nothing.

He sat in silence and listened to evening Shaan-Tar sounding beyond the walls of the hammam. Somewhere donkeys brayed, dishes clinked, people laughed. But here, in this warm, humid half-dark, there was only peace. Cleanliness. Lightness. And the quiet, trusting rest of a body that had at last received what it needed.
