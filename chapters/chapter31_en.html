He walked two more days before the land changed for good. The snow kept falling and falling, at times turning into stinging grains that the wind carried sideways as though trying to wipe every trace from the earth, at times growing soft and downy, settling over the world like a thin veil. But the main thing was this: it stopped melting. It lay thick, covering the stones, the grass, the tracks of animals. The world became white. And quiet. So quiet that August could hear his own breathing, the steady beat of his heart, and it even seemed he could hear the faint crunch of snowflakes settling on the fur of his hood.

He was walking along a snow-covered trail when the first cliffs appeared on the horizon. They were dark, almost black, and their summits vanished into the low clouds, as though they pushed straight into the grey sky and held it, keeping it from collapsing onto the earth. Somewhere beyond them began the Land of the Iron Cliffs.

August stopped at the edge of a low ridge and looked ahead. A valley spread before him — cold, stony, dotted with sparse patches of snow-laden shrub that stuck up out of the snow like dark needles. Far off, a dark band of sea could be seen. It was calm, almost motionless, as if it too had frozen together with this world, and only the thin line of foam along the shore spoke of water still alive.

He descended from the pass slowly and made for the cliffs. The snow creaked under his boots, and this sound seemed to him the only voice he had heard in days. It had become familiar, almost kin — like a rhythm the road itself set.

Towards evening he noticed smoke in the distance. A thin, barely visible thread rose above the rocks, losing itself in the grey sky and stretching upward, as if trying to reach for something greater. August quickened his pace, hoping to find shelter for the night.

The smoke came from a small fire laid between two large boulders, in a shallow hollow sheltered from the wind. A man sat by the fire. He was short but sturdy, with a thick dark beard and hair braided into thin plaits threaded with leather cords and metal rings. A cloak of coarse wool lay across his shoulders, and at his feet stood a staff with a dark head polished by years of use. As August came near, the man raised his head and looked at him attentively, but without hostility — with the gaze of someone who had seen much and did not hurry to judge.

“Sit,” he said in August’s own tongue. “I can see you’re from far away. From the south?”

August was surprised to hear his own language. He took off his pack, lowered himself onto a stone opposite, and stretched his hands toward the fire, feeling the warmth slowly work its way through frozen fingers, returning to them the sense of being alive.

“You speak our language,” said August.

“Traders from the south come here,” Einar answered. “Sometimes two tongues are spoken in our lands. I learned it to understand strangers and to trade. And to know what they say about us — those who do not know our land.”

August looked at him more closely. Einar’s eyes were grey like the northern sea, and in them there was a calm strength, as though he had seen storms and knew how to stand on the shore when the waves struck his face.

“My name is Einar,” he said. “I am wintering here. It is quiet here, and one can be alone and hear how the earth speaks. You, I see, are not from these parts.”

“No,” August answered. “I am from Alba-Mare.”

Einar nodded, and a shadow crossed his gaze, as though he recalled something distant and bitter.

“I know that city. The white city by the sea. I heard that it fell.”

“Yes,” said August. “It is no more.”

He fell silent, looking at the fire. The flame trembled, reflected in his eyes, and for a moment it seemed to him that he saw the white walls again, wrapped in smoke, and heard the silence that comes after the screaming. Einar did not question him — he simply pushed the kettle of hot tea closer and waited in silence while August warmed his hands and gathered the strength to go on speaking.

“Are you going to the Land of the Iron Cliffs?” he asked at last.

“Yes,” said August. “I want to understand what this place is. I want to understand why people kill one another.”

Einar regarded him for a long moment, as though weighing not the words but what lay hidden behind them. Then he turned his eyes to the fire and began to speak:

“This land bears its name for a reason. The cliffs here are sharp, almost black, and they run along the whole coast like the teeth of an ancient beast that has fallen asleep and will not wake again. No trees grow here, there are no fields and no warm seas. Only rock, cold, and wind that can knock a man off his feet. But people live here. They do not speak the same tongue, but they are people all the same. They survive.”

He took a stick and stirred the embers. Sparks leapt up and went out at once, dissolving into the darkness.

“They are not raiders, August. They are warriors and keepers of their families. But when winter lasts too long and the children have no food, the fathers take up the oars and go south, to bring back what their mountains do not hold. They do not kill out of malice. They do it to survive.”

August listened without interrupting, and suddenly understood that for the first time he was hearing neither an accusation nor a justification, but a simple, bitter truth in which there was no place for fine words.

“The southerners call them enemies,” Einar went on. “But no one asks what drives them from their homes and across the sea while the water is not yet frozen. They are people the same as you. But their land is different. And it does not forgive weakness.”

He fell silent, and a quiet settled between them — not an empty one, but filled with the crackle of the fire, the whistle of the wind and the distant cry of some northern bird. The snow fell softly, covering their tracks, as though wishing to erase the past and make room for what was new. The fire kept burning, casting warm light on their faces, and in that glow Einar’s features grew softer, his gaze deeper.

“Why do you need this land?” Einar asked quietly, almost in a whisper, as though afraid to frighten away the answer.

August was silent for a long time, watching the flame. He remembered his father clutching a broken spear at the gates, the smoke above the white walls, the silence of the empty shore that was more terrible than any scream. Then he said:

“To understand why people kill one another. To understand whether it can be avoided.”

Einar gave no answer. He simply threw dry branches on the fire and looked at the flames for a long time, as though searching in them for answers he could not put into words. At last he said:

“Then you did right to come here. And do not merely look — listen. Listen to the wind, which does not lie. Listen to the people who are silent when they are in pain. And then you will see that we are all the same — those who die of hunger, and those who burn in their own homes. Pain does not choose whom to visit. It comes to everyone.”

August raised his eyes to him. He did not know what to answer. But he felt that he had met a man who understood more than he said, and whose silence was worth more than many words.

“I will stay here a few days,” said Einar. “If you wish, stay with me. I will show you how to survive in this land. And how to hear its voice. Not the one that shouts — the one that whispers, if you know how to listen.”

August nodded. He felt neither joy nor relief — only a calm certainty that he had come to the place where he was meant to be.

The snow went on falling, wrapping the world in white silence, and the fire burned, warming two people who had met at the edge of the earth to share the silence and the words that are born in the cold. And in that warmth there was not only deliverance from the frost, but the hope that understanding is possible — even where, it seemed, only cliffs and wind remained.
